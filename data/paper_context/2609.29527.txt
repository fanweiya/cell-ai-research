3 Methodology CoSWA-YOLOv12 is built on a YOLOv12 instance-segmentation network with an added stride-four (P2) detection head, a configuration previously shown to aid tiny parasite detection [ 1 ] . Figure 1 shows where the three additions attach: a wavelet detail residual inside the strided downsampling of the backbone, a scale-adaptive Wasserstein term inside the label assigner, and a min-max Gaussian regression loss at the box head. The additions grow the model by only about one percent (Section 3.5 ). 3.5 Data, Compute, and Inference Dataset and split. We evaluate on a five-class Rwandan thick-smear dataset of Giemsa-stained fields captured at 100 × 100\times magnification, annotated with boxes and polygon masks for four Plasmodium species ( P. falciparum , P. malariae , P. ovale , P. vivax ) and white blood cells (WBC) [ 1 ] . Images are letterboxed to 2048 × 2048 2048\times 2048 and split image-wise into 1,915 1{,}915 training, 410 410 validation, and 411 411 test images ( ≈ 70 / 15 / 15 \approx\!70/15/15 ); the validation split is used only for checkpoint selection and all reported numbers are on the held-out test set. The classes span a wide size range, the median object side length being 34 34 pixels for P. falciparum , 78 78 for P. malariae , and 100 100 to 115 115 for P. ovale , P. vivax , and WBC, the four-fold spread that makes a scale-uniform treatment counterproductive. Training and compute. All models are YOLOv12n instance-segmentation networks with a P2 head, trained for 70 70 epochs at 2048 × 2048 2048\times 2048 with SGD (learning rate 0.005 0.005 , momentum 0.937 0.937 , weight decay 5 × 10 − 4 5\times 10^{-4} ), mixed precision, batch size 4 4 , and mosaic and copy-paste augmentation [ 3 ] , with backbone and neck initialised from a public YOLOv12n segmentation checkpoint. We set the gate from our measured object sizes ( ρ = 0.5 \rho=0.5 , τ = 56 \tau=56 px, β = 8 \beta=8 px), and the loss coefficients are λ = 0.5 \lambda=0.5 and α = 0.25 \alpha=0.25 (the M2IoU value [ 8 ] ); τ , β \tau,\beta are training hyperparameters, set from the target dataset’s object sizes. Training uses a single NVIDIA H100 GPU, the full model completing 70 70 epochs in about 11.4 11.4 hours. Synergy and necessity of the gate. The assignment and loss innovations form a 2 × 2 2\times 2 factorial over the WDR backbone (Table 1 , mask mAP@50-95): the gate alone adds 0.026 0.026 and the min-max loss alone 0.018 0.018 , but together 0.071 0.071 , a synergy of 0.027 0.027 , because with the gate in place the loss no longer spends capacity repairing large objects that global NWD mis-assigned. The gate is essential: sized from the data ( τ = 56 \tau{=}56 , β = 8 \beta{=}8 px) it opens to 0.94 0.94 for P. falciparum but below 0.06 0.06 for every larger class, so removing it lets global NWD collapse overall mask mAP@50-95 from 0.637 0.637 to 0.603 0.603 , which the gate restores before the M 2 \text{M}^{2} -NWD loss lifts every class above the baseline. 5 Discussion The results support this paper’s central claim: on a multi-species slide the obstacle to detecting tiny P. falciparum is not model capacity but a scale conflict inside training, and resolving it selectively is what unlocks the gains. The scale gate routes the Wasserstein treatment almost entirely to P. falciparum , which recovers the recall and mAP@50 it was missing while the larger classes keep, and even tighten, their localisation; the factorial shows the gate and the min-max loss to be synergistic because the gate frees the loss from repairing mis-assigned large objects. Because box and mask move together throughout, the improvement must lie upstream of either head, in assignment and feature preservation. Limitations and future work. Several residual P. falciparum false positives, on inspection, fall on unlabelled parasite-like structures, so the reported P. falciparum precision is likely a lower bound. The single-site evaluation leaves cross-site robustness, a known weakness for tiny parasites, as the natural next steps. Limitations and future work.
