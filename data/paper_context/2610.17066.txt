Uppercase symbols denote random variables, and lowercase symbols denote their realizations, such as a specific control image x T x_{T} or perturbed image x 0 x_{0} . (1) b b defines the sampling and evaluation strata, while the control image provides relevant information. Training pairs perturbed and control images using a joint sampling distribution π c , b ​ ( x 0 , x T ) \pi_{c,b}(x_{0},x_{T}) for each perturbation c c and experimental context b b . Pairing within the same batch or plate keeps the experimental context consistent. One possible construction samples the two images independently from their respective populations within each stratum. Stochastic bridge and image prediction. Diffusion bridges connect informative source and target distributions through stochastic intermediate states ( Zhou et al., 2024 ; Liu et al., 2023 ) . A Gaussian stochastic interpolant takes the form X t = α t ​ X 0 + β t ​ X T + γ t ​ Z , Z ∼ 𝒩 ⁡ ( 0 , I ) , X_{t}=\alpha_{t}X_{0}+\beta_{t}X_{T}+\gamma_{t}Z,\qquad Z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I), (2) where Z Z is independent of the image pair. We use a time coordinate t t , with t = 1 t=1 at the control and t = 0 t=0 at the perturbed target. The coefficients satisfy ( α 0 , β 0 , γ 0 ) = ( 1 , 0 , 0 ) (\alpha_{0},\beta_{0},\gamma_{0})=(1,0,0) and ( α 1 , β 1 , γ 1 ) = ( 0 , 1 , 0 ) (\alpha_{1},\beta_{1},\gamma_{1})=(0,1,0) . Hence, PerturbBridge predicts the perturbed image from an intermediate bridge state, allowing biological response objectives to act directly on that prediction. JiT ( Li & He, 2026 ) motivates direct data prediction through the manifold hypothesis: observed data can occupy a low-dimensional structure within a high-dimensional observation space, whereas noise-containing targets require modeling additional off-manifold variation. This motivates predicting the perturbed image in pixel space. Image prediction also provides a shared output for pixel-wise regression and biological response supervision. Feature-based losses make errors in encoded phenotypic properties contribute directly to training; App. A.2 gives a local mathematical interpretation. Perturbed-image prediction and bridge regression. A 1,024-dimensional perturbation embedding is added to the time embedding. The generative model predicts X ^ 0 = D θ ​ ( X t , t , c ) \hat{X}_{0}=D_{\theta}(X_{t},t,c) using a preconditioned parameterization. We train this prediction with weighted pixel-wise mean squared error: ℒ bridge = 𝔼 ⁡ [ w ⁡ ( t ) d x ​ ‖ X ^ 0 − X 0 ‖ 2 ] . \mathcal{L}_{\rm bridge}=\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{w(t)}{d_{x}}\left\lVert\hat{X}_{0}-X_{0}\right\rVert^{2}\right]. (4) Here, d x d_{x} is the number of image entries and w ⁡ ( t ) w(t) is the preconditioning-based regression weight. The expectation is over training image pairs and perturbation conditions, sampled times t t , and Gaussian noise Z Z . As in Section 2.2 , X ^ 0 \hat{X}_{0} denotes a random variable and x ^ 0 \hat{x}_{0} a realization. The control X T X_{T} enters through the bridge state and sampling equations, rather than as a separate network input. Let f i = ϕ ⁡ ( x ^ 0 , i ) ∈ ℝ d f f_{i}=\phi(\hat{x}_{0,i})\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{f}} , where ϕ \phi is a frozen Inception-v3 encoder ( Heusel et al., 2017 ) with d f = 2048 d_{f}=2048 . The encoder parameters remain fixed while gradients from the biological response losses pass through it to update the generative model. The biological response supervision pipeline uses the first three image channels with differentiable preprocessing; the bridge regression uses all image channels. Reference statistics and prototypes are constructed from real perturbed training data. The biological response objectives use the condition statistics and biological annotations available in each dataset. Condition-moment matching. When real perturbed training data are grouped by perturbation condition, their feature moments provide supervision targets. For each valid condition group c c in a minibatch, let μ ^ c , v ^ c \hat{\mu}_{c},\hat{v}_{c} be the gate-weighted feature mean and diagonal variance of predicted perturbed images, and μ c , v c \mu_{c},v_{c} the corresponding statistics of real perturbed training data. We use ℒ mom = 1 | 𝒞 B | ​ d f ​ ∑ c ∈ 𝒞 B [ ‖ μ ^ c − μ c ‖ 2 + λ var ​ ‖ v ^ c − v c ‖ 2 ] . \mathcal{L}_{\rm mom}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{C}_{B}|d_{f}}\sum_{c\in\mathcal{C}_{B}}\left[\left\lVert\hat{\mu}_{c}-\mu_{c}\right\rVert^{2}+\lambda_{\mathrm{var}}\left\lVert\hat{v}_{c}-v_{c}\right\rVert^{2}\right]. (5) Here, 𝒞 B \mathcal{C}_{B} is the set of valid condition groups in the minibatch, and λ var \lambda_{\mathrm{var}} balances mean and variance matching. We apply condition-moment matching on all three datasets; on BBBC021, λ var = 0.5 \lambda_{\mathrm{var}}=0.5 . Biological contrast. We compute probabilities q i ​ j q_{ij} by applying a softmax with temperature τ \tau to cosine similarities between predicted-image features and condition prototypes from real perturbed training data. Let 𝒫 i \mathcal{P}_{i} contain the positive prototypes for sample i i , defined by the requested condition and available biological annotations; the remaining prototypes are negatives. Following the multi-positive supervised contrastive reduction ( Khosla et al., 2020 ) , ℓ i con = − 1 | 𝒫 i | ∑ j ∈ 𝒫 i log q i ​ j . \ell_{i}^{\rm con}=-\frac{1}{|\mathcal{P}_{i}|}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{P}_{i}}\log q_{ij}. (6) The batch loss ℒ con \mathcal{L}_{\rm con} is a time-gated weighted average of these per-image losses. The loss has the same form across datasets, while the positive and negative sets depend on their condition definitions and annotations. On BBBC021, we use 26 condition prototypes and τ = 0.1 \tau=0.1 , with positives sharing the requested MoA. This encourages shared MoA-associated features, while moment matching retains condition-specific constraints. Joint training objective. Tables 3 and 4 compare PerturbBridge with the Bridge-only variant, which omits biological response supervision. On BBBC021, adding this supervision increases MoA accuracy from 67.7% to 72.4% on the standard split and from 50.7% to 59.0% on unseen compounds, with improvements in both macro-F1 and weighted-F1. On the standard split, FID decreases from 17.3 to 16.4 and KID from 1.02 to 0.91. These results show that biological response supervision improves MoA preservation beyond bridge regression alone, including for compounds excluded from training. PerturbBridge improves most evaluated metrics over CellFlux+bio. Tables 3 and 4 compare PerturbBridge with CellFlux+bio, which applies biological response supervision to perturbed images reconstructed from CellFlux velocity predictions. On BBBC021, CellFlux+bio reaches 66.3% MoA accuracy, compared with 72.4% for PerturbBridge. On JUMP, its FID is 9.8, compared with 8.3 for PerturbBridge. Together with the Bridge-only comparison, these results support the combined design of direct perturbed-image prediction and biological response supervision. PerturbBridge achieves lower FID with fewer model evaluations. Fig. 5 compares CellFlux at 20, 40, and 100 model evaluations (NFE) with PerturbBridge at 20 NFE. At the matched budget of 20 NFE, PerturbBridge achieves FID values of 16.4 on BBBC021 and 8.3 on JUMP, compared with 25.6 and 31.7 for CellFlux. On the standard split, FID decreases from 17.3 to 16.4 and KID from 1.02 to 0.91. These results show that biological response supervision improves MoA preservation beyond bridge regression alone, including for compounds excluded from training. PerturbBridge improves most evaluated metrics over CellFlux+bio. Tables 3 and 4 compare PerturbBridge with CellFlux+bio, which applies biological response supervision to perturbed images reconstructed from CellFlux velocity predictions. On BBBC021, CellFlux+bio reaches 66.3% MoA accuracy, compared with 72.4% for PerturbBridge. On JUMP, its FID is 9.8, compared with 8.3 for PerturbBridge. Together with the Bridge-only comparison, these results support the combined design of direct perturbed-image prediction and biological response supervision. PerturbBridge achieves lower FID with fewer model evaluations. Fig. 5 compares CellFlux at 20, 40, and 100 model evaluations (NFE) with PerturbBridge at 20 NFE. At the matched budget of 20 NFE, PerturbBridge achieves FID values of 16.4 on BBBC021 and 8.3 on JUMP, compared with 25.6 and 31.7 for CellFlux. These scores also improve on CellFlux at 100 NFE, which reaches 18.8 and 9.0, respectively. Both methods use the same logical NFE convention; with classifier-free guidance, each logical evaluation uses two network forwards (App. B ). 5 Conclusion We introduce PerturbBridge, a stochastic data-to-data bridge model that directly supervises phenotypic responses in predicted perturbed images during generative training. By jointly optimizing bridge regression and differentiable biological response losses through a frozen encoder, PerturbBridge incorporates biological objectives without requiring RL post-training or biological evaluation during sampling. Across BBBC021, JUMP, and AllenCell, the model improves MoA classification, perturbation retrieval, and image-distribution metrics relative to CellFlux. Gains under CellProfiler and DINOv2 representations extend beyond the features used for supervision. Ablations further demonstrate the contribution of biological response supervision. With a bridge-specific sampler, PerturbBridge achieves lower FID than the 100-NFE CellFlux baseline using 20 model evaluations. These findings support direct biological response supervision as a practical approach to cellular perturbation generation and highlight the value of evaluating generated images through downstream biological tasks alongside distributional metrics. Sampling an image pair, an interior time, and Gaussian noise constructs a training state without simulating a trajectory. We use the linear ECSI path and apply biological response supervision to predicted perturbed images.
