3 Methods 3.1 Data Uncertainty quantification methods for segmentation can be optimally evaluated using data with multiple annotations per image. Such data captures aleatoric uncertainty through annotator disagreement, and therefore allows for more principled evaluation of AMB and CAL [ 21 ] . In our benchmark, we use the three datasets listed in Tab. 1 , all of which are resized to a 128 × 128 128\times 128 resolution. The LIDC-IDRI dataset [ 2 ] ( LIDC ) consists of 1018 thoracic CT scans with annotated lung nodules (see Fig. 2 ), each annotated by at least four radiologists. For 2D segmentation, the 15,096 slices with exactly 4 annotations are commonly used [ 21 , 25 , 28 , 8 ] . Slices are lesion-centered crops, split per-patient into 60%/20%/20% train/val/test sets. Annotator disagreement is dominated by nodule presence/absence rather than boundary delineation, so up to 3 segmentations can be empty. We apply three synthetic OOD shifts (blur, noise, contrast) to the test images, yielding one ID and three OOD test sets. Figure 2 : Example images from the three datasets, showing both ID and OOD images. The four binary-mask delineations are shown on LIDC and NPC as red lines, while the five cup and disc segmentations are shown as blue and green lines on Chaksu. The MMIS NPC-170 [ 36 ] ( NPC ) dataset contains 170 multimodal magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) scans, 120 of which we can access. Each scan has aligned T1, T1-contrast and T2 sequences (shown as RGB in Fig. 2 ). Four senior radiologists independently annotated nasopharyngeal carcinoma gross tumor volumes for radiotherapy planning. The scans yield 2260 2D slices with at least one non-empty segmentation. Images are cropped square along the longer side and split per-patient into 64%/16%/20% train/val/test sets. As OOD shifts, we apply three synthetic augmentations to the test set (Gibbs, noise, hist), emulating common MRI artifacts (Gibbs and Rician noise), while random histogram shifts resemble differences across scanning procedures and machines. Each channel is augmented separately because the channels stem from different scans. The Chákṣu IMAGE dataset [ 22 ] ( Chaksu ) consists of 1345 RGB fundus images with optic disc and cup segmentations (see Fig. 2 ), each annotated by 5 ophthalmologists. We keep only the largest connected component of each annotation to remove small artifacts. The data was captured with three devices: Remidio, Bosch and Forus (1074, 145 and 126 images, respectively). Remidio serves as ID and Bosch/Forus as OOD. We use the original test split (264 ID images) and split the remaining 810 ID images 80/20 into training and validation. Images are cropped around the optic disc and cup, with the crop side length set to twice the per-device average bounding box sidelength, ensuring the OOD-shift reflects image characteristics rather than size differences. 3.2 Aleatoric and Epistemic Uncertainty Models Here, we describe all models and hyper-parameter settings used in our experiments. We evaluate the following methods for AU estimation: • Softmax : A deterministic UNet trained with a standard cross-entropy loss. Referred to as "softmax" for the softmax output layer giving rise to the probabilities used in the uncertainty measures. • Stochastic Segmentation Networks (SSN) [ 25 ] : A stochastic segmentation network, modeling the logits as a Gaussian distribution with a mean and variance output by the network. We use a rank of 10 for the covariance matrix, ϵ = 1 × 10 − 5 \epsilon=$1\text{\times}{10}^{-5}$ for numerical stability and 10 pretraining epochs estimating the mean before also modeling the variance. • Probabilistic UNet (Prob. UNet) [ 21 ] : A probabilistic UNet with prior and posterior encoder networks, trained with KL divergence as an additional loss term. We use a latent dimension of 6, β = 5 × 10 − 4 \beta=$5\text{\times}{10}^{-4}$ (LIDC) and β = 2.5 × 10 − 3 \beta=$2.5\text{\times}{10}^{-3}$ (Chaksu) for the KL weight, with linear warmup over the first 32 epochs. Our β \beta values are smaller than typical because we use mean rather than sum reduction for the reconstruction term. • Diffusion [ 13 , 1 , 8 ] : A diffusion model learning to reverse a noising process. We train with a uniformly weighted MSE loss in the data domain with a softmax head. The cosine noise schedule is used with input scaling [ 6 ] at b = 0.1 b=0.1 . For inference, we use the DDIM [ 31 ] sampler with 10 timesteps. For the EU estimation we studied the following approaches: • Deep Ensemble (Ensemble) [ 23 ] : A deep ensemble of 5 models, each trained from different random initializations. • MC Dropout (Dropout) [ 12 ] : A model with channel-wise [ 32 ] dropout (p=0.2) before convolutional layers in residual blocks, at train and test time. • Stochastic Weight Averaging-Gaussian (SWAG) [ 24 ] : SWAG approximates the weight posterior by fitting a Gaussian to weights collected during training. We use 30 snapshots from the last 30 epochs with AdamW instead of SGD, which performed better in early testing. The diagonal-only variant is referred to as SWAG-D . • No EU : No epistemic modeling; the AU model’s generative outputs are used directly following ValUES [ 17 ] , without swapping AU and EU labels. All model combinations are valid except softmax without EU (incompatible as it is deterministic). For each model, we adopt hyperparameters as specified in the original literature whenever possible. When hyperparameter values are ambiguous or may depend on the dataset or network architecture, we perform limited small-scale sweeps to select appropriate settings. 3.3 Model Architecture To reduce architecture-based variation all models share a common attention UNet backbone based on Simple Diffusion [ 14 , 15 ] . The probabilistic models require modifications to the base architecture. SSN adds lightweight heads for the diagonal variance and low-rank covariances of the Gaussian logits. Diffusion models add a timestep embedding. For Prob. UNet, two extra UNet encoders serve as the prior and posterior networks. To account for the added parameters, we scale the base channels for all three Prob. UNet networks from 32 to 24. The final model sizes are 15.78, 15.80, 15.81 and 16.89 million parameters for Softmax, SSN, Diffusion and Prob. UNet, respectively. We train all models from scratch, as using pretrained weights would be difficult for the more involved AU models (Diffusion, Prob. UNet), complicating fair comparison. Models with No EU or SWAG/SWAG-D can share a single training run since they do not affect training directly. 3.4 Uncertainty Quantification We generally follow the ValUES [ 17 ] framework, basing experiments on the AMB, OOD detection and CAL tasks. At test time, we draw 10 AU samples and use 10 EU model instances, yielding up to 100 joint predictions per image for computing uncertainty maps via Eq. 1 . Out-of-distribution detection. Out-of-distribution detection separates in-distribution (ID) from out-of-distribution (OOD) samples using estimated uncertainty maps, and is measured by the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC). An aggregation strategy is needed to reduce pixel-wise maps to image-level scores. ValUES suggested three strategies: the image-wise mean, the patch-level maximum uncertainty for a 10 × 10 10\times 10 patch, and a threshold-based strategy. Larger segmentations tend to have higher uncertainty under e.g. image-wise mean, and this can lead the model into predicting that all large lung nodules in LIDC are OOD and all small nodules are ID, which is undesirable. Therefore, we add the area-normalized and border-normalized strategies. The border length is the count of 1-connectivity neighboring pixel pairs with differing labels. If area or border is zero, we normalize by 1. Ambiguity modeling (AMB). AMB aims to capture data-inherent variance. To measure how well this variance is captured, we compute pixel-wise variance over multiple ground truth annotations and compare to predicted uncertainty maps using normalized cross-correlation (NCC). Calibration (CAL). The goal of calibration is to ensure that the predicted probabilities reflect the true likelihood of correctness. We use the expected calibration error (ECE), which is the average absolute difference between confidence and accuracy across 20 bins from 0 to 1, weighted by the number of pixels in each bin. We do not space the bins uniformly from 0 to 1 as is common, but instead use the (0%, 5%, 10%…100%) confidence quantiles as bin edges, avoiding situations where models can trivialize the score when all confidences are in a single bin. Uncertainty measures are converted to confidences by Platt scaling [ 27 ] their negatives, with separate parameters for AU, EU and TU. Unlike ValUES, which averaged per-image Platt parameters, we compute dataset-wide parameters, fitted on the validation split and applied to the test split. Entanglement proxy ( Δ \Delta ). The empirical entanglement can be assessed through the relative performance of uncertainty measures. Specifically, if a theoretically consistent measure outperforms the theoretically inconsistent measure for a given task, this suggests coupling between them. To summarize this behavior, we define the entanglement proxy as: Δ = s ​ arctan ⁡ ( U c / U i ) − π / 4 π / 4 = s ​ ϕ π / 4 , \Delta=s\frac{\arctan(U_{c}/U_{i})-\pi/4}{\pi/4}=s\frac{\phi}{\pi/4}, (2) where U c U_{c} and U i U_{i} are the performance of the consistent and inconsistent uncertainty measures, respectively. For metrics where a lower value is better, such as ECE, we set s = − 1 s=-1 and otherwise s = 1 s=1 . The proxy ranges from − 1 -1 to 1, with larger values indicating better disentanglement (see Fig. 3 ). It is worth noting that Δ \Delta does not directly measure statistical dependence between AU and EU and may be affected by the decomposition itself ( i.e . Eq. 1 ), model capacity, or task characteristics, rather than entanglement alone. Therefore, absolute values should be interpreted with caution. However, the data, task, decomposition, evaluation protocol, and shared backbone are fixed in our comparisons, while the AU-EU model combination varies. Therefore, relative differences in Δ \Delta nevertheless provide a useful comparison of model combinations. Figure 3 : A visualization of the entanglement proxy Δ \Delta . Models were trained for 1000, 320, and 500 epochs on LIDC, NPC, and Chaksu, respectively, with the exception of Prob. UNet models on LIDC, which were only trained for 500 epochs due to faster overfitting. EU magnitudes are substantially smaller than AU for nearly all models (epistemic collapse). This is especially problematic for CAL, where under the Kendall & Gal decomposition T ​ U = A ​ U + E ​ U ≈ A ​ U TU=AU+EU\approx AU prevents TU from providing information beyond AU. Addressing epistemic collapse could therefore affect Δ \Delta in CAL. Kirsch et al. [ 20 ] proposed extracting independent sub-networks post-training, and incorporating their methods in our framework is a promising way forward. We tried a post-hoc correction to boost EU ( e.g . by multiplication with a constant) before computing TU, thereby making the two components more comparable in magnitude, however this did not work empirically and we think epistemic collapse needs to be addressed on a fundamental level to reduce entanglement. Low entanglement in ensembles. Ensembles exhibit the best performance and most favorable entanglement-proxy values across most tasks. A plausible explanation is that each ensemble member follows an independent training trajectory from a different random initialization, leading to genuinely diverse network weights and predictions. Dropout and SWAG/SWAG-D obtain weight distributions from a single training run, constraining diversity and potentially causing EU to capture less epistemic variation, which leaks into AU. We observe less severe epistemic collapse for ensembles, supporting this. Models without an EU component. No EU models are consistently outliers in Fig. 6 : their proxy values are less favorable in CAL and more favorable in AMB, with generally worse performance. Since these models have no EU component and their predictions are used directly as the generative mean, epistemic collapse does not apply, explaining their lower CAL proxy values. Otherwise, these models show no clear trends consistent with a meaningful AU-EU decomposition, even if we swap AU and EU labels as ValUES [ 17 ] . We therefore argue against applying the Kendall & Gal decomposition to models without an explicit EU component, as done in ValUES [ 17 ] . To our knowledge, this application lacks a formal theoretical foundation in existing literature, and we therefore caution against it. Model choice recommendations. Model selection involves trading off performance, the entanglement proxy and computational cost. Ensembles require e.g . N = 5 N=5 model trainings instead of 1, while dropout and SWAG/SWAG-D only train once, with SWAG needing more storage. Among non-ensemble EU methods, dropout performs slightly better overall. If ensembles are too expensive given their training compute scaling with ensemble size, dropout could be the preferred cheaper option. For OOD detection, ensembles excel, with the best performance and most favorable proxy values. For AMB, softmax and SSN have more favorable Δ \Delta values. For CAL, softmax and SSN perform best while Prob. UNet has the most favorable proxy value. Overall, a softmax ensemble performs surprisingly well given its conceptual simplicity. Ignoring compute budget and ease of implementation, we outline our model recommendations in Tab. 3 . Limitations and future directions. Our study has several limitations. Tracking how entanglement evolves during training could shed light on when and why it emerges. Studying entanglement as a function of model size would clarify the role of epistemic collapse, which worsens with increasing capacity [ 20 , 11 ] . Incorporating non-generative AU methods such as test-time augmentation [ 3 ] would test whether trends generalize beyond probabilistic models. We also omit feature- and logit-based OOD baselines such as energy scores, Mahalanobis distance, and ViM as well as evidential deep learning, which do not map directly to the probability-based AU/EU/TU framework used here. Comparing a broader class of models is an important direction for future work. Further, our evaluation is limited to three medical imaging datasets, each with its own flaws. The aleatoric uncertainty in LIDC is not ideal since nodules are either present or absent, with little room for ambiguous mask delineations to have an effect. The LIDC and NPC datasets are natively 3D, but we sliced them into 2D images. While volumetric evaluation would be preferable, other native 2D datasets were too small to replace these data, and 2D LIDC/NPC slices are widely used in prior work. All datasets are cropped around the region of interest, which makes segmentation unnaturally easy in some applications. These choices were motivated by common practice for LIDC, improved segmentation fidelity for Chaksu (the optic disc is relatively easy to identify but small compared to the retina), and square cropping for NPC (without centralization). We think going beyond 128 × 128 128\times 128 resolution would not materially change downstream trends, since early experiments at 64 × 64 64\times 64 resolution were similar, and all our datasets were sampled close to native quality at 128 × 128 128\times 128 resolution. The ValUES framework requires datasets with multiple expert annotations, which are scarce due to the large annotation burden. As a result, most such datasets are relatively small or cover narrow segmentation problems with few structures. Finally, our study lacks large-scale clinical validation. Future work should acquire high-quality multi-annotator datasets and validate findings in broader clinical settings. 6 Conclusion Our systematic evaluation of 19 AU-EU model combinations on 3 datasets has shed light on the usefulness of these approaches and the challenges in uncertainty quantification. Our proposed entanglement proxy summarizes whether the theoretically consistent uncertainty measure outperforms the inconsistent one. Deep ensembles consistently achieve the best performance and most favorable proxy values. We identified epistemic collapse as a possible driver of entanglement and suggested potential mitigation strategies. Our results show that applying the Kendall & Gal decomposition to models without an explicit EU component is not well-motivated. Unless a generative model is required, our findings suggest that an ensemble of standard cross-entropy-trained softmax models is sufficient for downstream task performance. Our benchmark and entanglement proxy provide the tools to diagnose and reduce entanglement in future methods and ultimately develop better uncertainty quantification methods. 0.A.3 Architecture Details The UNet architecture uses 32 base channels which are increased with a channel multiplier of ( 1 , 2 , 4 , 8 ) (1,2,4,8) across the different resolution scales. The backbone mainly consists of residual blocks (ResBlocks) which use the following layers: • Norm : Group normalization with 32 channels and ϵ = \epsilon= 1e-5. • SiLU : The Sigmoid Linear Unit, SiLU ​ ( x ) = x / ( 1 + e − x ) \text{SiLU}(x)=x/(1+e^{-x}) (also used as the activation function throughout the network) • Conv : A standard 2D convolution with kernel size 3 × 3 3\times 3 . A ResBlock is defined by sequential application of the layers: Norm, SiLU, Conv, Norm, SiLU, Conv. The ResBlocks are residual, meaning the input is added to the output. If the number of channels is changed through a ResBlock, a 1 × 1 1\times 1 2D convolution is added to the residual path to account for the change. We use 3 downscaling/upscaling operations (as implied by the channel multiplier). Downscaling uses average pooling and upscaling uses nearest-neighbour interpolation followed by a 3 × 3 3\times 3 2D convolution. Each resolution scale has 2 ResBlocks in succession, with an additional 2 middle ResBlocks. We use additive skip connections between the encoder and decoder just before/after layers with downscaling/upscaling. The number of channels is changed in the first convolution after a downscaling/upscaling operation. Self-attention is used after each ResBlock when the feature map has a width of 32 or less (the two deepest scales). When applying self-attention, we consider each pixel 1 1 1 Pixel is used loosely here, referring to a spatial position in the downscaled feature tensor. as a separate token.
