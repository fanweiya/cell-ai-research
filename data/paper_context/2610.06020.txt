3.1 Model architectures For the search framework, we considered both a classical and a variational autoencoder. The encoder part of the classical autoencoder consists of 3 convolutional layers with kernel size 3 × \times 3, each of them followed by a max-pooling layer and one fully connected layer at the end. The decoder part is the inverse of the encoder, with transposed convolutional layers with kernel size 2 × \times 2 and stride 2, and without max-pooling layers. The classical autoencoder is trained using the multiscale structural similarity (MS-SSIM) [ 18 ] , a perceptual loss function. The variational autoencoder is based on the classical autoencoder, and the fully connected layer on the encoder side is replaced by two parallel ones, one for the mean and one for the variance of the Gaussian distribution. The variational autoencoder has an additional KL-divergence term, weighted by the β \beta parameter. The models were implemented using the PyTorch library. For the precise details on the implementation, we refer to [ 9 ] and the supplementary code repository. 4 Results and discussion In this section, we first test the capability of the learned descriptors to discriminate between different structures of interest. Precision, that is the number of returned patches that overlap with the ground truth regions of the structure of interest, divided by the total number of returned patches. Or the number of true positives (TP) divided by the number of true and false positives (FP). Precision = T ​ P T ​ P + F ​ P \text{Precision}=\frac{TP}{TP+FP} (5) An overlap of 1 pixel is already sufficient because our search framework aims to locate a structure of interest, rather than delineating the exact boundary. 2. Recall, that is the number of pixels of retrieved structures, divided by the total number of pixels of all occurrences of the structure. Or the number of true positives divided by the number of true positives and false negatives (FN). Recall = T ​ P T ​ P + F ​ N \text{Recall}=\frac{TP}{TP+FN} (6) A structure is retrieved if at least one patch overlaps with it. We take into account the number of pixels to give more importance to larger structures. Precision measures the quality of the retrieved patches, that is, what fraction of the retrieved patches truly belong to the structure of interest. Recall measures the completeness of the retrieval, using the pixel-weighted fraction of retrieved structures so that larger structures contribute proportionally more. Ideally, both precision and recall should be 1. We use precision and recall to visualize the Precision-Recall curves (PR-curves), which are often used as an evaluation method for retrieval frameworks. PR-curves are computed using different thresholds, which objectively measure the retrieval performance irrespective of the chosen threshold. Finally, we also compute the area under the PR curves, which is called the average precision (AP). The average precision can be used to compare the performance of different techniques. 4.3 Query selection strategy To mimic the way a human user would input labeled data into the framework, and using the ground-truth segmentation masks provided with the datasets, we implement a query selection strategy to extract positive and negative query patches evenly distributed across the vEM volume. We divide the dataset into groups with an equal number of slices, defined as the split size. The remaining slices are also considered as one group, even if it has fewer slices. The middle slice from each group is selected as a query slice, as is visually represented in Figure 5 . From these query slices, we extract patches measuring 80 × 80 80\times 80 pixels using a regular non-overlapping sliding window (i.e. stride equal to the patch size, so that patches tile the slice without overlap). The same scheme is applied both at query time and at evaluation time on every non-query slice of the volume. A patch is labeled as positive if it overlaps with a mitochondrion by at least 50 % of its pixels and as negative if there is no overlap. Patches with intermediate overlap (between 0 0 % and 50 50 %) are intentionally discarded for query construction, since they would provide an ambiguous training signal to the descriptor-based nearest-neighbor lookup; at evaluation time, in contrast, all patches in the dataset are scored, and a retrieved patch is counted as a true positive whenever it overlaps a target structure. The split size, which determines the number of slices per group, is set to 10, 20, 30, 50, or 100, corresponding to 10 %, 5 %, 3.33 %, 2 %, and 1 % of labeled slices, respectively. However, for the HeLa-EMBL dataset, which contains only 64 slices, split size 100 is excluded. A k k -value of 1 is used in the nearest neighbor search, which proved optimal for this query selection approach. Figure 5: Illustration of the query selection strategy. We plot the precision-recall curves and compute the average precision, which is the integral over the PR-curve. 4.6.1 Discussion on the PR-curves In Figure 14 , the PR-curves are shown for the HeLa-EMBL dataset. We show the PR-curves for the fine-tuned classical autoencoder, which gave the best overall accuracy (see Section 4.6.2 ). The PR-curves correspond to split sizes of 10 (left, queries from 1 of every 10 slices) and 30 (right, queries from 1 of every 30 slices). The performance improves as the number of query slices increases, likely due to spatial coherence in the dataset, whereby slices at farther distances exhibit less similarity. The points represent reduction rates : the labeled value is the fraction of patches the user no longer needs to inspect, i.e., 1 1 minus the retention rate (the fraction of highest-similarity patches that the user retains and inspects). For reduction rates as high as 70%, we observe a recall of nearly one in most cases, indicating that almost all structures are retrieved from the data while discarding the majority of irrelevant patches. Similar results are observed for the VIB and EPFL datasets. This demonstrates the method’s potential to reduce the search space when searching for a structure of interest, which is highly valuable for biologists. 4.6.2 Discussion on the average precisions In Table 2 , the average precisions (APs) are given for the pretrained and fine-tuned autoencoder models and different combinations of datasets and split sizes. From these results, we can conclude that the fine-tuned models perform significantly better than the pretrained models. In all experiments, the pretrained models are outperformed by the fine-tuned models, sometimes with over 5 % performance increase. To verify this observation statistically, we performed paired one-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank tests over all n = 14 n=14 (dataset, split-size) configurations of Table 2 . Fine-tuning yields a significant improvement both for the classical autoencoder (median Δ ​ AP = + 3.65 \Delta\text{AP}=+3.65 pp, W = 105 W=105 , p = 4.9 ⋅ 10 − 4 p=4.9\cdot 10^{-4} ) and for the variational autoencoder (median Δ ​ AP = + 2.80 \Delta\text{AP}=+2.80 pp, W = 97 W=97 , p = 1.5 ⋅ 10 − 3 p=1.5\cdot 10^{-3} ). Also, the classical autoencoder performs comparatively better than the variational autoencoder, especially after fine-tuning. The corresponding paired one-sided Wilcoxon test (fine-tuned AE vs. fine-tuned VAE, n = 14 n=14 ) confirms that the gap is significant (median Δ ​ AP = + 0.90 \Delta\text{AP}=+0.90 pp, W = 96 W=96 , p = 3.1 ⋅ 10 − 3 p=3.1\cdot 10^{-3} ). In some cases, the fine-tuned variational autoencoder performs worse than the pretrained version, which could be explained by the fact that variational autoencoders are more challenging to train as a descriptor, and we cannot assume that we can simply fine-tune an existing VAE with the same β \beta parameter. This aligns with the conclusions of [ 9 ] , which state that classical autoencoders are perhaps better suited for descriptor models due to the difficulty of training variational autoencoders. Finally, we can also notice different performance gains for the various datasets. These gains are relatively high for the HeLa-EMBL and EPFL datasets but much lower for the VIB dataset. This can be explained by the fact that it is difficult to fine-tune learned features on electron microscopy data in general, as we have only fine-tuned on a few datasets, and these datasets have different statistical properties. In particular, the VIB dataset differs from HeLa-EMBL and EPFL in ways that make descriptor matching more challenging. The structures of interest are smaller relative to the image scale, increasing sensitivity to partial matches, local background clutter, and patch-level misalignment. Moreover, their appearance differs from the corresponding structures in the other datasets, so retrieval cannot rely as strongly on shared global appearance cues such as intensity or texture. Instead, the descriptor must capture more local shape- and boundary-related information. These factors make VIB a more demanding setting and help explain the lower performance observed on this dataset. The VIB results should therefore be interpreted as a conservative estimate of the framework’s performance, rather than as a fundamental limitation. Precision, that is the number of returned patches that overlap with the ground truth regions of the structure of interest, divided by the total number of returned patches. Or the number of true positives (TP) divided by the number of true and false positives (FP). Precision = T ​ P T ​ P + F ​ P \text{Precision}=\frac{TP}{TP+FP} (5) An overlap of 1 pixel is already sufficient because our search framework aims to locate a structure of interest, rather than delineating the exact boundary. 2. Recall, that is the number of pixels of retrieved structures, divided by the total number of pixels of all occurrences of the structure. Or the number of true positives divided by the number of true positives and false negatives (FN). Recall = T ​ P T ​ P + F ​ N \text{Recall}=\frac{TP}{TP+FN} (6) A structure is retrieved if at least one patch overlaps with it. We take into account the number of pixels to give more importance to larger structures. Precision measures the quality of the retrieved patches, that is, what fraction of the retrieved patches truly belong to the structure of interest. Recall measures the completeness of the retrieval, using the pixel-weighted fraction of retrieved structures so that larger structures contribute proportionally more. Ideally, both precision and recall should be 1. We use precision and recall to visualize the Precision-Recall curves (PR-curves), which are often used as an evaluation method for retrieval frameworks. PR-curves are computed using different thresholds, which objectively measure the retrieval performance irrespective of the chosen threshold. Finally, we also compute the area under the PR curves, which is called the average precision (AP). The average precision can be used to compare the performance of different techniques. 4.4 Visual evaluation of the local image descriptors To gain insights into the performance of AEs and VAEs for locating structures of interest, we analyze how different structures are mapped in the latent space using t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (t-SNE) [ 28 ] via scikit-learn [ 29 ] . We plot the precision-recall curves and compute the average precision, which is the integral over the PR-curve. 4.6.1 Discussion on the PR-curves In Figure 14 , the PR-curves are shown for the HeLa-EMBL dataset. We show the PR-curves for the fine-tuned classical autoencoder, which gave the best overall accuracy (see Section 4.6.2 ). The PR-curves correspond to split sizes of 10 (left, queries from 1 of every 10 slices) and 30 (right, queries from 1 of every 30 slices). The performance improves as the number of query slices increases, likely due to spatial coherence in the dataset, whereby slices at farther distances exhibit less similarity. The points represent reduction rates : the labeled value is the fraction of patches the user no longer needs to inspect, i.e., 1 1 minus the retention rate (the fraction of highest-similarity patches that the user retains and inspects). For reduction rates as high as 70%, we observe a recall of nearly one in most cases, indicating that almost all structures are retrieved from the data while discarding the majority of irrelevant patches. Similar results are observed for the VIB and EPFL datasets. This demonstrates the method’s potential to reduce the search space when searching for a structure of interest, which is highly valuable for biologists. 4.6.2 Discussion on the average precisions In Table 2 , the average precisions (APs) are given for the pretrained and fine-tuned autoencoder models and different combinations of datasets and split sizes. From these results, we can conclude that the fine-tuned models perform significantly better than the pretrained models. In all experiments, the pretrained models are outperformed by the fine-tuned models, sometimes with over 5 % performance increase. To verify this observation statistically, we performed paired one-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank tests over all n = 14 n=14 (dataset, split-size) configurations of Table 2 . Fine-tuning yields a significant improvement both for the classical autoencoder (median Δ ​ AP = + 3.65 \Delta\text{AP}=+3.65 pp, W = 105 W=105 , p = 4.9 ⋅ 10 − 4 p=4.9\cdot 10^{-4} ) and for the variational autoencoder (median Δ ​ AP = + 2.80 \Delta\text{AP}=+2.80 pp, W = 97 W=97 , p = 1.5 ⋅ 10 − 3 p=1.5\cdot 10^{-3} ). Also, the classical autoencoder performs comparatively better than the variational autoencoder, especially after fine-tuning. The corresponding paired one-sided Wilcoxon test (fine-tuned AE vs. fine-tuned VAE, n = 14 n=14 ) confirms that the gap is significant (median Δ ​ AP = + 0.90 \Delta\text{AP}=+0.90 pp, W = 96 W=96 , p = 3.1 ⋅ 10 − 3 p=3.1\cdot 10^{-3} ). In some cases, the fine-tuned variational autoencoder performs worse than the pretrained version, which could be explained by the fact that variational autoencoders are more challenging to train as a descriptor, and we cannot assume that we can simply fine-tune an existing VAE with the same β \beta parameter. This aligns with the conclusions of [ 9 ] , which state that classical autoencoders are perhaps better suited for descriptor models due to the difficulty of training variational autoencoders. Finally, we can also notice different performance gains for the various datasets. These gains are relatively high for the HeLa-EMBL and EPFL datasets but much lower for the VIB dataset. This can be explained by the fact that it is difficult to fine-tune learned features on electron microscopy data in general, as we have only fine-tuned on a few datasets, and these datasets have different statistical properties. In particular, the VIB dataset differs from HeLa-EMBL and EPFL in ways that make descriptor matching more challenging. The structures of interest are smaller relative to the image scale, increasing sensitivity to partial matches, local background clutter, and patch-level misalignment. Moreover, their appearance differs from the corresponding structures in the other datasets, so retrieval cannot rely as strongly on shared global appearance cues such as intensity or texture. Instead, the descriptor must capture more local shape- and boundary-related information. These factors make VIB a more demanding setting and help explain the lower performance observed on this dataset. The VIB results should therefore be interpreted as a conservative estimate of the framework’s performance, rather than as a fundamental limitation. Figure 16 reports the results on the VIB dataset with split size 30 30 and a top- 10 10 % similarity threshold.
