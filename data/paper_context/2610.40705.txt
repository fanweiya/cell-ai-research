III Methods III-A Overview DiffUltra addresses foreground–background compatibility through three sequential stages: structural representation of a finding, contextual positioning of the finding within an anatomical background, and task-based filtering of the resulting synthetic images. The pipeline separates generative model training from inference-time synthesis and filtering for downstream detector training (Fig. 1 ; Algorithm 1). • During training, an autoencoder with encoder Enc ⁡ ( ⋅ ) \mathrm{Enc}(\cdot) and decoder Dec ⁡ ( ⋅ ) \mathrm{Dec}(\cdot) learns a compressed latent representation z = Enc ⁡ ( I ) z=\mathrm{Enc}(I) . A latent diffusion model ε θ \varepsilon_{\theta} is then trained on frames annotated with ground-truth bounding boxes, conditioned on a composite condition c = ( F , B ) c=(F,B) constructed from structural representations of real annotated findings (Section III-C ). • During inference, each target image is paired with one of two contextual positioning strategies, INSERT or SWAP. These strategies construct c c using anatomically plausible positions and structurally matched skeletons retrieved from real patient data (Section III-D ). The diffusion model ε θ \varepsilon_{\theta} generates an image from c c , which the SIF (Section III-E ) accepts or rejects before the image is added to the real detector training set (Section III-F ). III-C Structural Representation Conditioning on a finding’s original texture would preserve its low-level appearance and limit diversity, whereas a binary mask would discard echogenic structure. DiffUltra instead uses a tri-level skeleton mask m m that preserves geometric boundaries and coarse echogenicity while letting fine-grained texture vary. Given a pathology region of interest R R cropped from an annotated bounding box, Otsu’s method [ 32 ] progressively segments R R into three echogenicity classes (Fig. 2 ): m : R → { v 1 , v 2 , v 3 } , m:R\rightarrow\{v_{1},v_{2},v_{3}\}, (4) where v 1 v_{1} , v 2 v_{2} , and v 3 v_{3} are assigned to anechoic, hypoechoic, and hyperechoic pixels, respectively. These classes capture acoustic structures relevant to the two LUS pathologies considered: anechoic regions represent fluid such as pleural effusion, hypoechoic regions capture consolidated tissue, and hyperechoic regions capture reflective structures such as the pleural line. This procedure is applied to every annotated finding to construct both the foreground condition F F used in diffusion-model training and the skeleton dictionary 𝒟 \mathcal{D} used for retrieval. Explicitly representing these subregions is intended to reduce failure modes of binary conditioning, including hallucinated content within anechoic fluid regions and blurred boundaries within consolidated tissue. IV EXPERIMENTAL SETTINGS We evaluate DiffUltra at multiple real-data scales to characterize how synthetic augmentation utility changes with training-set size. Experiments use 10%, 50%, and 100% of the real training data, with augmentation volume defined relative to pathology-positive training frames at each scale. IV-A Datasets The autoencoder is pretrained on all labeled and unlabeled data in the training partition, comprising 5.2M frames from 2,110 patients across 19 clinical sites. The diffusion model, skeleton dictionary, synthetic image filter (SIF), and downstream detectors use a fully annotated cohort of 598 patients across 12 sites, comprising 719,997 pleural effusion frames and 746,052 consolidation frames. Patients ranged from 0 to 90 years of age (mean ± \pm SD: 47.3 ± \pm 25.1); 42.3% were female and 57.7% male. Table I summarizes dataset composition. Video-level ground truth was established by majority vote among three independent LUS-trained emergency physicians. Trained researchers annotated frame-level bounding boxes for pleural effusion and consolidation, which were reviewed by at least one physician. Training, validation, and test partitions were split at the subject level. All data were collected under IRB-approved protocols with written informed consent. IV-B Implementation Details Autoencoder: A VQGAN [ 33 ] encodes 512 × 512 512\times 512 frames into 64 × 64 × 8 64\times 64\times 8 latent features [ 10 ] . Training uses L1 reconstruction, structural similarity index measure (SSIM) [ 34 ] , learned perceptual image patch similarity (LPIPS) [ 35 ] , KL loss, and patch-based adversarial loss [ 36 ] . The checkpoint with the lowest total validation loss is selected. Conditional latent diffusion model: Following [ 10 ] , the denoising network uses a Stable Diffusion-style backbone [ 30 ] as in MedFusion [ 37 ] . The denoiser is trained for 100 epochs with batch size 6 using the noise-prediction objective (Eq. ( 2 )) with a scaled-linear noise schedule ( β start = 0.002 \beta_{\mathrm{start}}=0.002 , β end = 0.02 \beta_{\mathrm{end}}=0.02 ). The inference checkpoint is selected based on foreground structural similarity and image mean squared error on the validation set. DDIM sampling uses 150 steps. Structural representation and contextual positioning: The region of interest R R is cropped from each annotated bounding box. For INSERT, the spatial binning function ψ ⁡ ( ⋅ ) \psi(\cdot) discretizes ( Δ ​ x , Δ ​ y , x , y ) (\Delta x,\Delta y,x,y) into an N × N × N × N N\times N\times N\times N grid ( N = 10 N=10 ) to estimate the PMF P ( Δ X , Δ Y | X , Y ) P(\Delta X,\!\Delta Y|X,\!Y) . The anatomical landmark center ( x , y ) (x,y) is obtained from annotated pleural-line boxes. For SWAP, the candidate set uses size tolerance ρ = 1.3 \rho=1.3 and Dice threshold τ Dice = 0.2 \tau_{\text{Dice}}=0.2 . For the skeleton mask m m , echogenicity values are v 1 = 0.24 v_{1}=0.24 , v 2 = 0.5 v_{2}=0.5 , and v 3 = 1 v_{3}=1 . Task-based SIF and downstream detection model: Each detector is a separately trained off-the-shelf YOLOv5n model (2.17M parameters, 3.3 GFLOPs) [ 38 ] , selected for its real-time detection capability [ 39 , 40 ] and lightweight architecture, which supports deployment on resource-constrained ultrasound hardware [ 41 , 42 ] . Detectors are trained for 300 epochs at 512 × 512 512\times 512 resolution using Adam (learning rate 0.001), a linear learning-rate schedule, and default augmentation settings and hyperparameters [ 38 ] . The SIF detector is trained exclusively on real images; the downstream detector uses the same real images plus filtered synthetic images. All experiments use τ conf = 0.1 \tau_{\text{conf}}=0.1 and τ IoU = 0.01 \tau_{\text{IoU}}=0.01 . Models are implemented in PyTorch and trained on NVIDIA L40S GPUs. IV-C Evaluation Protocol and Metrics Frame-level detection is evaluated using average precision at IoU 0.5 (AP@0.5) [ 43 ] . Video-level classification uses AUROC, with the k k -th highest frame-level detection confidence as the video score ( k = 8 k=8 ) [ 14 ] . Each augmentation strategy (INSERT, SWAP, and DiffTumor [ 8 ] ) is compared with real-only training at the same scale; INSERT is also compared with DiffTumor. Differences ( Δ \Delta ) are reported in percentage points. 95% confidence intervals (CIs) and two-sided p p -values are estimated by paired patient-level bootstrap (2,000 iterations) [ 44 ] . Pooled effects are the unweighted mean of per-scale differences computed within each bootstrap iteration. IV-D Baseline Comparisons and Ablation Experiments DiffUltra is compared with real-only training and with DiffTumor [ 8 ] , chosen as the most closely related lesion-insertion method that has demonstrated gains in downstream tumor detection. SIF is applied only to DiffUltra, as DiffTumor has no task-based filtering step. For the ultrasound setting, DiffTumor is adapted by replacing boundary-based placement with the INSERT pathology position sampler and tumor masks with binary skeleton masks that provide basic structural guidance for ultrasound findings. Ablations at the 100% real-data scale assess the contributions of the structural mask, conditional-PMF positional guidance, and task-based filtering. Video-level classification uses AUROC, with the k k -th highest frame-level detection confidence as the video score ( k = 8 k=8 ) [ 14 ] . Each augmentation strategy (INSERT, SWAP, and DiffTumor [ 8 ] ) is compared with real-only training at the same scale; INSERT is also compared with DiffTumor. Differences ( Δ \Delta ) are reported in percentage points. 95% confidence intervals (CIs) and two-sided p p -values are estimated by paired patient-level bootstrap (2,000 iterations) [ 44 ] . Pooled effects are the unweighted mean of per-scale differences computed within each bootstrap iteration. IV-D Baseline Comparisons and Ablation Experiments DiffUltra is compared with real-only training and with DiffTumor [ 8 ] , chosen as the most closely related lesion-insertion method that has demonstrated gains in downstream tumor detection. SIF is applied only to DiffUltra, as DiffTumor has no task-based filtering step. For the ultrasound setting, DiffTumor is adapted by replacing boundary-based placement with the INSERT pathology position sampler and tumor masks with binary skeleton masks that provide basic structural guidance for ultrasound findings. Ablations at the 100% real-data scale assess the contributions of the structural mask, conditional-PMF positional guidance, and task-based filtering. IV-E Targeted Subgroup Experiments Targeted synthesis is evaluated for two data coverage gaps. First, costophrenic views account for 7.4% of training data, compared with 92.6% for intercostal views, and have a higher prevalence of severe pathology. Targeted SWAP augmentation is applied exclusively to costophrenic training data and evaluated on a held-out costophrenic subgroup. Second, an acquisition system absent from training and validation serves as an out-of-distribution subgroup. V Results V-A Detection Performance Across Real-Data Scales Performance when training on real-only data was pathology-dependent (Fig. 5 and Table II ). PE AP increased steadily with data scale, from 0.227 at 10% to 0.372 at 50% and 0.421 at 100% scale. CON AP rose sharply from 0.130 at 10% to 0.258 at 50%, then more gradually to 0.310 at 100%. The effects of synthetic augmentation varied by pathology and real-data scale. Averaged across scales, INSERT significantly improved video-level AUROC over the real-only baseline for both PE (0.9 points; p = 0.017 p=0.017 ) and CON (1.1 points; p < 0.01 p<0.01 ). INSERT also improved AP by an average of 4.5 points for PE ( p < 0.01 p<0.01 ) and 0.8 points for CON (n.s.). For PE, the improvement in AP using INSERT was observed at every data scale, by 6.1, 2.7, and 4.8 points at 10%, 50%, and 100%, respectively. INSERT also improved CON AP at all scales (1.0, 1.2, and 0.2 points), although this did not reach significance. The largest AUROC gains occurred at the small (10%) scale (3.2 points for PE, p < 0.01 p<0.01 ; 2.2 points for CON, p = 0.048 p=0.048 ). The benefit of SWAP was more variable. For PE, SWAP improved AP at 50% and 100% (2.2 and 3.0 points), but not AUROC. For CON, SWAP numerically underperformed the real-only baseline, though differences were not significant. Compared with DiffTumor [ 8 ] , INSERT yielded larger AP gains for both pathologies at every scale, despite retaining fewer images after SIF filtering (Table II ). For PE, both methods improved AP at every scale, but INSERT outperformed DiffTumor by 2.7, 1.4, and 2.8 points at 10%, 50%, and 100% (2.3 points on average; p = 0.065 p=0.065 ). For CON, DiffTumor remained below the real-only baseline in AP at all scales, whereas INSERT consistently exceeded it. Averaged across scales, INSERT outperformed DiffTumor by 1.5 AP points ( p < 0.01 p<0.01 ) and 0.9 AUROC points ( p = 0.011 p=0.011 ). Synthetic insertion improved detection of both pathologies on average across real-data scales, with the largest gains when real training data were scarce. The benefit was pathology-dependent, with larger PE gains that persisted at full scale and smaller CON gains concentrated at low and intermediate scales. This contrast suggests that synthetic-data utility should not be assumed uniform across pathologies. One possible explanation is greater heterogeneity in PE presentation, e.g., variation in fluid volume and loculation, although this was not directly tested. With DiffTumor, binary-mask insertion improved AP for PE but not CON, whereas INSERT improved AP for both pathologies. This pattern is consistent with the tri-level structural representation being particularly valuable for consolidation, whose mixed hypoechoic and hyperechoic content a binary mask cannot capture. These findings support pathology-specific evaluation of synthetic augmentation. INSERT and SWAP also behaved differently. INSERT improved AP at every scale for both pathologies, whereas SWAP improved AP for PE but not for CON (Table II ). Because SWAP replaces an existing finding, it primarily varies pathology appearance rather than anatomical context, and pathologies may differ in their sensitivity to this distinction. SWAP nevertheless improved the targeted costophrenic subgroup (Table IV ), suggesting that the two strategies address different coverage gaps. Because each synthetic image is screened against the intended detections, the same framework supports both general and targeted synthesis. The costophrenic and held-out-system experiments apply identical structural representation, positioning guidance, and task-based filtering criteria to purposefully selected subsets. Targeted synthesis produced additional performance gains for these subgroups at full data scale, indicating that coverage gaps can remain even when aggregate training data are abundant. These experiments also illustrate the value of assessing performance across clinically relevant anatomical and acquisition subgroups rather than relying only on aggregate metrics [ 45 ] . Targeted synthesis may help address identified coverage gaps when additional positive cases from the target subgroup are difficult to obtain. Several limitations should be considered. First, some targeted subgroups are modest in size (13–74 positive cases), so these effect estimates are less precise than those from the full evaluation cohort and require confirmation in larger, independent cohorts before clinical use. Second, SIF shares its architecture with the downstream detector and is trained for a similar task, so the models may share failure modes, allowing systematic errors in synthetic images to pass filtering. This risk may be greater in targeted subgroups, where a small number of undetected failures could disproportionately affect performance. Filtering with ensembles of detectors, and accepting only images on which they agree, may reduce this risk [ 46 ] , [ 47 ] , as may expert review of accepted images. Third, all experiments used a fixed synthetic-to-real ratio ( γ syn = 1 \gamma_{\text{syn}}=1 ), a single detector architecture (YOLOv5n) [ 38 ] for SIF and downstream training, and a single autoencoder and diffusion backbone. YOLOv5n was chosen because it is a widely used real-time detector [ 39 ] , [ 40 ] suitable for deployment on resource-constrained ultrasound hardware [ 41 ] , [ 42 ] . The magnitude of the gains may depend on these choices, and the optimal synthesis ratio may vary by pathology and data scale. Fourth, comparison was limited to DiffTumor [ 8 ] , the most closely related mask-conditioned lesion-insertion method. GAN-based ultrasound synthesis [ 16 ] , [ 23 ] and copy-paste augmentation [ 28 ] were not evaluated. Copy-paste transfers a finding’s original pixels without adapting them to the host anatomy, whereas DiffUltra explicitly addresses this compatibility problem; for GAN-based synthesis, latent diffusion models have compared favorably with GANs in medical image synthesis [ 37 ] . Both are useful reference points for future comparative studies. Fifth, the experiments focus on CON and PE in LUS; generalization to other pathologies, anatomical regions, imaging modalities, acquisition systems, and anatomical references beyond the pleural line (e.g., rib shadows) remains to be established. VII Conclusion We present DiffUltra, a latent diffusion framework for lung ultrasound pathology synthesis that addresses foreground–background compatibility through three stages: structural representation, contextual positioning, and task-based filtering. Synthetic insertion improved detection of both pathologies on average across training-set sizes, with the largest gains when real data were scarce. Targeted synthesis further improved performance in underrepresented anatomical and acquisition subgroups at full data scale. These findings suggest that task-guided synthesis is a promising strategy for addressing residual coverage gaps in ultrasound AI training data.
