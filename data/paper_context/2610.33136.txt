II-B Self-Supervised Denoising Approaches Self-supervised denoising avoids the requirement for clean targets by exploring self-supervision from noisy observations only. Blind-spot methods, such as Noise2Self [ 14 ] , Noise2Void [ 13 ] , PN2V [ 33 ] , Noise2Same [ 34 ] , Self2Self [ 35 ] , and Blind2Unblind [ 36 ] , use masking strategies to enable single-image-based self-denoising. Complementing these approaches, subsampling-based methods, such as Neighbor2Neighbor [ 37 ] , Noise2Fast [ 38 ] , ZSN2N [ 39 ] , and Pixel2Pixel [ 40 ] , construct surrogate training pairs from neighboring pixels. For dynamic and scientific measurements, representative self-supervised methods include UDVD [ 41 ] , UMVD [ 42 ] , SHINE [ 5 ] , and STBN [ 15 ] , which exploit temporal redundancy or interpolation to restore noisy video and microscopy data. In related modalities, SSD [ 43 ] uses spectral structure for low-light Raman hyperspectral reconstruction, whereas bit2bit [ 44 ] models binary particle detections via a Bernoulli observation process. While these methods exploit domain-specific spatial, temporal, or spectral structure, they do not explicitly characterize the exposure-dependent accumulation of Poisson counts. Several approaches more explicitly address non-Gaussian noise. Self-supervised Poisson–Gaussian denoising extends blind-spot learning to mixed-noise settings [ 45 ] ; Poisson2Sparse [ 46 ] combines single-image self-supervision with an unrolled sparse-representation network; and GR2R [ 47 ] generalizes recorruption-based learning to Poisson and other exponential-family distributions. These methods accommodate Poisson statistics, yet they rely on masking, sparse priors, or synthetic recorruption rather than estimating an exposure-dependent particle-arrival rate. PARM estimates this rate from a single observation by Poisson thinning, which splits the measurement into complementary lower-exposure views that remain independent given the latent clean rate and therefore serve as self-supervised targets. II-C Discrete Probability Paths and Count-Process Modeling Flow matching constructs generative models by learning a continuous velocity field along a prescribed probability path [ 16 , 48 ] . Beyond generative sampling, flow-matching models have also been adapted to image inverse problems. For instance, Pourya et al. [ 49 ] use a pretrained flow model to address linear inverse problems through flow-consistent destination estimation, measurement-aware refinement, and time progression, interpreting this procedure as an approximate Bayesian posterior sampling. While these methods operate on continuous image-valued trajectories, recent work has extended this idea to discrete and stochastic state spaces. Specifically, Discrete Flow Matching [ 50 ] characterizes probability evolution over discrete states, whereas Generator Matching [ 17 ] formulates learning through Markov generators or transition rates. These perspectives motivate transition-rate fields as the discrete counterparts to continuous velocity fields. Parallel to these developments, count-valued generative models have also incorporated thinning mechanisms and Poisson processes. For instance, Learning to Jump [ 51 ] employs forward thinning and reverse thickening to model latent counts, while ItDPDM [ 52 ] develops a discrete Poisson diffusion framework that completely avoids continuous relaxation. However, these approaches focus primarily on unconditional sample generation and generative density modeling, do not explicitly use the physical particle-arrival process for conditional image denoising. While PARM draws conceptual motivation from these frameworks, it differs in objective and formulation. Unlike related inverse solvers that rely on pretrained continuous generative priors, PARM directly models the physical accumulation of discrete particle counts with respect to exposure. Its derived transition-rate field provides a principled statistical target for restoration, enabling the network to learn the normalized spatial allocation of particle-arrival rates directly from noisy observations. III Methods III-A Overview: From Observed Counts to Particle-Arrival Rates PARM reframes low-count image denoising as conditional particle-arrival-rate estimation. An observed count image is a stochastic realization of an underlying particle-arrival process. Therefore, rather than directly regressing a clean image from noisy counts, PARM estimates the rate field that statistically explains the observed count state. The threshold Λ th = 0.4 \Lambda_{\mathrm{th}}=0.4 is an empirical operating point: at Λ = 0.4 \Lambda=0.4 , the conditional multi-event fraction Pr ⁡ ( N ≥ 2 ∣ N > 0 ) \Pr(N\geq 2\mid N>0) is about 20 % 20\% . Thus Λ ^ occ \widehat{\Lambda}_{\mathrm{occ}} is a global sparsity indicator, and the consistency term acts as a weak low-count stabilizer. The following section describes how the learned allocation is combined with the observed global particle mass to obtain the final reconstruction. III-G Inference and Observed-Mass-Preserving Reconstruction The rate-allocation formulation separates two quantities: the relative distribution of particle arrivals, inferred by the network, and the global particle mass, obtained from the observation. This separation allows PARM to restore an acquisition-scale image without the network predicting an absolute exposure-dependent magnitude. From Sec. III-C , the conditional rate field is v τ ​ ( y ) = 𝔼 ⁡ [ λ 0 ​ X ∣ Y τ = y ] v_{\tau}(y)=\mathbb{E}[\lambda_{0}X\mid Y_{\tau}=y] , whose normalized form is the Bayes-optimal allocation π τ ∗ ​ ( y ) \pi_{\tau}^{*}(y) of Eq. ( 13 ). The posterior mean of the clean count field satisfies 𝔼 ⁡ [ λ 0 ​ τ ​ X ∣ Y τ ] = τ ​ v τ ​ ( Y τ ) \mathbb{E}[\lambda_{0}\tau X\mid Y_{\tau}]=\tau v_{\tau}(Y_{\tau}) , so the multiplicative factor τ \tau cancels from this normalization. Thus, the allocation is invariant to a common rate–count scaling, although its conditional dependence on the observation distribution may still vary with exposure. When exposure is not provided as a network input, the trained allocation corresponds to the exposure-marginalized predictor of Sec. III-E . Theorem 1 characterizes inputs drawn from the randomized thinning distribution, so applying the trained network to the complete observation Y τ Y_{\tau} is a practical inference rule rather than a direct consequence of the theorem. In the reported experiments, the inference allocation aggregates K = 3 K=3 network evaluations: the original input and sequentially thinned versions are each processed by the network, and their positive candidate fields are combined by empirical score-weighted aggregation (Supplementary Material, Sec. The final PARM reconstruction distributes the observed total count according to this allocation. Let M ^ τ = ∑ j ∈ Ω Y τ , j \widehat{M}_{\tau}=\sum_{j\in\Omega}Y_{\tau,j} denote the observed particle mass over Ω \Omega . Then C ^ τ , i = M ^ τ ​ π θ , i inf ​ ( Y τ ) , \widehat{C}_{\tau,i}=\widehat{M}_{\tau}\,\pi_{\theta,i}^{\mathrm{inf}}(Y_{\tau}), (21) so that ∑ i ∈ Ω C ^ τ , i = M ^ τ \sum_{i\in\Omega}\widehat{C}_{\tau,i}=\widehat{M}_{\tau} : the network determines how the observed particle mass is distributed, while the measurement determines its global magnitude. Poisson additivity makes M ^ τ \widehat{M}_{\tau} a conditionally unbiased estimate of the latent total count M τ = ∑ i ∈ Ω λ 0 ​ τ ​ X i M_{\tau}=\sum_{i\in\Omega}\lambda_{0}\tau X_{i} , and because it is aggregated over Ω \Omega , its relative fluctuation is smaller than that of individual low-count sites. This observed-mass-preserving reconstruction is a mass–allocation factorization, not an exact Bayes estimator: M ^ τ \widehat{M}_{\tau} is conditionally unbiased but is not the posterior mean, so when M ^ τ = 0 \widehat{M}_{\tau}=0 the reconstruction is identically zero even though the posterior mean can be positive. The equivalence result assumes calibrated integer-valued Poisson counts, known exposure intervals, and unrestricted predictors optimized under GKL; the consistency regularizer and observed-mass-preserving reconstruction are practical components outside the theorem’s guarantee (Appendix A-C ). IV Experiments We evaluate PARM in three settings: (i) a controlled synthetic Cameraman benchmark with known ground truth, (ii) standard natural videos corrupted by synthetic Poisson noise, and (iii) real low-dose electron microscopy without clean ground truth. For the first two settings, the clean source data permit quantitative evaluation using peak signal-to-noise ratio (PSNR) and structural similarity index (SSIM). For electron microscopy, we assess structural preservation and agreement with local multi-frame, high-SNR references used only as references, not as ground truth. We compare PARM against three categories of baselines, including model-based frameworks, single-image deep learning methods, and sequence-based deep learning approaches. The model-based methods are BM3D [ 22 ] , VBM4D [ 53 ] , and PURE-LET [ 20 ] . The single-image deep learning methods include Noise2Void (N2V) [ 13 ] and Pixel2Pixel [ 40 ] , while the sequence-based methods are SHINE [ 5 ] , UDVD [ 41 ] , and bit2bit [ 44 ] . Methods without a native volumetric or video formulation (such as BM3D, PURE-LET, and Pixel2Pixel) are evaluated only in the 2D setting, and we do not introduce new 3D backbones solely for comparison. IV-A Implementation Details and Baseline Settings PARM uses a 2D U-Net for single-frame experiments and a 3D U-Net for sequence-based experiments. The corresponding network input is a noisy count image or spatiotemporal count sequence, and neither the clean image nor the exposure level is provided as an additional input. For the synthetic Cameraman benchmark, the 3D network input size is 32 × 64 × 64 32\times 64\times 64 ( T × H × W T\times H\times W ). PARM is optimized separately for each evaluated sequence and particle level. The 2D and 3D networks are initialized from their corresponding architecture-specific checkpoints pretrained on the noisy Flower sequence using the same self-supervised PARM objective. No clean frames or ground-truth targets are used during pretraining. Following initialization, all network parameters are optimized on the noisy target sequence at the corresponding particle level. Thus, the final models are specific to each sequence and particle level, though they share a fixed self-supervised initialization. Because Flower is included in Table II , its result corresponds to same-sequence self-supervised warm-starting rather than evaluation on a previously unseen sequence. We preserve the original training settings of each baseline whenever possible. Sequence-specific self-supervised training is used only when supported by the original method. For N2V, SHINE, and bit2bit, we follow the recommended configurations and change only input dimensions or noise-related parameters required for compatibility with the evaluated data. UDVD is evaluated with its original default configuration, while BM3D and VBM4D use their standard settings. No clean targets are used for PARM or any self-supervised baseline. PARM(Sup.) is trained with clean targets and is included only as an empirical supervised reference. Additional qualitative comparisons on Cameraman, natural video, and electron microscopy are provided in the Supplementary Material. V Conclusion We introduced PARM, a self-supervised framework for low-count image denoising based on particle-arrival-rate matching. Rather than treating denoising as a static regression problem at a fixed exposure, PARM models particle accumulation as an exposure-indexed Poisson counting process on a discrete state space. The Kolmogorov forward equation yields the conditional particle-arrival-rate field, which equals the posterior mean of the clean particle-arrival rate and therefore defines the statistical target for denoising. For exact calibrated Poisson counts, thinning decomposes a single noisy observation into complementary lower-exposure views, whose exposure-normalized count differences provide conditionally unbiased rate targets. Under the GKL divergence, regression to these self-supervised targets and supervised clean-rate regression share the same Bayes-optimal predictor. In implementation, PARM separates the global count scale from the normalized spatial allocation, while bidirectional thinning and soft cross-view consistency improve stability at extremely low counts. On synthetic Poisson data and standard video benchmarks, PARM achieved competitive or leading performance across particle levels while retaining fine structures. On real low-dose electron microscopy data, PARM preserved nanoparticle structures and showed the strongest agreement with group-averaged multi-frame references across the evaluated settings. Because clean microscopy ground truth was unavailable, this comparison demonstrates reconstruction consistency rather than absolute fidelity to an unknown clean image. Future work could extend the framework to mixed Poisson–Gaussian sensor noise, more efficient multiscale architectures, and real-time low-count video and microscopy pipelines. The rate-field perspective links particle-counting models with self-supervised restoration, particularly for microscopy without clean references. The equivalence result is a population statement about the Bayes-optimal predictor.
