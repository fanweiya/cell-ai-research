3 Method 3.1 Problem Statement Let 𝐝 a , 𝐝 b ∈ 𝕊 D − 1 \mathbf{d}_{a},\mathbf{d}_{b}\in\mathbb{S}^{D-1} be ℓ 2 \ell_{2} -normalized descriptors of size D D extracted at the same spatial location from modalities m a , m b ∈ ℳ m_{a},m_{b}\in\mathcal{M} . In practice, 𝐝 a \mathbf{d}_{a} can differ substantially from 𝐝 b \mathbf{d}_{b} , as descriptor responses depend on modality-specific appearance. We therefore seek to bridge this discrepancy by learning a crossing function : C ψ : 𝕊 D − 1 × ℳ × ℳ → 𝕊 D − 1 , 𝐝 ^ = C ψ ​ ( 𝐝 a , m a , m b ) , C_{\psi}:\mathbb{S}^{D-1}\times\mathcal{M}\times\mathcal{M}\to\mathbb{S}^{D-1},\quad\hat{\mathbf{d}}=C_{\psi}(\mathbf{d}_{a},m_{a},m_{b}), (1) that transforms a source descriptor into the target feature space so that 𝐝 ^ ≈ 𝐝 b \hat{\mathbf{d}}\approx\mathbf{d}_{b} . After crossing, standard nearest-neighbor matching in the target space yields cross-modal correspondences. A descriptor encodes two types of information: (i) geometry , the local spatial structure (edges, corners, curvature) that is largely shared across modalities imaging the same scene, and (ii) appearance , modality-specific intensity and contrast patterns. Because structural cues may be attenuated or absent in some modalities (e.g., boundaries visible in MRI but not ultrasound), cross-modal crossing must transform appearance while preserving geometric content. We introduce an architecture that explicitly separates these components (Fig. 2 ). Figure 2 : Overview of CrossFeat. A descriptor 𝐝 a \mathbf{d}_{a} extracted from an image of modality m a m_{a} is encoded by E ϕ E_{\phi} and decomposed into geometry z geom z_{\text{geom}} and appearance z app z_{\text{app}} . The appearance is transformed by the modality-conditioned crosser C ψ C_{\psi} to obtain z ^ app \hat{z}_{\text{app}} compatible with the target modality m b m_{b} , while z geom z_{\text{geom}} is preserved. The decoder G θ G_{\theta} recombines z geom z_{\text{geom}} and z ^ app \hat{z}_{\text{app}} to reconstruct a descriptor 𝐝 ^ \hat{\mathbf{d}} in the target modality space, enabling cross-modal matching without retraining the original descriptor. 3.2 Disentangled Descriptor Crossing A direct approach is to learn a single network 𝐝 ^ = f ψ ​ ( 𝐝 a , m a , m b ) \hat{\mathbf{d}}=f_{\psi}(\mathbf{d}_{a},m_{a},m_{b}) that maps the full descriptor conditioned on the modality pair. This treats the descriptor as a monolithic vector and relies on the network to implicitly discover what to change and what to preserve. In practice, we found that such models either alter geometry or under-transform appearance, ultimately hurting matching. We therefore make the geometry–appearance decomposition architecturally explicit: the descriptor is split into a modality-invariant geometry code and a modality-specific appearance code , and crossing acts only on the latter (See Fig. 3 ). Encoder. A shared encoder E ϕ E_{\phi} maps a descriptor from any modality to a geometry code and an appearance code: E ϕ : 𝕊 D − 1 → ℝ d g × ℝ d a , E ϕ ​ ( 𝐝 ) = ( 𝐳 geom , 𝐳 app ) . E_{\phi}:\mathbb{S}^{D-1}\to\mathbb{R}^{d_{g}}\times\mathbb{R}^{d_{a}},\quad E_{\phi}(\mathbf{d})=(\mathbf{z}_{\text{geom}},\mathbf{z}_{\text{app}}). (2) An MLP backbone produces an intermediate representation from which two separate projection heads yield 𝐳 geom \mathbf{z}_{\text{geom}} and 𝐳 app \mathbf{z}_{\text{app}} . Sharing the backbone across modalities forces the encoder to learn a common representation space; the split into two heads is what enables selective crossing. Because 𝐳 geom \mathbf{z}_{\text{geom}} bypasses the crosser, the architecture guarantees that geometry is unchanged during crossing. The training losses of Section 3.3 provide the complementary pressure, encouraging 𝐳 geom \mathbf{z}_{\text{geom}} to capture modality-invariant structure and 𝐳 app \mathbf{z}_{\text{app}} to absorb modality-specific statistics. The appearance head is variational: it outputs a mean 𝝁 app \bm{\mu}_{\text{app}} and log-variance log ⁡ 𝝈 app 2 \log\bm{\sigma}_{\text{app}}^{2} , from which the appearance code is sampled via the reparameterization [ 22 ] : 𝐳 app = 𝝁 app + 𝝈 app ⊙ 𝜺 , 𝜺 ∼ 𝒩 ⁡ ( 𝟎 , 𝐈 ) . \mathbf{z}_{\text{app}}=\bm{\mu}_{\text{app}}+\bm{\sigma}_{\text{app}}\odot\bm{\varepsilon},\quad\bm{\varepsilon}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I}). (3) The geometry code 𝐳 geom \mathbf{z}_{\text{geom}} remains a deterministic point estimate. This asymmetry is deliberate: geometry must be a stable representation for reliable matching, whereas appearance benefits from stochastic regularization that prevents the encoder from memorizing modality-specific patterns. At inference, we set 𝐳 app = 𝝁 app \mathbf{z}_{\text{app}}=\bm{\mu}_{\text{app}} (no sampling). Crosser. The crossing operation acts exclusively on the appearance code: C ψ : ℝ d a × ℳ × ℳ → ℝ d a , 𝐳 ^ app = C ψ ​ ( 𝐳 app ( a ) , m a , m b ) . C_{\psi}:\mathbb{R}^{d_{a}}\times\mathcal{M}\times\mathcal{M}\to\mathbb{R}^{d_{a}},\quad\hat{\mathbf{z}}_{\text{app}}=C_{\psi}(\mathbf{z}_{\text{app}}^{(a)},m_{a},m_{b}). (4) The crosser combines Feature-wise Linear Modulation (FiLM) [ 38 ] with a residual MLP. Learned modality embeddings for m a m_{a} and m b m_{b} are concatenated and passed through a small network that generates per-dimension affine parameters ( γ , β ) (\gamma,\beta) . The appearance code is first modulated by FiLM with γ \gamma centered at identity for near-identity initialization: 𝐳 mod = ( 1 + γ ) ⊙ 𝐳 app ( a ) + β \mathbf{z}_{\text{mod}}=(1+\gamma)\odot\mathbf{z}_{\text{app}}^{(a)}+\beta . A residual MLP then refines the modulated code, with a skip connection from the original appearance: 𝐳 ^ app = 𝐳 app ( a ) + MLP ⁡ ( 𝐳 mod ) . \hat{\mathbf{z}}_{\text{app}}=\mathbf{z}_{\text{app}}^{(a)}+\mathrm{MLP}(\mathbf{z}_{\text{mod}}). (5) The residual structure ensures that the crosser defaults to an identity mapping before training, and that gradients flow directly to the encoder. Decoder. A decoder G θ G_{\theta} reconstructs a descriptor from the two codes: G θ : ℝ d g × ℝ d a → 𝕊 D − 1 , 𝐝 ^ = G θ ​ ( 𝐳 geom , 𝐳 app ) . G_{\theta}:\mathbb{R}^{d_{g}}\times\mathbb{R}^{d_{a}}\to\mathbb{S}^{D-1},\quad\hat{\mathbf{d}}=G_{\theta}(\mathbf{z}_{\text{geom}},\mathbf{z}_{\text{app}}). (6) The decoder concatenates 𝐳 geom \mathbf{z}_{\text{geom}} and 𝐳 app \mathbf{z}_{\text{app}} , processes them through an MLP, and ℓ 2 \ell_{2} -normalizes the output to project it back onto 𝕊 D − 1 \mathbb{S}^{D-1} . It receives FiLM conditioning on the target modality, allowing it to adapt reconstruction to modality-specific descriptor statistics (e.g., different gradient magnitude distributions across MRI sequences). Full crossing path. At inference, given a source descriptor 𝐝 a \mathbf{d}_{a} from modality m a m_{a} , we encode it, cross its appearance toward target modality m b m_{b} , and decode: 𝐝 ^ = G θ ​ ( 𝐳 geom ( a ) , C ψ ​ ( 𝐳 app ( a ) , m a , m b ) ) . \hat{\mathbf{d}}=G_{\theta}\!\bigl(\mathbf{z}_{\text{geom}}^{(a)},\;C_{\psi}(\mathbf{z}_{\text{app}}^{(a)},m_{a},m_{b})\bigr). (7) The crossed descriptor 𝐝 ^ \hat{\mathbf{d}} lives in the feature space of m b m_{b} and can be matched against target descriptors using standard nearest-neighbor search. Figure 3 : Disentanglement of geometry and appearance. Top: correspondences across descriptors (T2, ceT1, US, FLAIR) obtained after crossing the appearance representation while preserving geometry. Bottom: sensitivity analysis of the latent components. Under rotation, the geometry component z geom z_{\text{geom}} changes significantly more than z app z_{\text{app}} . Conversely, across modality transitions, the appearance component exhibits larger variations than the geometry component, confirming that CrossFeat disentangles modality-dependent appearance from modality-invariant geometric structure. 3.3 Training Objective The losses below serve three purposes: reconstruction of individual descriptors, accurate crossing between modality spaces, and disentanglement of geometry from appearance in the latent codes. Reconstruction loss. The latent codes must retain sufficient information to recover the input. For each modality we encode and decode the descriptor and penalize the cosine distance to the original: ℒ recon = ( 1 − cos ⁡ ( G θ ​ ( 𝐳 geom ( a ) , 𝐳 app ( a ) ) , 𝐝 a ) ) + ( 1 − cos ⁡ ( G θ ​ ( 𝐳 geom ( b ) , 𝐳 app ( b ) ) , 𝐝 b ) ) . \mathcal{L}_{\text{recon}}=\bigl(1-\cos(G_{\theta}(\mathbf{z}_{\text{geom}}^{(a)},\mathbf{z}_{\text{app}}^{(a)}),\,\mathbf{d}_{a})\bigr)+\bigl(1-\cos(G_{\theta}(\mathbf{z}_{\text{geom}}^{(b)},\mathbf{z}_{\text{app}}^{(b)}),\,\mathbf{d}_{b})\bigr). Without this term the encoder is free to discard information, producing degenerate codes that satisfy downstream objectives vacuously. Geometry alignment loss. Co-located descriptors from different modalities image the same physical structure; their geometry codes should therefore agree: ℒ geom = ‖ 𝐳 geom ( a ) − 𝐳 geom ( b ) ‖ 2 2 . \mathcal{L}_{\text{geom}}=\left\lVert\mathbf{z}_{\text{geom}}^{(a)}-\mathbf{z}_{\text{geom}}^{(b)}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}. The squared norm encourages soft alignment while tolerating small differences arising from modality-dependent visibility of structural features. This is the primary disentanglement pressure: it forces modality-specific information out of 𝐳 geom \mathbf{z}_{\text{geom}} and into 𝐳 app \mathbf{z}_{\text{app}} . Crossing loss. The primary supervision for descriptor crossing measures how well a crossed descriptor reproduces the target: ℒ cross = 1 − cos ⁡ ( G θ ​ ( 𝐳 geom ( a ) , C ψ ​ ( 𝐳 app ( a ) , m a , m b ) ) , 𝐝 b ) . \mathcal{L}_{\text{cross}}=1-\cos\!\bigl(G_{\theta}(\mathbf{z}_{\text{geom}}^{(a)},\,C_{\psi}(\mathbf{z}_{\text{app}}^{(a)},m_{a},m_{b})),\;\mathbf{d}_{b}\bigr). (8) The source geometry code 𝐳 geom ( a ) \mathbf{z}_{\text{geom}}^{(a)} is paired with the crossed appearance code and decoded; the result is compared to the actual target descriptor 𝐝 b \mathbf{d}_{b} via cosine distance. Note that geometry comes from the source, not the target. C ψ C_{\psi} must encode appearance that, combined with source geometry alone, reconstructs 𝐝 b \mathbf{d}_{b} . Adversarial modality loss. ℒ geom \mathcal{L}_{\text{geom}} encourages geometry agreement only at paired points. To enforce modality invariance globally, we attach a linear modality classifier p ω p_{\omega} to 𝐳 geom \mathbf{z}_{\text{geom}} and train it with gradient reversal [ 14 ] : ℒ adv = − 𝔼 ⁡ [ log ⁡ p ω ​ ( m ∣ 𝐳 geom ) ] , \mathcal{L}_{\text{adv}}=-\mathbb{E}\bigl[\log p_{\omega}(m\mid\mathbf{z}_{\text{geom}})\bigr], where gradients are negated before reaching the encoder. The classifier learns to predict modality from 𝐳 geom \mathbf{z}_{\text{geom}} ; gradient reversal makes the encoder actively strip modality information, complementing the pointwise constraint of ℒ geom \mathcal{L}_{\text{geom}} . Variational regularization. The variational appearance head (Eq. 3 ) is regularized by a KL divergence toward a learned, modality-conditioned prior [ 21 ] : ℒ kl = KL ( q ( 𝐳 app ∣ 𝐝 ) ∥ p ( 𝐳 app ∣ m ) ) , \mathcal{L}_{\text{kl}}=\KL\!\bigl(q(\mathbf{z}_{\text{app}}\mid\mathbf{d})\;\|\;p(\mathbf{z}_{\text{app}}\mid m)\bigr), where q ⁡ ( 𝐳 app ∣ 𝐝 ) = 𝒩 ⁡ ( 𝝁 app , diag ⁡ ( 𝝈 app 2 ) ) q(\mathbf{z}_{\text{app}}\mid\mathbf{d})=\mathcal{N}(\bm{\mu}_{\text{app}},\diag(\bm{\sigma}_{\text{app}}^{2})) is the encoder posterior and p ⁡ ( 𝐳 app ∣ m ) = 𝒩 ⁡ ( 𝝁 m , diag ⁡ ( 𝝈 m 2 ) ) p(\mathbf{z}_{\text{app}}\mid m)=\mathcal{N}(\bm{\mu}_{m},\diag(\bm{\sigma}_{m}^{2})) is a prior whose mean and variance are learnable parameters indexed by modality m m . Conditioning the prior on the modality label makes this an identifiable Variational Autoencoder (VAE) [ 21 ] : the modality acts as an observed auxiliary variable that enables identification of the latent factors. A free-bits mechanism [ 23 ] prevents posterior collapse by exempting each latent dimension from the penalty when its per-dimension KL falls below a threshold. No KL penalty is applied to 𝐳 geom \mathbf{z}_{\text{geom}} , which remains deterministic. Contrastive discrimination loss. ℒ cross \mathcal{L}_{\text{cross}} encourages a crossed descriptor to be close to its target but provides no incentive to be far from other targets. We add an InfoNCE loss [ 37 ] over crossed–target pairs within the batch: ℒ nce = − 1 B ∑ i = 1 B log exp ⁡ ( cos ⁡ ( 𝐝 ^ i , 𝐝 b , i ) / τ ) ∑ j = 1 B exp ⁡ ( cos ⁡ ( 𝐝 ^ i , 𝐝 b , j ) / τ ) , \mathcal{L}_{\text{nce}}=-\frac{1}{B}\sum_{i=1}^{B}\log\frac{\exp\bigl(\cos(\hat{\mathbf{d}}_{i},\,\mathbf{d}_{b,i})\,/\,\tau\bigr)}{\sum_{j=1}^{B}\exp\bigl(\cos(\hat{\mathbf{d}}_{i},\,\mathbf{d}_{b,j})\,/\,\tau\bigr)}, where τ \tau is a temperature hyperparameter and B B the batch size. This provides hard-negative discrimination, which is critical for nearest-neighbor matching where the correct target must rank above all alternatives. Full objective. The complete loss combines data-fidelity terms with disentanglement pressures and variational regularization: ℒ total = λ r ​ ℒ recon + λ g ​ ℒ geom + λ c ​ ℒ cross + λ adv ​ ℒ adv + λ kl ​ ℒ kl + λ nce ​ ℒ nce . \mathcal{L}_{\text{total}}=\lambda_{r}\mathcal{L}_{\text{recon}}+\lambda_{g}\mathcal{L}_{\text{geom}}+\lambda_{c}\mathcal{L}_{\text{cross}}+\lambda_{\text{adv}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{adv}}+\lambda_{\text{kl}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{kl}}+\lambda_{\text{nce}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{nce}}. (9) where λ r \lambda_{r} , λ g \lambda_{g} , λ c \lambda_{c} , λ adv \lambda_{\text{adv}} , λ kl \lambda_{\text{kl}} , and λ nce \lambda_{\text{nce}} are set to 1.0, 0.5, 1.0, 0.03, 0.01, and 0.5, respectively. These weights were selected once through an offline hyperparameter search over candidate configurations ( ≈ 1000 \approx 1000 runs) and then kept fixed for all reported tasks and domains without retuning. Gradients of ℒ total \mathcal{L}_{\text{total}} update the shared encoder E ϕ E_{\phi} , the appearance crosser C ψ C_{\psi} , and the decoder G θ G_{\theta} jointly. We evaluate our method across three multimodal matching tasks spanning medical imaging, autonomous driving, and satellite imagery: (i) a Medical task, we train on the ReMIND dataset [ 20 ] composed of brain images acquired using ceT1, T2, FLAIR, and ultrasound modalities, and evaluate on BRATS (ceT1–T2, ceT1–FLAIR) [ 34 ] and RESECT (T1–US) [ 51 ] , (ii) a Driving task, where we train on the synthetic EventScape dataset [ 15 ] (RGB, depth, event) or urban images and evaluate on the real-world DELIVER dataset [ 53 ] , and (iii) a Satellite task, where we train on WHU-OPT-SAR (RGB, SAR) [ 25 ] of satellite images and evaluate on QXS-SAROPT [ 18 ] . This yields 10 modality pairs across tasks; the architecture scales to arbitrary N N . Evaluation Metrics. We evaluate CrossFeat against dense matching methods using Success Rate (SR) and the Area Under the Curve (AUC) at different thresholds. In addition, when comparing with sparse methods we also report Precision, Recall, and the average number of matches per image. Training and Implementation details. We extract descriptors at keypoint locations detected independently in each modality. To ensure balanced spatial coverage, we adopt a dual-anchor sampling strategy: half of the training keypoints are detected in the source modality and half in the target modality. This avoids biasing the training distribution toward structures salient in only one modality. Descriptors from all modalities are pooled and whitened via PCA. We train one crossing model per task, each handling all modality pairs within its domain. The crossing network (Sec. 3.2 ) uses latent dimensions d g = D d_{g}{=}D and d a = D / 2 d_{a}{=}D/2 with hidden dimension 2 ​ D 2D , totaling ∼ 0.5 {\sim}0.5 – 0.6 0.6 M parameters. Training takes ∼ {\sim} 1–3 hours on an NVIDIA A100 GPU. Images are contrast-normalized with CLAHE [ 55 ] before descriptor extraction. We train with AdamW [ 30 ] ( β 1 = 0.9 \beta_{1}{=}0.9 , β 2 = 0.999 \beta_{2}{=}0.999 , weight decay 10 − 4 10^{-4} ) and cosine annealing, using batch size 2048. The learning rate is 3 × 10 − 4 3{\times}10^{-4} for all tasks. Training runs up to 500 epochs, with early stopping on validation top-1 accuracy and 40 epochs patience. At test time, we optionally apply a lightweight adaptation step (TTA) after crossing. After an AdaIN warm-up, we run five iterations in which source and target descriptors are matched by mutual nearest neighbors, geometrically consistent pairs are selected by rigid RANSAC, and these inliers are used as pseudo-labels to fit a closed-form ridge regression predicting a small residual correction for the crossed source descriptors. The corrected descriptors are added residually and ℓ 2 \ell_{2} -normalized before the next iteration. Because TTA depends on the model’s own RANSAC inliers, it improves alignment when initial matches are reliable, but can reduce precision when they are too few or noisy. Dense end-to-end matchers do not expose an equivalent per-keypoint descriptor interface, and therefore this TTA procedure is only applied to sparse descriptor-based methods. We first compare CrossFeat against a set of representative sparse matching pipelines combining different descriptors and matchers, including SuperPoint (SP) [ 8 ] , DISK [ 48 ] , ALIKED [ 54 ] , and SIFT [ 31 ] paired with LightGlue (LG) [ 26 ] , as well as SuperPoint paired with MINIMA-LG [ 40 ] and SIFT with nearest-neighbor matching (NN). Table 1 and Figure 4 reports results across the three evaluation tasks. On the Medical task, CrossFeat, applied on SIFT, improves performance across most metrics, achieving the best results in SR and AUC. On the Driving task, baseline methods obtain very low success rates, while CrossFeat substantially increases matching accuracy, again achieving the best SR and AUC values. On the Satellite task, which is particularly challenging, most baselines fail to produce reliable matches, whereas CrossFeat remains the only method achieving non-zero success rates across thresholds, with the strongest performance obtained without TTA. These results demonstrate consistent improvements across domains and modality pairs. CrossFeat primarily improves match reliability rather than match density. It often retrieves fewer correspondences, leading to comparatively lower recall, but the retained matches are more geometrically consistent. This explains the strong SR@1 and AUC@1 values despite modest recall. TTA increases recall by recovering additional matches, but can reduce precision when the initial inlier set is too small or noisy, as in the Satellite task. Dense Methods. We further compare CrossFeat with recent dense matching methods, including MatchAnything [ 16 ] , MINIMA-LoFTR [ 44 , 40 ] , and MINIMA-RoMa [ 12 , 40 ] . Table 2 reports SR and AUC metrics across the three tasks. On the medical task, CrossFeat with TTA achieves the highest AUC across all thresholds and the best SR@1, while MINIMA-RoMa attains the highest SR at larger thresholds. On the driving task, CrossFeat substantially outperforms all baselines, achieving the highest SR and AUC values across thresholds. On the satellite task, dense methods struggle to produce reliable correspondences, whereas CrossFeat remains the only approach achieving consistent success rates and high AUC values. As for runtime , our method takes ∼ \sim 0.5–1 s per image to perform detection, description, crossing, and matching (can be sped up when using LightGlue instead of NN), while dense matching methods require roughly 5 s per image. Applying CrossFeat on SIFT, ALIKED, SuperPoint, and DISK consistently improves their performances. 4.3 CrossFeat improves descriptor performances We perform experiments across multiple medical modality pairs (ceT1–T2, ceT1–FLAIR, T1–US) to analyze how CrossFeat improves descriptor performance. As reported in Figure 6 , applying CrossFeat consistently improves all evaluated descriptors, including SIFT, SuperPoint, ALIKED, and DISK. The gains are particularly pronounced for SIFT, but also observed for learned descriptors, demonstrating that CrossFeat can enhance both classical and learned representations by adapting them to multimodal appearance changes. Figure 5 illustrates the matching improvement on one sample. Using CrossFeat improves robustness to geometric distortions. As shown in Fig. 7 , the error ( Δ \Delta TRE) increases much more slowly under increasing shear when using CrossFeat compared to the baseline without crossing, indicating that crossing the descriptors preserves geometric properties of the original descriptor while improving tolerance to appearance changes. We analyze descriptor discriminability by measuring the cosine similarity between descriptors of matching and non-matching keypoints across modality pairs (Fig. 8 ). Crossing the descriptor shifts the similarity of true correspondences toward higher values while leaving non-matching pairs largely unchanged. This increases the separation between the two distributions and leads to significantly higher sensitive values d ′ d^{\prime} , indicating that CrossFeat improves the ability of the descriptor to distinguish correct matches from distractors in multimodal settings. We evaluate our method across three multimodal matching tasks spanning medical imaging, autonomous driving, and satellite imagery: (i) a Medical task, we train on the ReMIND dataset [ 20 ] composed of brain images acquired using ceT1, T2, FLAIR, and ultrasound modalities, and evaluate on BRATS (ceT1–T2, ceT1–FLAIR) [ 34 ] and RESECT (T1–US) [ 51 ] , (ii) a Driving task, where we train on the synthetic EventScape dataset [ 15 ] (RGB, depth, event) or urban images and evaluate on the real-world DELIVER dataset [ 53 ] , and (iii) a Satellite task, where we train on WHU-OPT-SAR (RGB, SAR) [ 25 ] of satellite images and evaluate on QXS-SAROPT [ 18 ] . This yields 10 modality pairs across tasks; the architecture scales to arbitrary N N . Evaluation Metrics. We evaluate CrossFeat against dense matching methods using Success Rate (SR) and the Area Under the Curve (AUC) at different thresholds. In addition, when comparing with sparse methods we also report Precision, Recall, and the average number of matches per image. Training and Implementation details. We extract descriptors at keypoint locations detected independently in each modality. To ensure balanced spatial coverage, we adopt a dual-anchor sampling strategy: half of the training keypoints are detected in the source modality and half in the target modality. This avoids biasing the training distribution toward structures salient in only one modality. Descriptors from all modalities are pooled and whitened via PCA. We train one crossing model per task, each handling all modality pairs within its domain. The crossing network (Sec. 3.2 ) uses latent dimensions d g = D d_{g}{=}D and d a = D / 2 d_{a}{=}D/2 with hidden dimension 2 ​ D 2D , totaling ∼ 0.5 {\sim}0.5 – 0.6 0.6 M parameters. Training takes ∼ {\sim} 1–3 hours on an NVIDIA A100 GPU. Images are contrast-normalized with CLAHE [ 55 ] before descriptor extraction. We train with AdamW [ 30 ] ( β 1 = 0.9 \beta_{1}{=}0.9 , β 2 = 0.999 \beta_{2}{=}0.999 , weight decay 10 − 4 10^{-4} ) and cosine annealing, using batch size 2048. The learning rate is 3 × 10 − 4 3{\times}10^{-4} for all tasks. Training runs up to 500 epochs, with early stopping on validation top-1 accuracy and 40 epochs patience. At test time, we optionally apply a lightweight adaptation step (TTA) after crossing. After an AdaIN warm-up, we run five iterations in which source and target descriptors are matched by mutual nearest neighbors, geometrically consistent pairs are selected by rigid RANSAC, and these inliers are used as pseudo-labels to fit a closed-form ridge regression predicting a small residual correction for the crossed source descriptors. The corrected descriptors are added residually and ℓ 2 \ell_{2} -normalized before the next iteration. Because TTA depends on the model’s own RANSAC inliers, it improves alignment when initial matches are reliable, but can reduce precision when they are too few or noisy. Dense end-to-end matchers do not expose an equivalent per-keypoint descriptor interface, and therefore this TTA procedure is only applied to sparse descriptor-based methods. We first compare CrossFeat against a set of representative sparse matching pipelines combining different descriptors and matchers, including SuperPoint (SP) [ 8 ] , DISK [ 48 ] , ALIKED [ 54 ] , and SIFT [ 31 ] paired with LightGlue (LG) [ 26 ] , as well as SuperPoint paired with MINIMA-LG [ 40 ] and SIFT with nearest-neighbor matching (NN). Table 1 and Figure 4 reports results across the three evaluation tasks. On the Medical task, CrossFeat, applied on SIFT, improves performance across most metrics, achieving the best results in SR and AUC. On the Driving task, baseline methods obtain very low success rates, while CrossFeat substantially increases matching accuracy, again achieving the best SR and AUC values. On the Satellite task, which is particularly challenging, most baselines fail to produce reliable matches, whereas CrossFeat remains the only method achieving non-zero success rates across thresholds, with the strongest performance obtained without TTA. These results demonstrate consistent improvements across domains and modality pairs. CrossFeat primarily improves match reliability rather than match density. It often retrieves fewer correspondences, leading to comparatively lower recall, but the retained matches are more geometrically consistent. This explains the strong SR@1 and AUC@1 values despite modest recall. TTA increases recall by recovering additional matches, but can reduce precision when the initial inlier set is too small or noisy, as in the Satellite task. Dense Methods. We further compare CrossFeat with recent dense matching methods, including MatchAnything [ 16 ] , MINIMA-LoFTR [ 44 , 40 ] , and MINIMA-RoMa [ 12 , 40 ] . Table 2 reports SR and AUC metrics across the three tasks. On the medical task, CrossFeat with TTA achieves the highest AUC across all thresholds and the best SR@1, while MINIMA-RoMa attains the highest SR at larger thresholds. On the driving task, CrossFeat substantially outperforms all baselines, achieving the highest SR and AUC values across thresholds. On the satellite task, dense methods struggle to produce reliable correspondences, whereas CrossFeat remains the only approach achieving consistent success rates and high AUC values. As for runtime , our method takes ∼ \sim 0.5–1 s per image to perform detection, description, crossing, and matching (can be sped up when using LightGlue instead of NN), while dense matching methods require roughly 5 s per image. Consistent improvements are observed across different descriptors and modality pairs (see Fig 5 and 6 ). Robustness experiments further show that crossing maintains geometric consistency under distortions (see Fig 7 ), while the similarity analysis demonstrates increased separation between matching and non-matching descriptors, leading to improved discriminability in multimodal conditions (see Fig 8 ). Across all tasks, CrossFeat consistently outperforms sparse matching pipelines (See Tab. 1 ), particularly in challenging modality gaps where baseline methods often fail to produce reliable correspondences. Despite operating within the classical detector–descriptor–matching pipeline, CrossFeat achieves performance that is competitive with, and in several cases superior to, recent dense matching approaches (See Tab. 2 ). Importantly, CrossFeat is substantially more efficient than dense matchers during both training and inference, since it operates on sparse descriptors and relies on a lightweight crossing network rather than large correlation-based architectures, resulting in ∼ \sim 100 × \times fewer parameters and ∼ \sim 5-10 × \times faster inference. A limitation of the current formulation is that the modality pair must be specified at inference time, as the crossing network learns modality-dependent transformations. Future work will explore estimating the modality gap directly from descriptors or image statistics and using this signal to guide the crossing process. Such a mechanism could adaptively determine the required appearance transformation and enable more flexible operation when modalities are unknown or vary continuously. Overall, these results suggest that bridging the modality gap at the descriptor level is an effective strategy for multimodal correspondence. By enabling existing descriptors to generalize across sensing modalities, CrossFeat offers a simple and efficient alternative to training modality-specific descriptors or adopting entirely new matching architectures.
