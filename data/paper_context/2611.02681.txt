2 Methods 2.1 Model architecture and training HistoTx is a multimodal foundation model that jointly encodes tissue morphology from histology images and gene expression from spatial transcriptomics data (see figure 1 ). It comprises a vision transformer based morphology encoder as the backbone, and a gene expression encoder. 2.1.1 Unimodal tokenization We train on a mixture of Visium spot-based and probe-based Xenium data. Xenium provides individual transcript coordinates, enabling tokens to be constructed at arbitrary spatial resolution by counting per-gene detections within non-overlapping T × T T\times T pixel regions, each yielding one transcript count vector. By varying the size of T T , we can adapt the number of transcript tokens. For an image of size 224 × 224 224\times 224 pixels, this yields 256, 64, 16, or 1 tokens for T = 14 , 28 , 56 , or ​ 256 T=14,28,56,\text{or}~~256 respectively. The physical area covered by each token depends jointly on T T and the image resolution: at mpp = 0.5 \text{mpp}=0.5 , T = 14 T=14 corresponds to a 7 × 7 ​ μ ​ m 2 7\times 7\mu m^{2} region, while at mpp = 2.0 \text{mpp}=2.0 , T = 56 T=56 covers 112 × 112 ​ μ ​ m 2 112\times 112\ \mu m^{2} . By varying T T and mpp, tokens can be constructed at different spatial scales, capturing structures from subcellular to tissue-level. Since Visium data is spot-based, providing only aggregated expression at fixed spot locations rather than individual transcript coordinates, we follow HEST-1k ( Jaume et al., 2024 ) and extract 224 × 224 224\times 224 pixel images at mpp = 0.5 \text{mpp}=0.5 centered on each spot yielding one transcript token per image. At coarser resolutions, a single image encompasses multiple spots, up to 4 and 16 at mpp = 1 \text{mpp}=1 and mpp = 2 \text{mpp}=2 respectively, each contributing its aggregated count vector as a separate transcript token. Transcript count vectors are encoded by a gene expression encoder: here we use the pretrained gene foundation model Geneformer ( Theodoris et al., 2023 ) , producing transcript token embeddings of dimension D expr D_{\text{expr}} . Images are tokenized via the patch embedding layer of the morphology encoder, producing N I N_{I} image token embeddings of dimension D morph D_{\text{morph}} per image. We deliberately decouple the spatial resolution of transcript tokens from that of image tokens for two reasons: first, high-resolution Xenium data enabling fine-grained transcript tokenization is scarce and decoupling allows Visium data to complement Xenium in training; second, at very high resolution, individual transcript tokens may be dominated by noise rather than meaningful signal. 2.1.2 Multimodal fusion After tokenization, transcript embeddings are projected to match the image token dimension via a linear layer and rescaled to match the norm of the image tokens. The morphology encoder’s positional embeddings are then applied to both modalities; when the number of transcript tokens N T N_{T} differs from the number of image tokens N I N_{I} , positional embeddings are adapted to the transcript spatial grid via dynamic spatial resampling ( Dosovitskiy et al., 2021 ) . The fused sequence [ CLS ∣ 𝐳 I 1 : N I ∣ 𝐳 G 1 : N G ] [\text{CLS}\mid\mathbf{z}_{I}^{1:N_{I}}\mid\mathbf{z}_{G}^{1:N_{G}}] of length 1 + N I + N G 1+N_{I}+N_{G} is passed through the remaining ViT blocks. The output CLS token serves as the global multimodal representation, while patch and transcript tokens occupy the remaining positions. At inference, the model supports CLS, mean, max, or flatten pooling over the output sequence. 2.1.3 Self-supervised learning Starting from a pathology foundation model pretrained on large WSI collections, we continue pretraining on paired image-transcript data using DINOv2. To accommodate the multimodal setting, we extend the notion of an image view to image-transcript pairs. For Xenium data, the random crop and resize transformation is applied jointly to both modalities, retaining only transcript detections falling within the cropped region; the cropped image then undergoes standard photometric augmentation in DINO transform (colour jitter, Gaussian blur, etc.). To introduce a cross-modality learning signal within the DINOv2 framework, transcripts are included only in global views while local views remain image-only, such that global CLS tokens are multimodal and local CLS tokens are image-only. The standard DINO local crop loss then enforces consistency between these two, driving cross-modal knowledge distillation from transcripts into the visual encoder. (see figure 1 B). For Visium data, where we always obtain one single transcript token per patch, the token is retained for global views and omitted for local views. Our experiments are conducted primarily on a ViT-B/14 backbone initialized from an internal Midnight ( Karasikov et al., 2025 ) checkpoint of the same size. The gene expression encoder uses the gf-6L-10M-i2048 variant of GeneFormer, kept frozen throughout training. Full model and training hyperparameters are reported in Appendix A . Since, HEST1k contains H&E images with spot-based (Visium Spatial Transcriptomics) or Xenium data we use this data for multimodal training, as well as image-only training. 3 Results We trained a family of HistoTx models differing in how expression tokens are constructed from Xenium data; implementation details and full results are provided in Appendix A , Tables 3 and 4 . Here we focus on the variant using one expression token per Visium spot and 16 expression tokens per Xenium image of size 224x224 px. We hypothesize that too many small tokens introduce noise as transcript counts at very fine spatial scales are unreliable, while too few tokens sacrifice spatial resolution and molecular detail. We compare HistoTx against two baselines: the vision-only foundation model used as our starting point, and SEAL ( Hemker et al., 2026 ) , which adapts a pretrained image encoder via CLIP-style multimodal fine-tuning. For SEAL we use the CONCH backbone, as CONCH is also a ViT-B model, comparable in scale to our HistoTx backbone. Downstream benchmarks include image-only classification (Camelyon16, Panda Radboud), image-only segmentation (Tan Tock Seng, MoNuSAC), and gene expression prediction in both image-only and multimodal settings (HEST-Bench, HEST-Expr); see Appendix B for task descriptions. HistoTx outperforms the image-only baseline on most benchmark tasks, with the most notable gains on gene expression prediction. On HEST-Bench, which requires predicting 50 highly variable genes from H&E patches, Pearson correlation improves from 0.31 to 0.38. This improvement is partially attributable to multimodal pretraining, though increased exposure to HEST images during continued pretraining may also contribute. HistoTx also outperforms the contrastive SEAL-CONCH baseline on this task. On HEST-Expr (predicting 380 fixed-panel genes from H&E) and BEAT (predicting 100 Xenium add-on genes from H&E of the ETOP BEAT-meso data), HistoTx improves Pearson correlation over the image-only baseline from 0.26 to 0.36 and from 0.53 to 0.66 respectively. Providing non-target gene expression at inference yields further gains: HEST-Expr reaches 0.36 with 1,000 input genes, and BEAT reaches 0.66 with 380 input genes, demonstrating HistoTx ’s ability to leverage multimodal inputs. A supervised baseline trained directly for the BEAT prediction task ( Martinelli et al., 2026 ) — using a similar tokenization but task-specific supervision — outperforms HistoTx . We improve over the image-only base most clearly on regression (HBench, HexprIO, BEATIO) and segmentation tasks (CNP, MNS, TTS), improving 0.01 to 0.08 points over the baseline. On multi-class classification tasks, we observe performance drops on breast cancer benchmarks (BACH, BRACS, BreakHis) and mixed results on prostate tasks: we see improvement on PANDA-Radboud but a loss on Gleason TMA (Tables 3 & 4 ). These tasks share a common difficulty: distinguishing visually similar cancer subtypes. Continued multimodal pretraining may reorient the encoder toward gene-expression-aligned features, weakening the subtle morphological detail these subtype tasks rely on. Whether this is tissue-specific or task-specific remains open. In summary we trained HistoTx , an early-fusion multimodal transformer that integrates H&E image and spatial transcriptomics tokens into a shared ViT token sequence, by continuing DINOv2 self-supervised pretraining on paired image and ST data from a vision-only pathology foundation model. HistoTx outperforms the image-only baseline and SEAL-CONCH on gene expression prediction tasks HEST-Bench and HEST-Expr, and shows further improvements when transcript inputs are available at inference. Performance on image-only classification tasks is largely preserved, though drops on breast cancer benchmarks suggest a potential trade-off between multimodal representation learning and fine-grained subtype discrimination. Appendix A Model details A.1 Training data • 7924 TCGA ( TCGA, 2025 ) diagnostic slides, also used in training the baseline backbone, are used to preserve the model’s base image processing capabilities. • HEST-1k (v1.1.0): 1,229 WSIs spanning 26 organs and two organisms (human and mouse), comprising 65 Xenium samples and 1,164 spot-based expression samples (Visium and Spatial Transcriptomics) ( Jaume et al., 2024 ) . From this we constructed paired image-transcript samples from human Xenium and Visium data at varying image and transcript token resolutions, as well as image-only samples from the WSIs. We follow the official HEST-Bench train/test split, using only the training split during pretraining. • ETOP BEAT-meso data from the ETOP IBCSG Partners Foundation consisting 163 mesothelioma patients with paired H&E and Xenium measurements using the core Human Immuno-Oncology Panel (380 genes) and an additional custom panel (100 genes) used for both paired H&E and Xenium training as well as image-only training. We used 122 samples for training and 41 samples for testing. A.2 Architecture We trained a family of multimodal foundation models that jointly encode tissue morphology and gene expression. The morphology branch is a ViT-B/14 backbone, initialized from a DINOv2 checkpoint pretrained in-house on pathology data and fine-tuned during training. The gene expression branch is a frozen Geneformer encoder (gf-6L-10M-i2048) ( Theodoris et al., 2023 ) . Its output passes through a BatchNorm1d layer over the embedding dimension, which re-scales each feature to zero mean and unit variance (with a learned affine transform), and is then linearly projected to the morphology embedding dimension. Before fusion, every expression token is additionally rescaled in L2 norm to match its corresponding morphology token (when expression and morphology tokens are aligned one-to-one) or to the mean norm of the morphology patch tokens (when expression tokens are aggregated). The two streams are then fused by token concatenation before being projected by the DINO head. The morphology features were sized as 16 × \times 16 tokens per patch. Variants of the model differ only in how gene expression is aggregated into tokens (Section A.6 ); the rest of the architecture, optimization, and data pipeline is shared across all configurations. A.3 Self-supervised framework Models were trained with the DINOv2 objective including the iBOT masked-image-modeling auxiliary loss ( Oquab et al., 2024 ) . The full criterion is a weighted sum of a DINO loss (weight 1.0), an iBOT loss (weight 1.0), and a KDE regularizer (weight 0.05). Teacher targets were produced with Sinkhorn–Knopp centering and a warmup teacher temperature of 0.07. The DINO projection head followed the standard three-layer MLP design with input dimension 768, hidden dimension 2048, bottleneck dimension 384, and output dimension 131’072. The last layer of the head was kept frozen for the first three epochs of training. Both the morphology backbone and the projection head were initialized from the same in-house DINOv2 pathology run; the head’s prototypes were therefore warm-started rather than learned from scratch. A.4 Optimization We optimized with AdamW ( β = ( 0.9 , 0.999 ) \beta=(0.9,0.999) ) at a base learning rate of 2 × 10 − 4 2\times 10^{-4} and an initial weight decay of 0.04. The learning rate followed a cosine schedule with a 10-epoch linear warmup, decaying to 10% of the base value over 200 epochs. Weight decay was cosine-annealed from 0.04 to 0.2, and the EMA teacher momentum was cosine-annealed from 0.994 to 1.0. Training used BF16 mixed precision, gradient clipping at 1.0, distributed data parallelism (DDP), and a fixed random seed of 42. All models were trained for at most 200 epochs. A.5 Data and patch extraction Training mixed six data streams via weighted sampling with equal weights for all datasets: BEAT-MESO images (17%), BEAT-MESO images paired with Xenium transcripts (17%),HEST-Xenium with paired spatial transcripts (17%), TCGA image-only (17%), HEST image-only (17%), and HEST spot-level expression supplied as cell sentences (17%). HEST–Xenium patches were filtered to retain at least 100 transcripts and 10 unique genes; HEST cell-sentence inputs were truncated to a maximum of 200 genes per spot. Image patches were 224 × 224 224\times 224 pixels and were sampled at multiple resolutions: 0.5, 1.0, and 2.0 µm/px for HEST–Xenium, and 0.25, 0.5, 1.0, and 2.0 µm/px for the TCGA and HEST image-only streams. We applied DINOv2 multi-crop augmentation with two global views ( 224 × 224 224\times 224 ) and six local views ( 112 × 112 112\times 112 ). iBOT block-wise masking was applied with probability 0.5 and a mask ratio drawn uniformly from [ 0.1 , 0.5 ] [0.1,0.5] .
