Results Overview of Multi4D Multi4D is an end-to-end multi-component classification framework for 4D-STEM data, as summarized in Fig. 1b. The overall architecture takes as input an unlabeled experimental diffraction dataset and a candidate set of crystal structures represented by crystallographic information files (CIFs) (see Supplementary Table 1). These candidate structures are enumerated from prior knowledge, complementary characterization, or physically motivated hypotheses regarding the interface composition. Multi-slice simulations are then performed over random crystal orientations to generate a labeled diffraction dataset. By integrating experimental measurements with the simulation-derived prior, Multi4D assigns each diffraction pattern to one candidate component and thereby produces a spatially resolved component map of the interface. It is worth to emphasize that before entering the neural network workflow, experimental DPs are preprocessed to correct tractable experimental variations. This preprocessing includes beam-center and elliptical-distortion corrections Savitzky et al. (2021) , together with denoising Sadri et al. (2024) , to reduce systematic geometric artifacts and experimental noise while preserving the diffraction features required for downstream domain alignment and multi-component classification. After these corrections, the DPs are examined for signals beyond the transmitted central disk. Probe positions showing neither off-center diffraction spots nor a diffuse amorphous ring is treated as empty, while patterns containing only an amorphous ring and no discrete Bragg reflections are labeled as purely amorphous. These patterns are removed before candidate-component analysis. Because this screening is based on directly observable diffraction features, rather than a learned model, the neural network can focus on the more difficult task of identifying overlapping crystalline components in heterogeneous regions. Next, we illustrate the two modules in Multi4D in details. The first module of Multi4D, termed Sim2real, is designed to address the first challenge outlined above related to the domain gap between idealized diffraction simulations and experimental data (Fig. 2a). In experimental 4D-STEM, DPs essentially are the scattering information arise from not only the underlying crystallographic structure, but also the dynamical scattering, orientational overlap, reduced crystallinity, crystal defects, thickness variation, and low-dose acquisition. Taken together, these effects can weaken, broaden, or obscure high-frequency Bragg features that are critical for reliable component identification. By contrast, multi-slice simulations remain comparatively idealized and merely encode the diffraction response of the presumable structure under controlled conditions. In our experiments, we found that classifiers trained directly on simulated DPs are poorly generalized to experimental data. Multi4D therefore introduces a dedicated Sim2real module that translates labeled simulated DPs into experimental-style counterparts. We formulated the translation as a physics-preserving domain alignment problem rather than generic image stylization. Although experimental diffraction appearance can vary across material systems, microscopes, specimen thicknesses, and acquisition conditions, the Bragg configuration that encodes structural identity must remain unchanged. The Sim2real module is therefore designed to include experimental appearance statistics while preserving the diffraction geometry required for downstream classification. In addition, because simulated and experimental DPs are not naturally paired, Multi4D constructs pseudo-paired guidance through retrieval and a shared latent diffraction representation as shown in Fig. 2b. For each simulated input, the model uses attention-based matching in a global feature space to retrieve the most similar experimental reference. The retrieved examples thus guide the translation toward the target experimental domain by capturing its specific imaging characteristics, without introducing arbitrary peak displacements. To further constrain the process within a physical framework, both simulated and experimental DPs are encoded into a shared latent space using a diffraction-specialized autoencoder. A retrieval-conditioned latent Diffusion Transformer then performs the translation under explicit physics-based constraints. Within this shared latent space, dual style embeddings encode complementary spatial and textural characteristics of experimental diffraction, including background statistics, dynamical scattering, and intensity distributions (Fig. 2d). The translation is further constrained by Gram loss and peak-value L1 loss, which align higher-order appearance statistics with the experimental domain while preserving Bragg peak geometry (Fig. 2e). In this way, the Sim2real module achieves domain alignment while maintaining the structural fidelity of the simulated DPs. The second module of Multi4D performs final component recognition using a rotation-invariant classifier, termed RIC-CNN (Fig. 2e) Mo and Zhao (2024) . This module is designed to handle the strong rotational variation commonly observed in DPs, which can be difficult for standard vision models. Once the patterns are recentered, rotating DPs usually indicates a change in local crystal orientation rather than a different material component. The classifier must therefore remain insensitive to orientation while retaining the Bragg-pattern features needed to distinguish crystallographic components. Specifically, RIC-CNN replaces standard convolutions with rotation-invariant coordinate convolutions defined with respect to the diffraction centre (Fig. 2f) Mo and Zhao (2024) ; Dai et al. (2017) . Rather than relying on a fixed Cartesian sampling grid, these operations are formulated in a centre-referenced coordinate system, allowing equivalent diffraction structures to remain comparable under arbitrary in-plane rotation. By stacking such rotation-aware operations, RIC-CNN learns feature representations that are robust to rotational variation while preserving structurally informative diffraction geometry. Together, these two modules define the core logic of Multi4D. Sim2real aligns simulated diffraction patterns with the experimental domain while preserving diffraction structure, whereas RIC-CNN enables robust component recognition despite rotational variation. Their integration allows direct, automated, and spatially resolved identification of local crystallographic components in complex 4D-STEM datasets. Independent benchmarking of the Sim2real and RIC-CNN modules To benchmark the two core modules of Multi4D, we performed separate evaluations of Sim2real and RIC-CNN. For Sim2real, domain alignment was quantified using Fréchet inception distance (FID), kernel inception distance (KID), and LPIPS against experimental diffraction data in Fig. 2g–i. To assess whether the translation preserved the crystallographic information in the original simulation without introducing spurious diffraction features, we further measured LPIPS between the translated outputs and the simulated inputs (Fig. 2j). We compared our method against InstructPix2Pix and ControlNet in addition to the unmodified simulated patterns (Origin) and a simple noise-augmented baseline (+Noise) as references. Overall, Sim2real outperformed other models on all three metrics of experimental-domain alignment, while ranking second to the simulated input in source-consistency comparison. These results confirm that Sim2real narrows the simulation–experiment gap while preserving the crystallographic content of the DPs. In addition, RIC-CNN was benchmarked against representative convolutional and transformer-based architectures on both five-class and twenty-class classification tasks using the Sim2real-aligned diffraction dataset. The validation curves show that RIC-CNN consistently outperformed the baseline models in both settings, particularly in twenty-class regime in Fig. 2k,l). RIC-CNN exhibited the strongest rotation invariance among the compared models (Fig. 2n). Taken together, these results validate the effectiveness of the two core modules underlying Multi4D. Accurate multi-component classification of experimental 4D-STEM diffraction patterns We next benchmark Multi4D as an end-to-end workflow on 4D-STEM dataset acquired from various nanoparticles comprising Au, ZnO, TiO 2 , Pd, and Co 3 O 4 . This dataset serves as a standard test for crystallographic classification, as it represents a diverse range of crystal structures and varying nanoparticle morphologies under realistic conditions. On this experimental benchmark, Multi4D achieved a classification accuracy of 98.82%. Notably, the model occasionally fails to correctly classify highly symmetric DPs, representing a limitation in orientation-agnostic mapping. Nonetheless, the representative results, illustrated in Fig. 3a–d, show precise agreement between the translated experimental diffraction patterns and their simulated crystallographic templates along with accurate identification of the nanoparticle boundaries. To determine whether these performance gains require our integrated architecture, we compared the full Multi4D workflow against standard computer vision backbones and individual ablation baselines under identical training conditions. Specifically, we compared the full framework against a conventional backbone trained directly on simulated DPs, an RIC-CNN classifier without Sim2real alignment, and a Sim2real-enhanced backbone lacking RIC-CNN. The complete Multi4D model achieved the highest accuracy and macro F1 score on the five-class benchmark shown in Fig. 3e,f, demonstrating that neither domain alignment nor rotation-invariant recognition alone is sufficient. A robust multi-component classification requires the synergistic integration of both modules within a unified workflow. Beyond peak performance, we examined how each model responded to increasing amounts of simulated training data in Fig. 3g. The full Sim2real + RIC-CNN workflow achieved the highest experimental-domain accuracy at every training fraction, with the clearest advantage in the low-data regime. Trained on the same Sim2real-aligned dataset, RIC-CNN substantially outperformed ConvNeXtV2 when only 5% of the training data were available and remained more accurate as the dataset increased, although the gap gradually narrowed. Because the domain-alignment component was identical in this comparison, the result points to the classifier architecture as the main source of the difference. In particular, the rotation-invariant design of RIC-CNN is better suited to the rotational variability of diffraction patterns, enabling the network to learn crystallographic features from fewer training examples than a general-purpose vision backbone. A different trend emerged when the classifiers were trained directly on unaligned simulated data. Neither model showed a consistent improvement in experimental-domain accuracy as more simulated data were added. Instead, their performance fluctuated and declined at larger training fractions, indicating negative scaling under simulation-to-experiment domain shift. This pattern is consistent with the models becoming increasingly dependent on simulation-specific cues that do not transfer reliably to experimental diffraction patterns. Although the result does not by itself prove simulation-domain overfitting, it shows that increasing the amount of unaligned simulated data is not sufficient to improve cross-domain generalization and may even reduce it. Taken together, these findings indicate that Sim2real alignment is necessary for additional simulated data to translate into better experimental performance, while the diffraction-specific design of RIC-CNN provides a further advantage, particularly when training data are limited. Resolving a complex superconducting interface with Multi4D We further apply the Multi4D on a commercialized superconductor device with structurally complex superconducting interface that dictate its performance. Unlike the controlled benchmark setting of which the sample is structurally simple in Fig. 4, the focus ion beam (FIB) cross-section of the device exhibits highly heterogeneous landscape with crystalline and amorphous regions mixed at nanoscale. As shown in Fig. 4, Multi4D resolves different phases across the interface, identifying an upper CeO 2 layer, ultrathin LaMnO 3 /MgO interfacial regions, a buried Y 2 O 3 layer, and a lower amorphous Al-containing layer. More important, a close examination of the interface shows an unprecedented spatial resolution down to single pixel that is approximately 1 nm 2 . Within the Y2O3 layer, Multi4D successfully identifies several embedded CeO2 nanodomains in Fig. 4c. These nanoscale features are difficult to resolve using conventional imaging techniques and fall below the detection limit of energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX) due to their small volume fraction and projection overlap. Resolving the embedded domains is of high physical and technological significance because such secondary-phase impurities can act as localized defects or flux-pinning centers deteriorate the superconducting property. This phase mapping is supported by three validations. First, the reconstructed components are consistent with prior structural knowledge and the real-space Z-contrast observed in the bright-field image. Second, representative diffraction patterns extracted from each layer closely match the simulated diffraction patterns of the corresponding crystal structures, while the lower Al-containing layer exhibits the diffuse halo characteristic of an amorphous AlOX phase. Third, the classification is corroborated by energy dispersive X-ray spectrometer (EDX) elemental mapping in Fig. 4d and e, where Ce is enriched in the upper layer, La, Mn and Mg are localized near the buffer interface, Y is concentrated in the seed layer, and Al and O dominate the lower region. This high consistency among structural prior knowledge, diffraction-pattern matching, and chemical mapping provides strong orthogonal validation for the recovered phase assignments. Taken together, these results demonstrate that Multi4D can robustly disentangle multiple adjacent phases in a real superconducting interface rather than only in standard reference specimens, extending the validation of the framework to experimentally heterogeneous engineering materials. Notably, within the reconstructed phase mapping in Fig. 4b, while bulk-like regions are assigned as well-defined single phases, the interfacial transition zones exhibit remarkable local variation with overlapping yet faint Bragg peaks as shown in Fig. 4c. This indicates that hard phase labels alone are insufficient to fully describe the structural heterogeneity along the projection direction. This physical limitation of standard classification models motivates the introduction of a quantitative descriptor for local interfacial complexity in the following section. Predictive uncertainty reveals local interfacial structural complexity Although the superconductor served as an effective model system, its diffraction patterns at each probe position typically originate from only a single constituent. In more complex heterogeneous interfaces, overlapping diffraction patterns from multiple phases along the beam direction (z-axis) are frequently observed, which poses a major challenge for resolving individual components. However, Multi4D provides a mathematically natural route to quantify this complexity because the classifier outputs a probability distribution over the physically plausible candidate phases. Rather than treating this distribution solely as a confidence score, we interpret its spread as predictive uncertainty within the candidate structural space. To project this uncertainty into a spatially resolved descriptor of interfacial heterogeneity, we define diffraction-inferred structural complexity (DISC) as the normalized predictive entropy of the classifier output. In fact, the rationale for defining such a quantity from the network output is rooted in the structure of the Multi4D pipeline. The largest entry in this vector determines the primary component assignment, but the full distribution contains additional information about how decisively the diffraction signal favors that assignment over competing alternatives. The DISC is thereby defined as: DISC = − ∑ k = 1 K p k ​ log ⁡ p k log ⁡ K , \mathrm{DISC}=-\frac{\sum_{k=1}^{K}p_{k}\log p_{k}}{\log K}, (2) Under this formulation, DISC approaches 0 when the classifier assigns most of the predicted probability to a single component, indicating a decisive structural assignment. Conversely, DISC approaches 1 when the predicted probability is distributed more evenly across multiple plausible components, indicating greater assignment uncertainty within the candidate structural space. The distribution of DISC can be calculated across the entire dataset, reflecting how uniquely the measured diffraction signal supports the principal structural assignment. Therefore, this parameter provides a quantitative and spatially resolved description of local interfacial heterogeneity derived directly from predictive uncertainty. We emphasize that the classifier is trained on physically grounded simulated diffraction patterns from a restricted candidate set rather than random one. Consequently, the output probability vector does not merely represent an abstract confidence score, but provides an operational estimate of how strongly the measured signal can be attributed to each candidate phase. For instance, in bulk-like regions, the diffraction signal matches a single component, producing a sharply peaked probability distribution (DISC=0). Conversely, at transition zones or disordered boundaries, the signal shares similarities with multiple candidates, distributing the probability mass more broadly when DISC closed to 1. Rather than measuring phase fractions or compositional mixing ratios, DISC quantifies the degree to which a diffraction response can be uniquely assigned to a single structural class under the candidate set considered. This entropy-based descriptor is validated by nanoparticle references and independently corroborated on the superconducting interface via prior structural knowledge, diffraction-pattern matching, and EDX mapping. Based on these results, we propose that DISC can serve as a standard parameter in 4D-STEM structural identification. Visualizing the spatial distribution of DISC alongside classification maps ensures the fidelity of the phase assignments while revealing local variations that are invisible to hard, discrete classification alone. Incorporating this parameter enables Multi4D to offer a quantitative, spatially resolved description of structural complexity across diverse material systems, moving automated microscopy closer to high-throughput industrial quality control. Generalization across diverse interfacial material systems To extend the generality of Multi4D as a unified framework for complex interfacial analysis, we further applied it to material systems with fundamentally different chemistries, crystallographic motifs, and degrees of structural heterogeneity. Beyond the superconducting interface, we examined two representative cases: a corroded Mg-alloy interface and a degraded sulfur-based solid-electrolyte interface. Together, these systems encompass markedly different crystal structures and interfacial morphologies, providing a stringent test of the framework’s transferability across material classes. In both cases, Multi4D produced spatially coherent component classification maps and corresponding DISC distributions. This consistent performance across distinct material environments indicates that the framework is not limited to a particular chemical system or interfacial geometry. As shown in the Fig. 5, for the Mg-alloy case, we examined corrosion products formed on a stainless magnesium alloy (Mg–11 wt.% Y–1 wt.% Al) after 1.5 h of exposure to a 3.5 wt.% NaCl solution. The cross-sectional structure consists of the protective W layer deposited during focused ion beam preparation, a surface corrosion-product layer, an amorphous passivation layer, and the underlying substrate from top to bottom in Fig. 5a. Multi4D identifies the surface corrosion products as primarily MgO and Mg(OH) 2 with good crystallinity, as indicated by the red and cyan regions in the classification map. An enlarged view of region 1 is shown in Fig. 5b overlays representative experimental diffraction patterns with the Bragg reflections identified by the framework, confirming the accuracy of phase assignment. Similarly, in region 2 from Fig. 5c, Multi4D detected diffraction signatures of MgO alongside the primary magnesium lattice, indicating localized oxidation during sample preparation or environmental exposure. These results demonstrate that the framework distinguishes multiple adjacent corrosion products and maps their spatial distribution across the interface. We also applied Multi4D to observe moisture-induced degradation process of sulfur-based argyrodite solid electrolyte, which is a widely used solid-state battery material as shown in Fig. 6. The framework successfully resolves a complex degradation layer, identifying the spatial distributions of eight distinct decomposition products including hydrated lithium compounds, phosphorus sulfides, metal sulfides, and lithium chloride derivatives that previous not possible to observe. Notably, the amorphous fraction, which is typically overlooked in conventional crystallographic mapping, was reliably separated and mapped along grain boundaries and near the exposed surface. The spatial mapping reveals the correlation among diverse degradation products, potentially providing insight into the reaction sequence and the role of local structural heterogeneity in facilitating water penetration along the moisture-driven failure pathway. We want to point out that the spatial distribution of DISC reveals a consistent qualitative trend: bulk regions far from interfaces exhibit low structural complexity, whereas phase boundaries, degradation fronts, and transition zones display significantly elevated complexity. This observation is reasonable as single-phase bulk regions yield stable, highly defined diffraction signatures, while transition layers contain overlapping or locally perturbed Bragg reflections that broaden the classifier’s probability distribution. The recurrence of this spatial trend across diverse material systems indicates that DISC is not an artifact of a specific dataset, but a generalizable physical descriptor of local structural ambiguity. By jointly mapping dominant phases and quantifying structural complexity, the framework captures the spatial extent and internal disorder of interfacial transition zones where discrete classification labels are insufficient. In conclusion, these results demonstrate that Multi4D generalizes effectively across diverse material classes, establishing a unified framework for multi-component identification and spatially resolved structural complexity analysis. By combining physics-preserving style translation, rotation-invariant coordinate convolutions, and predictive entropy mapping, Multi4D extends 4D-STEM from qualitative visualization to robust, quantitative analysis of complex material interfaces. Discussion Multi4D demonstrates that large 4D-STEM datasets can be converted into spatially coherent multi-component maps without reducing the analysis to manual pattern matching or purely data-driven clustering. By linking experimental diffraction signals to a physically constrained structural library, it produces assignments with direct crystallographic meaning across interfaces containing several adjacent components. This is the main practical advance of the classification framework: it preserves a discrete, interpretable map while remaining scalable to datasets that would be difficult to examine pattern by pattern. The resulting label, however, represents the dominant candidate at each probe position. It should not be read as a complete decomposition of every structure contributing along the beam direction. This distinction changes how the classification map should be interpreted. A discrete label can identify the dominant component even when the diffraction signal is not uniquely described by that component alone. DISC supplies the missing context by indicating how strongly the selected class is preferred over the other physically plausible candidates. The recurring concentration of elevated DISC around interfacial transition regions suggests that these regions are better understood as spatially extended zones of assignment non-uniqueness rather than as perfectly sharp boundaries between discrete phases. Multi-component classification and DISC therefore serve complementary purposes: one organizes the most likely structural identities, while the other marks where a single-label representation becomes incomplete. There are important limits to this interpretation. DISC depends on the composition of the candidate library and on the calibration of the classifier probabilities. An elevated value cannot, on its own, tell whether the underlying cause is phase coexistence, local disorder, experimental noise, domain shift, or similarity among candidate structures. Conversely, a low value does not establish that an assignment is correct when the relevant structure is missing from the library or the model is overconfident. DISC should not be used to estimate phase fractions, and comparisons between experiments require comparable candidate spaces and probability calibration. These constraints are particularly relevant to the present single-label implementation. Although an amorphous-like category can be recognized from diffuse scattering, the framework does not yet resolve distinct forms of short-range order or separate strongly overlapping crystalline signals into their constituent contributions. Multi-label mixture models, representations tailored to diffuse scattering, and a dedicated out-of-library score would address different aspects of these limitations. The practical value of combining the two outputs is that classification provides a working structural map, while DISC indicates where that map deserves closer examination. Regions with non-unique assignments can then be prioritized for complementary spectroscopy, higher-resolution acquisition, additional simulations, or revision of the candidate library. Predictive uncertainty becomes part of the experimental decision process rather than a by-product of classification. With improved probability calibration and explicit detection of structures outside the candidate space, each map could inform the next measurement or model update. The longer-term contribution of Multi4D may therefore lie not only in accelerating multi-component mapping, but also in showing where the current structural interpretation requires additional evidence.
