2 Proposed approach We started from the prompt shown in Fig. 1 right: the two "experimental" images served as input to the models, but not the reference image. Beyond this baseline which is close to the ideal MLLM application setting, we evaluated several in-context learning approaches, providing the MLLMs with more and more guidance on the task, up to the full segmentation of the body part in question. In more detail, we considered the following setups: • "Baseline": the full prompt is shown in Fig. 3 . • "Reference": the reference image of Fig. 1 left is provided along with the prompt. • "Example": two ants with the segmented body part highlighted in green are provided as shown in Fig. 2 . The query is for another pair without segmentation. • "Object": with the same example image of two ants, we refer to the body part as "object" in the query to avoid the semantic bias of biological prior knowledge. • "Segmentation": in the query image the body part is segmented and highlighted in green. • "Segmentation+Object": in the query image, we refer the segmented region as "object". 3 Experiments and Observed Outcome 3.1 Data Generation We construct each sample by first placing the four main body parts, the head, thorax, waist, and abdomen, as the body axis. Attached structures including the eyes, legs, mandibles, antennae, and bristles are subsequently positioned relative to their corresponding main body components (Fig. 1 ). We then generate morphological variants from this template by independently scaling each component around its fixed center. Each body part is assigned a predefined set of categorical levels, and we enumerate the Cartesian product of all attribute levels before randomly shuffling the resulting configurations. To introduce within-category variation, each categorical scale factor is multiplied by a Gaussian perturbation, ϵ ∼ 𝒩 ⁡ ( 1 , 0.01 2 ) \epsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(1,0.01^{2}) . In total, we generated 20,000 images of ants and used them to randomly select 10,000 pairs for comparison, always maintaining a balance between "left", "same" and "right" answers. 3.2 Baseline prompt design Each prompt specifies the image setup, task goal, target body part location and shape, and comparison rules. We find a strong association between models’ ability to recognize a target structure and their ability to reliably compare its visual attributes. Furthermore, failure to recognize one structure can distort the grounding of another, impairing comparisons even for concepts that the model can otherwise recognize. This is surprising because, for a human observer, the semantic identity of the structures is largely irrelevant to the comparison: estimating the object size is a simple perceptual operation independent of knowing what the object is. For the models, in-context learning improves performance, with the largest gains obtained if a segmentation of the structures to be compared is provided. While encouraging, this setting is not useful in practice, since measuring the size of segmented objects is a simple operation that does not require AI. We hypothesize that the failure arises from a loss of fine-grained visual structure in the vision encoder during multimodal training: visually similar image patches may cease to be represented similarly unless their similarity is reinforced through association with a shared semantic concept. Analysis of vision-text associations in the MLLM and of patch encodings in different layers of the Qwen3.8 vision encoder provides evidence consistent with this hypothesis. For open-weight MLLMs, semantic recognition thus appears to act as a gating mechanism for downstream visual reasoning, even for tasks that do not intrinsically require semantic understanding. This limitation is particularly consequential for biological imaging, where the structures of interest may be altered by mutation, disease, perturbation, or new imaging conditions and therefore poorly represented by the model’s learned semantic associations. Still, while our results indicate that MLLMs cannot yet be reliably used for this type of visual reasoning, we see substantial grounds for optimism in the rapid improvement of model performance from 2025 to 2026, particularly in the progression from Qwen3.5 to Qwen3.8. The closed-source frontier model ChatGPT5.6-Terra leads the comparison for classes that open-weight models struggle to ground visually, whereas open-weight models outperform it on the more readily recognizable “Bristles” and “Legs” classes. We therefore hope that continued competition and progress at the frontier will pave the way to automated phenotype comparison and further visual reasoning in microscopy analysis pipelines.
