2.1 Histopathology Foundation Models Foundation models have shifted computational pathology from training task-specific feature extractors toward reusing large pretrained representation backbones across diverse downstream tasks. In this paradigm, tissue patches are first encoded into dense embedding vectors using a pretrained model and these embeddings are then reused for classification, retrieval, clustering, slide-level aggregation, and multimodal analysis. Existing histopathology foundation models differ primarily in their pretraining objectives. Vision-only models, such as UNI [ 4 ] and Virchow [ 31 ] , are typically trained on large pathology image collections using self-supervised objectives similar to DINO-style distillation [ 3 ] or masked image modeling [ 9 ] . These models learn general-purpose visual representations of tissue morphology. A complementary family consists of vision-language models, which jointly learn image and text representations from pathology image–caption supervision [ 25 ] . CONCH belongs to this family and produces semantically enriched embeddings aligned with textual pathology descriptions. Recent work suggests that vision-language pretraining may encourage representations that align more closely with semantic textual descriptions [ 28 , 12 ] . This motivates our use of CONCH as the primary model for sparse representation analysis, while UNI2-h provides a complementary vision-only embedding space for robustness evaluation. Despite strong empirical performance, the internal organization of these embedding spaces remains insufficiently understood. A dense embedding may contain information about tissue type, morphology, staining variation, artifacts, and diagnostic context, but these sources of variation are not exposed as directly interpretable components. Addressing this gap is especially important in pathology, where model behavior should ideally be related back to recognizable histological patterns and expert reasoning. Our work therefore treats foundation-model embeddings themselves as the object of analysis and asks whether their structure can be decomposed into sparse and inspectable latent features. 3 Method 3.1 Terminology and Concept Framing We distinguish between three levels of interpretation. A latent feature denotes one sparse dimension learned by the SAE. A candidate concept denotes a latent feature selected for interpretation because it is supported by quantitative, visual, or spatial evidence, such as label enrichment, coherent top-activating patches, or meaningful WSI-level activation patterns. A validated concept denotes a candidate concept that has been semantically assessed by pathologists. This distinction is important because sparse latent features are not guaranteed to correspond to human-understandable concepts. They may be dataset-specific, artifact-associated, polysemantic, or only partially aligned with expert-defined histological abstractions. 3.2 Dataset and Embeddings We train and evaluate sparse autoencoders on histopathology patch embeddings extracted from the NCT-CRC-HE-100K dataset [ 11 ] . The dataset contains hematoxylin–eosin (H&E) stained colorectal cancer tissue patches of size 224 × 224 224\times 224 pixels organized into nine tissue classes: adipose tissue ( ADI ), background ( BACK ), debris ( DEB ), lymphocytes ( LYM ), mucus ( MUC ), smooth muscle ( MUS ), normal colon mucosa ( NORM ), cancer-associated stroma ( STR ), and colorectal adenocarcinoma epithelium ( TUM ). We use the provided training split of 100,000 patches and validation split of 7,180 patches. For each patch, we extract embeddings using CONCH [ 20 ] and UNI2-h [ 4 ] . We use patch-level image embeddings as the object of analysis throughout this work. CONCH produces 512-dimensional embeddings, whereas UNI2-h produces 1536-dimensional embeddings. We refer to these vectors as raw embeddings to indicate that they are used in their extracted, non- ℓ 2 \ell_{2} -normalized form. This yields paired CONCH and UNI2-h embeddings for 100,000 training patches and 7,180 validation patches. Each embedding is paired with the corresponding NCT-CRC tissue label, which is used only for post-hoc analysis of latent-feature enrichment, coherence, and downstream class-signal preservation. 3.3 Sparse Autoencoder Training We train sparse autoencoders to reconstruct dense patch embeddings through a sparse intermediate representation. We therefore assess candidate concepts using complementary evidence: quantitative characterization, top-activating patches, and WSI-level activation maps (Figure 4 ). While top-activating patches reveal image structures associated with a latent feature, WSI-level activation maps reveal how the same feature deploys within larger tissue architecture. This additional context can help distinguish meaningful tissue patterns from artifact-associated or fragmented activation behavior. To support expert inspection, we developed an interactive interface linking latent features, top-activating patches, and WSI-level activation maps (Appendix F ). In our framework, latent features are treated as candidate concepts whose interpretation is informed by multiple forms of evidence rather than patch-level retrieval alone. 3.2 Dataset and Embeddings We train and evaluate sparse autoencoders on histopathology patch embeddings extracted from the NCT-CRC-HE-100K dataset [ 11 ] . Activation frequencies are highly sparse, with a median activation frequency of 4.67 × 10 − 5 4.67\times 10^{-5} across active features. Many latent features therefore activate only on a very small subset of image patches. At the same time, many active features exhibit strong tissue-label enrichment despite being learned without supervision. The median label entropy across active features is 0.256 nats, corresponding to normalized entropy 0.116. This indicates that activation mass is often concentrated within a small subset of tissue classes. Class-frequency normalization produced the same qualitative conclusion. Several latent features exhibit highly selective activation behavior for individual tissue categories. For example, latent 0058 assigns 98.9% of its activation mass to lymphocyte patches, latent 0150 assigns 98.1% to adipose tissue, latent 1883 assigns 98.1% to tumor epithelium, and latent 1966 activates exclusively on background patches. Representative active features often retrieve visually coherent histological or technical patterns, including lymphocyte-rich regions, adipose tissue, tumor-associated morphology, stromal structure, artifacts, and background (Figure 4 ). These observations suggest that at least part of the active latent features capture recurring tissue-associated structure present in the original CONCH embedding space. Using CONCH embeddings from the NCT-CRC dataset as the primary analysis, with an additional UNI2-h robustness evaluation, we showed that BatchTopK SAEs achieve high-fidelity sparse reconstruction while preserving most downstream tissue-class information. The learned latent features exhibit coherent visual behavior and strong tissue-label enrichment, and retrieve more coherent patch groups than raw embedding dimensions or PCA components. Together, these results suggest that sparse dictionary learning can make parts of histopathology foundation-model embedding spaces more inspectable. We emphasize that SAE latent features should be viewed as candidate concepts rather than automatically validated histopathological concepts. In our framework, quantitative characterization, top-patch inspection, and WSI-level visualization provide complementary evidence that supports expert interpretation. Important limitations include the use of a single main dataset, limited foundation-model coverage, the absence of systematic pathologist evaluation, and the correlational nature of the analysis. Future work should test whether these candidate concepts are stable across datasets and foundation models, evaluate them systematically with pathologists, and examine their causal relevance through latent interventions.
