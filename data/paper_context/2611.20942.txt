2 Related approaches and scope Related work situates inference scale, representation learning, reconstruction, and clustering as separate interventions. 2.1 Dense visual descriptors and spatial sampling Amir et al. demonstrate dense DINO-ViT descriptors for correspondence, co-segmentation, and part discovery [ 21 ] . Their inference procedure increases descriptor sampling density by reducing the patch-embedding stride, allowing overlapping patches while retaining image-wide transformer context. Their supplement also uses flips and 95% random-crop augmentations; cropping and augmentation are therefore not absent from this precedent. FFA instead repeatedly encodes much smaller, enlarged fields of view and reconstructs their descriptors in source coordinates. The distinction concerns sampling and supplied context, not the first use of dense ViT features or crops. Conceptually, FFA shares the hint of local observation with VICRegL. VICRegL learns global and spatially matched local representations during self-supervised training [ 22 ] . It addresses representation learning itself, whereas the present intervention changes the inputs and reconstruction of an already-trained encoder. Neither approach establishes that local training is universally necessary, or that inference-time cropping recovers biologically meaningful structures without validation. 2.2 High-resolution inference with scales, tiles, and transformed views Scaling on Scales ( S 2 S^{2} ) evaluates a frozen encoder at several image scales, divides enlarged inputs into regular-sized subimages, reassembles and pools their features, and concatenates representations across scales [ 23 ] . Its parameter-free extraction wrapper is distinct from its downstream-head training and separate pretraining experiments. This establishes multiscale tiled inference as prior work; FFA retains one focus at a time and evaluates reconstructed dense fields rather than claiming novelty for scale aggregation. Docherty et al. transform inputs to a frozen DINOv2 encoder, upsample feature grids, inverse-align the transformations, and average the resulting maps [ 24 ] . Their method uses shifts and flips, supports rotations, and is compatible with reduced patch-embedding stride. This is a direct precedent for the inverse-aligned reflection ensemble used here; the procedure appeared in their 2024 preprint before its 2026 journal publication. FFA additionally specifies restricted enlarged crop contexts, a regular overlapping grid, and Hann-weighted reconstruction for the present ecological study. I do not claim that transformation averaging itself is new. 2.3 Feature-map upsampling and hybrid reconstruction Feature-map upsampling estimates a denser field from coarse descriptors, while high-resolution inference obtains additional descriptors by re-encoding RGB at different scales or locations. FeatUp uses learned image-guided upsampling or per-image implicit fitting with multiview consistency [ 25 ] . FeatSharp combines upsampled global features with independently encoded local tiles through learned fusion [ 26 ] . It is consequently a close tiled-reconstruction precedent, not merely interpolation of a single feature map. LoftUp learns coordinate-based high-resolution feature prediction using RGB guidance and cross-attention to coarse features; its later training stage uses crop outputs from a learned teacher upsampler as targets [ 27 ] . AnyUp trains a universal, encoder-agnostic feature upsampler against crop-aligned descriptors from a frozen encoder [ 28 ] . Their learned reconstruction components distinguish these methods from the retained FFA procedure, which re-encodes enlarged crops and uses bilinear placement with normalized taper weights. None of these learned upsamplers, including AnyUp, is part of the retained pipeline. These methods have not been benchmarked on the five-domain collection, so the present results cannot establish superiority over these reconstruction methods. 2.4 Ecological crop inference and the scope of FFA The verified Foveate workshop record describes exemplar-guided instance segmentation with frozen DINOv3: exemplar similarity gates foreground patches, connected components propose tighter crops, and recursive re-embedding refines instances [ 29 ] . This supports ecological crop inference as relevant prior work. Its abstract describes adaptive, target-guided zooming, whereas FFA uses declared regular crop geometry for image-only cue reconstruction. Only the record and abstract were verified; they do not establish whether Foveate also uses reflections, overlap tapering, or other reconstruction details. The contribution of this study is the specified ecological workflow and its evidence: local crop enlargement and context restriction, inverse-aligned reflection averaging, overlap-weighted reconstruction, appearance-boundary evaluation, and analysis of clustering complexity and computational cost. The individual ingredients have precedents. DINOv3 features are clustered, while automatic SAM3 masks are resolved by a declared overlap rule; neither human text nor exemplar prompts are supplied to SAM3 [ 30 ] . This common RGB-edge endpoint does not make their outputs semantic equivalents or their computation equal. Methods specifies the controls, and Discussion distinguishes exploratory cues from biological segmentation. Grouping is a separate summary stage: silhouette and BIC assess a feature partition or density model, not taxonomic identity [ 31 , 32 ] . Varimax rotates a retained subspace for inspection [ 33 ] . The tested complexity penalty and display choices therefore require their own interpretation alongside feature reconstruction. 3 Materials and methods 3.1 Forced Focused Attention (FFA) algorithm workflow FFA combines local inspection with global aggregation. When a Vision Transformer (ViT) processes an entire image, its self-attention can relate local features to the broader scene [ 16 ] . FFA instead restricts each encoder input to a small crop, excluding the surrounding image from that inference pass. The term “forced focused” describes this externally imposed restriction of the model’s visual context. Enlarging the crop makes local edges, shapes, textures, and colour differences more prominent within the encoder input. Features extracted from overlapping crops are then blended back into a whole-image representation. The approach changes the input context without retraining the encoder or changing its learned parameters. 1. Local focus for feature extraction. First, choose a focus fraction f f relative to the working image’s short side L = min ⁡ ( W , H ) L=\min(W,H) , giving a square crop side c f = max ⁡ { c min , round ⁡ ( f ​ L ) } c_{f}=\max\{c_{\min},\operatorname{round}(fL)\} . For instance, f = 0.10 f=0.10 specifies 10% of the short-side length of the image, not 10% of the image area. Second, slide the crop along both axes with a step of approximately ( 1 − α ) ​ c f (1-\alpha)c_{f} , where α \alpha is the overlap fraction ( α = 0 \alpha=0 means no overlap), and add edge-aligned windows to cover the entire image. Third, resize each crop by bicubic interpolation to the chosen encoder input resolution, and apply the checkpoint’s RGB rescaling and channel standardization. Fourth, extract spatial patch descriptors with a pretrained encoder such as DINOv3. Following Docherty et al. [ 24 ] , token grids from the original, horizontally reflected, vertically reflected, and doubly reflected versions of each rescaled crop are inverse-aligned and averaged. For the DINOv3 comparison in Table 2 , the FFA settings were f = 0.05 f=0.05 or 0.10 0.10 , c min = 16 c_{\min}=16 pixels, nominal α = 0.75 \alpha=0.75 , and an encoder input resolution of 384 × 384 384\times 384 pixels; these are experimental choices rather than requirements of FFA. 2. Global aggregation from locally extracted features. Bilinearly interpolate each local feature map to its source crop’s pixel grid and place it back at the corresponding coordinates in the working image. Where crops overlap, weight their descriptor vectors with a floored separable Hann taper, sum the weighted vectors, and divide by the total coverage weight. A Hann taper (also called a Hann window or cosine bell) is a bell-shaped weighting function that emphasizes crop centres and downweights their edges, helping reduce stitching artefacts when overlapping feature maps are blended [ 34 ] . This normalized aggregation produces one image-aligned continuous feature field for each focus setting; it averages features, not display colours or cluster identifiers. 3.2 Datasets for FFA experimentation The datasets were selected to expose FFA to varied ecological settings, organism groups, and scales of visible structure. RGB-edge recovery was the primary quantitative endpoint in this study; visual displays and sensitivity analyses of automatic grouping provide separate checks. Further evaluation could address downstream tasks such as prediction, object detection, and counting. Here, the evaluation focuses on appearance-boundary recovery and computational cost. 3.3.1 Models and baseline comparisons The primary feature comparison uses a frozen DINOv3 ViT-B/16 encoder [ 14 ] . The DINOv3 original control processes the complete working image once at its native working resolution and in one orientation. The FFA5% and FFA10% conditions instead use DINOv3 to encode overlapping local crops and reconstruct their descriptors on the same working-image grid. The 5% and 10% values of f f were chosen subjectively; a wider range was tested in the crop-size analysis (Section 3.3.4 ). The backbone weights are identical across conditions. DINOv3 supplies 768-dimensional patch descriptors at a 16-pixel stride; local crops are rescaled to 384 × 384 384\times 384 pixels before encoding. “Original” identifies this control protocol, rather than the uncropped source photograph or the checkpoint’s default thumbnail size. In addition to DINOv3, an automatic SAM3 mask-generation pipeline was tested as a baseline [ 15 , 30 ] . In this study, the pipeline takes the image as input and internally generates a 32 × 32 32\times 32 point-prompt grid, without manually selected prompts. Overlaps are resolved using predicted mask IoU scores; uncovered pixels remain background. Its boundaries come from the resulting mask partition, whereas DINOv3 boundaries come from clustered encoder features. Model revisions, preprocessing, mask filters, and numerical settings are recorded in Supplementary Section S1.1 . 3.3.2 From feature maps to predicted boundaries PCA-based visualization and clustering were employed as downstream applications of the reconstructed feature field. Each DINOv3 patch descriptor contains 768 learned feature values. Principal component analysis (PCA) identifies orthogonal directions of decreasing variance [ 37 ] ; here, each descriptor is represented by 16 scores along the leading directions estimated from a calibration sample. Sixteen is a fixed analysis setting, rather than a dimension count selected by an explained-variance threshold. For each image in Table 2 , a common PCA mean and basis are estimated from balanced samples across conditions and reused for all treatment fields. Mean centering is applied without whitening, so component variances are not forced to be equal. Each treatment is independently partitioned using K-means [ 38 ] with K = 6 K=6 , fitted to the 16-score feature vectors at the same sampled image positions. The position indices locate the vectors; image coordinates are not added as clustering features. Each model estimates its own six cluster centers, and all source-grid locations are then assigned to their nearest center using distances in the 16-dimensional PCA space. A pixel becomes a predicted boundary when its label differs from its right or lower neighbor. This evaluates the encoder–partition combination without requiring arbitrary cluster IDs to correspond across methods. The choice of K = 6 K=6 was subjective, and K = 12 K=12 was also tested in a sensitivity analysis (Supplementary Section S1.3 ); the supplement also records sampling and fitting settings. For visualization, the first three ordinary PCA scores, or the first three axes of the full 16-dimensional varimax rotation [ 33 ] , supply red, green, and blue display channels. Stretching and clipping are applied only to these false-color displays; primary clustering uses all 16 scores before display transformations (Supplementary Section S1.5 ). For exploratory visual grouping, the number of clusters is also selected automatically rather than fixed at six. Automatic K-means compares K = 3 , … , 12 K=3,\ldots,12 using held-out silhouette scores, favoring counts closest to five among candidates within one standard error of the best score. Full-covariance Gaussian mixture models compare K = 1 , … , 12 K=1,\ldots,12 using a BIC-style fit–complexity criterion with a strengthened penalty; the displayed maps use a multiplier of four, chosen after inspecting these images. These choices summarize appearance variation and do not estimate a known number of biological groups. Supplementary Section S3.1 gives the selection procedures and their sensitivity to the penalty and retained dimension. 3.3.3 Reference boundaries and performance metrics The primary evaluation asks whether reconstructed boundaries follow strong visible changes in an image. Applying the same image-derived reference rule across the five domains permits this comparison without requiring reviewed object-boundary annotations in every dataset. Each unclustered RGB image is converted to grayscale brightness and lightly smoothed to reduce pixel-scale fluctuations; strong local brightness changes then define the reference edges, independently of the encoder, PCA, or clustering. These appearance edges can include texture, shadows, and imaging artifacts, while missing weak transitions or color boundaries with similar brightness. They are not verified biological object outlines. Predicted and reference edges are restricted to a valid interior obtained by eroding the entire working-image domain by two pixels. With a two-pixel Manhattan tolerance, precision measures the fraction of predicted boundary pixels near a reference edge, and recall measures the fraction of reference pixels reached by a predicted boundary. Their harmonic mean is RGB-edge boundary F1 (Eq. S7 ). This tolerant set comparison does not measure region IoU or biological segmentation accuracy. Boundary density is reported because additional boundaries can improve recall. For comparison with the FFA-based results, the corresponding RGB images were also partitioned directly using the same K-means procedure. This control tests how directly partitioning RGB appearance recovers the selected visible edges. Direct RGB clustering was expected to perform well on this appearance-based endpoint. Higher RGB-edge boundary F1 indicates better agreement with the RGB-derived edge reference. Performance was evaluated separately for each image and summarized by ecological domain. The model comparisons in Table 2 used paired scores from the same images; the paired-comparison procedures and limitations are given in Supplementary Section S2.1 . 3.3.4 Effect of local-focus size The focus fraction f f determines how crop scale and supplied context affect recovery of RGB appearance boundaries. Alongside the comparison at 5% and 10% focus, the range was tested at eight complete settings: 100%, 40%, 20%, 10%, 5%, 4%, 3%, and 2% of the working image’s short side. Each setting covers all 50 images, giving 400 image–focus observations in total ( 50 × 8 50\times 8 ). The control is native-resolution, single-orientation DINOv3 original. All crop-size settings use approximately 75% overlap and coverage-normalized reconstruction. RGB-edge boundary F1 was expected to increase as crop size decreased with lower f f . Crop geometry and measurement-stage definitions are given in Supplementary Sections S1.2 and S3.2 . 3.3.5 Computational cost and performance–energy tradeoff Energy consumption was expected to increase as crop size decreased with lower f f . To test this, the operational cost of the declared inference stages was quantified on one NVIDIA RTX 4000 SFF Ada GPU. DINOv3 feature extraction for the crop-size analysis included encoding and two PCA projection/reconstruction calculations. It excluded calibration, checkpoint I/O, power-bracketing waits, and clustering/scoring. Board power was sampled approximately every 200 ms. Energy was estimated by integrating board power over the measured stage without idle-baseline subtraction; it excluded CPU and host-memory energy, wall-plug losses, and carbon accounting. Efficiency was calculated as inverse joules per image, summarized as the mean of individual reciprocals. Detailed timing boundaries and the integration formula are in Supplementary Section S3.2 . Across the crop-size range, both RGB-edge boundary F1 and energy consumption were expected to increase as f f decreased. F1 is bounded by 1, whereas finer crops can require more energy. The analysis aimed to identify a suitable balance between gains in F1 and energy consumption. To test this, the operational cost of the declared inference stages was quantified on one NVIDIA RTX 4000 SFF Ada GPU. DINOv3 feature extraction for the crop-size analysis included encoding and two PCA projection/reconstruction calculations. It excluded calibration, checkpoint I/O, power-bracketing waits, and clustering/scoring. Board power was sampled approximately every 200 ms. Energy was estimated by integrating board power over the measured stage without idle-baseline subtraction; it excluded CPU and host-memory energy, wall-plug losses, and carbon accounting. Efficiency was calculated as inverse joules per image, summarized as the mean of individual reciprocals. Detailed timing boundaries and the integration formula are in Supplementary Section S3.2 . Across the crop-size range, both RGB-edge boundary F1 and energy consumption were expected to increase as f f decreased. F1 is bounded by 1, whereas finer crops can require more energy. The analysis aimed to identify a suitable balance between gains in F1 and energy consumption. For adjacent complete focus settings, paired changes in RGB-edge boundary F1 and energy were averaged within domains and then equally across domains. Falling gain per joule and rising cost per gain describe diminishing energy efficiency, which is distinct from a zero-gain performance plateau. 5 Discussion This study examined whether local focus could help vision foundation models represent the dense, fine-scale visual structures found in ecological photographs. To address this question, we developed forced focused attention (FFA), implemented in [ 20 ] in python, which combines representations extracted from enlarged, overlapping local crops without retraining the encoder. We evaluated the approach through visual inspection and quantitative boundary comparisons using 50 photographs across five ecological image domains. With DINOv3, FFA exposed finer appearance differences in feature displays and achieved higher mean RGB-edge boundary F1 than direct DINOv3 encoding and automatic SAM3 in every domain. Narrower focus further improved boundary preservation, although the gains diminished at finer settings while computation time and GPU energy consumption increased exponentially. These findings support local feature extraction and reconstruction as an approach to revealing fine visual cues in complex ecological imagery, with the choice of focus determining the balance between visible detail and computational cost. 5.1 Key findings and broader implications The principal finding is that changing how a frozen vision encoder views an image can substantially improve the representation of fine visual structures without retraining. FFA made individual leaves, fungal branches, and canopy boundaries more explicit in DINOv3 feature maps. A plausible explanation involves both spatial scale and contextual scope: enlarging local crops represents small structures with more encoder patches, while restricting the field of view changes the surrounding information available when their descriptors are computed. This interpretation connects with the human-vision studies introduced earlier: attention can be concentrated within a narrower spatial region [ 17 ] , global structure can take precedence over local elements in hierarchical displays [ 18 ] , and fine-scale information can refine an initial coarse interpretation of a scene [ 19 ] . These parallels support the broader value of local inspection, while the present study demonstrates its benefit for representing fine appearance structure in ecological imagery. FFA complements recent efforts to recover dense spatial detail from vision foundation models. FeatUp learns an image-guided upsampler or fits an implicit representation to each image [ 25 ] , while AnyUp learns an upsampler that transfers across encoders using crop-aligned descriptors as training targets [ 28 ] . FFA shares tiled inference with S 2 S^{2} [ 23 ] and local tile encoding with FeatSharp , which learns to fuse these features with a global representation [ 26 ] . FFA constructs its field directly from enlarged, overlapping local encodings through inverse-aligned reflection averaging and fixed spatial weights, without learning an auxiliary upsampler or fitting an implicit reconstruction network to each image. Its contribution lies in this controllable local-focus workflow and its evaluation of appearance-boundary preservation and computational cost across five ecological domains. Beyond direct cue inspection, the reconstructed descriptors could provide image-derived teacher targets for a student trained to predict locally resolved features from a full-image input. This would offer a route to self-supervised feature distillation, related to FeatSharp ’s enhanced teacher features and LoftUp ’s local-to-global teacher–student supervision [ 26 , 27 ] ; student training and its benefits remain to be evaluated. Downstream ecological predictions depend on whether feature maps retain the visual variation relevant to the target question. Inadequate representation of fine visual cues could have been a technical barrier to applying general-purpose foundation models in ecological and eco-evolutionary research. FFA could help address this limitation across levels of ecological organization. At the individual level, delineating structures could support measurement of morphological traits and color variation in the source photographs, comparisons among conspecific phenotypes, genotype–phenotype analyses with matched genetic data, or screening of plant condition through yellowing and senescence [ 39 , 40 ] . Hyphal density, branching, and network morphology provide related traits for studying fungal foraging strategies [ 36 ] . At the population level, instance delineation could support counts and density estimates over a known surveyed area [ 41 ] ; at the community level, visual clusters linked to verified taxonomic labels could inform species-composition mapping [ 42 ] . At the ecosystem level, habitat classification could delineate channel and riparian units, including candidate riffle–pool structure along river continua [ 43 ] , and provide habitat-map inputs for landscape connectivity analyses and corridor assessment [ 44 ] . While these prospective applications require separate validation against biological or habitat reference data, the approach may unlock the utility potential of existing foundation models for various research topics. The representation challenge illustrated by ecological photographs also arises in other domains where many locally distinct structures coexist within a single field of view. In medical imaging, histological sections contain diverse cell types, tissue substructures, and pathological features that can be distinguished using self-supervised representations [ 45 ] ; color fundus photographs combine small retinal lesions, including microaneurysms, hemorrhages, and exudates, with normal anatomical structures [ 46 ] . Beyond biology, metallographic micrographs contain particles, grain-boundary carbide networks, and matrix regions whose delineation enables quantitative characterization of material microstructure [ 47 ] . Crowded aerial images similarly place small vehicles and ships alongside larger infrastructure, with substantial variation in scale and orientation [ 48 ] . Across these settings, structures occupying little image area may still carry information relevant to the target question. These parallels motivate testing whether controlled local encoding can make such distinctions more accessible in the descriptors of a frozen encoder.
