RESULTS Autonomous microrobot navigation framework We developed a closed-loop magnetic microrobot navigation framework that combines real-time microscopy, deterministic geometric planning, reactive local obstacle avoidance, and magnetic actuation (Figure 1 ). The framework is organized around a global–local decomposition. Image processing converts each incoming frame into the microrobot state, obstacle geometry, and target location; the analytic geometry-based planner (AGP) then computes a collision-free route from the current state. When nearby obstacle motion creates a short-horizon conflict, a local escape controller temporarily overrides the nominal route. After the conflict is resolved, control returns to AGP and the route is recomputed from the updated image. This decomposition allows long-range progress and short-range reactivity to be handled by different modules while preserving a transparent geometric global plan. The same architecture is used across static, dynamic, and flowing environments. In the benchtop system, camera feedback closes the loop between perception and a three-axis Helmholtz coil system that actuates a rolling magnetic microrobot. The planner and local controller are updated repeatedly from the live image stream rather than executing a precomputed open-loop trajectory. Analytic geometry-based planner AGP converts the detected microrobot and obstacle geometries into a geometric path-planning problem by inflating each obstacle according to the microrobot radius, allowing the microrobot to be treated as a point moving among safety regions (Figure 2 (A)). Starting from the direct start–goal line, obstacles unlikely to constrain the route are first removed using their projected positions and distances from the ideal path (Figure 2 (B)). For the remaining obstacles, AGP constructs a sparse sequence of primary nodes using perpendicular projections and tangent lines around nearby safety regions (Figure 2 (A,C)). The construction is advanced sequentially toward the target, selecting geometrically valid tangent or projection-based nodes that remain outside the active safety regions. Once the sparse route is obtained, additional waypoints are inserted along the path for closed-loop tracking. Extended or noncircular structures are represented by multiple local safety regions rather than a single large enclosing region, preserving navigable free space near curved boundaries while allowing the same geometric construction to be applied (Figure 2 (D)). Benchmarking against established planners We next asked whether the low computational cost of AGP was obtained at the expense of trajectory quality. AGP was compared with weighted A*, RRT*, and circular-obstacle pathfinding (COP), representing raster search, sampling-based planning, and geometry-aware graph search, respectively (Figure 2 (E)). All four planners returned valid collision-free paths in all evaluated environments. AGP produced short paths with an average length of approximately 291 ​ μ ​ m 291\,\mu\mathrm{m} , comparable to RRT* and COP and shorter than weighted A* in the evaluated implementation. Weighted A* yielded lower cumulative turning, whereas AGP, RRT*, and COP showed comparable turning-angle distributions associated with continuous geometric detours around circular obstacles. The clearest separation was computational: AGP required approximately 1.25 ​ ms 1.25\,\mathrm{ms} per planning query, compared with tens of milliseconds for weighted A*, approximately one second for COP, and several seconds for RRT*. Thus, the goal is not global path optimality, but a practical combination of deterministic construction, competitive path quality, and millisecond-scale replanning compatible with closed-loop imaging. Reactive local control for moving obstacles Frame-by-frame replanning updates the global route as the environment changes, but a path that is collision-free in the current image can still become unsafe before the microrobot reaches the next waypoint if a nearby obstacle moves rapidly. We therefore augment AGP with two local controllers that operate only during short-range conflicts (Figure 3 ). The local module does not replace the global planner: its role is to resolve the immediate interaction, after which control returns to the current AGP route. The first controller uses a deterministic geometric escape rule and requires no training. Depending on whether the predicted one-step microrobot motion or the microrobot itself enters an obstacle safety region, a temporary escape target is constructed by reflecting either the predicted motion point or the obstacle center about the microrobot position (Figure 3 (A,B)). The temporary target is updated as the relative geometry changes and is released once the local conflict is cleared. The second controller learns short-range escape actions using deep Q-learning. The learned policy is deliberately restricted to local obstacle interactions and does not observe the global target or AGP path. It acts only when the microrobot enters a local safety region and selects among nine discrete motion actions based on the relative positions and velocities of nearby obstacles (Figure 3 (C)). This formulation restricts the learning problem to local collision avoidance rather than requiring the policy to learn end-to-end navigation from the current state to a distant target. Validation success increased above the random-policy baseline during training, with the strongest checkpoints approaching 90 % 90\% success (Figure 3 (D)). A representative learned avoidance sequence is shown in Figure 3 (E). The complete controller definitions, reward formulation, and DQN training procedure are provided in Methods and Supplementary Tables S1–S3. Global–local dynamic navigation We evaluated the complete global–local architecture in a 2000 × 2000 2000\times 2000 -pixel workspace containing both moving and static circular obstacles (Supplementary Table S1). The navigation evaluation used the same obstacle counts, obstacle-size range, and microrobot parameters. Moving obstacles traveled at 4 4 – 8 8 pixels per step along one of 16 uniformly spaced directions and reflected from workspace boundaries and other obstacles. AGP used α = 6 \alpha=6 for obstacle pruning in these dynamic scenes. The evaluated configurations were AGP alone, AGP combined with the rule-based controller, AGP combined with the RL-based controller, and each local controller without AGP. In the coupled configurations, AGP replanned from the latest obstacle state at every simulation step, while the selected local controller temporarily replaced the nominal command when its activation condition was met. The local-controller-only configurations were used to isolate the contribution of global planning. A benefit of using AGP online rather than as a one-time planner is that the remaining route can be updated continuously from the current microrobot position. In the representative static example in Supplementary Fig. S6 , successive replanning shortened the executed route relative to following the initial solution as a fixed path. Online replanning also provides a degree of adaptability to dynamic environments. In several representative scenarios involving randomly moving obstacles, coherently translating obstacles, moving targets, and vascular-like geometries, repeated global replanning alone was sufficient to reach the target (Supplementary Fig. S5 ; Supplementary Video 2). However, geometric replanning from the current obstacle configuration does not explicitly account for short-horizon obstacle motion. AGP alone could therefore still collide with a fast obstacle even when the route was recomputed at every step (Figure 4 (A)). This behavior exposes the distinction between snapshot-level geometric feasibility and short-horizon dynamic safety. When either local controller was added, the microrobot could resolve the immediate encounter and continue toward the fixed target. The target-distance histories in Figure 4 (B) show that both global–local configurations reached the goal in the representative trial. The learned controller generally produced fewer prolonged plateaus, whereas the rule-based controller occasionally moved temporarily away from the goal because its reflected escape point is determined only by local geometry. The global planner was also necessary for sustained progress. When the local controllers were run without AGP, they could escape individual conflicts but had no representation of the distant objective, leading to unnecessary detours, plateaus, or motion away from the target. Reintroducing AGP restored global guidance for both local policies (Figure 4 (C,D)). The same architecture was evaluated in the vascular-like simulation in Figure 4 (E), where vessel walls were represented as boundary obstacles and moving circles represented flowing cells or aggregates. The global planner guided the microrobot between upstream and downstream targets, while the local controller handled nearby moving obstacles without interrupting overall progress toward the targets. The added reactivity did not remove the real-time computational advantage of the framework. Across the fixed-target, moving-target, and vascular-like scenarios, per-frame computation remained below 50 ​ ms 50\,\mathrm{ms} (Figure 4 (F)), which is compatible with the image-update rates used in the benchtop experiments. Experimental autonomous navigation We next integrated the planner and local controllers with the magnetic actuation and microscopy platform. Static experiments first tested whether image-derived AGP routes could be executed by the physical microrobot. In silica-particle and CHO-cell environments, the measured trajectories closely followed the planned routes while maintaining separation from the detected obstacles (Figure 5 (A,B); Supplementary Video 1). AGP was further validated across increasing obstacle densities, experimental silica-particle and CHO-cell fields, vascular geometries, and a maze-like environment (Supplementary Fig. S4 ). AGP was recomputed from the live image stream, so the path evolved with the measured microrobot state rather than being executed as a fixed open-loop trajectory (Figure 5 (C)). Additional single-target and sequential multi-target experiments in a CHO-cell environment further demonstrated continuous online AGP replanning during closed-loop navigation (Supplementary Fig. S7 ; Supplementary Video 3). Navigation was also demonstrated inside a 200 ​ μ ​ m 200\,\mu\mathrm{m} -wide microfluidic channel without imposed flow (Figure 5 (D)). Dynamic experiments then activated the local escape module. Representative rule-based and RL-based avoidance events are shown in Figure 5 (E,F) and Supplementary Video 7. In both cases the microrobot was redirected around moving obstacles and subsequently resumed navigation toward the target. The corresponding target-distance and estimated-speed histories (Figure 5 (G)) show repeated short local perturbations superimposed on overall progress toward the target. These experiments verify that the global–local switch can operate with real imaging, actuation latency, tracking noise, and nonideal microrobot motion rather than only in simulation. To test the framework under a sustained environmental disturbance, we introduced controlled flow and suspended biological or particle obstacles. Under downstream conditions, a 10 ​ μ ​ m 10\,\mu\mathrm{m} microrobot navigated through diluted human red blood cells; under upstream conditions, silica particles were used as moving obstacles while the microrobot rolled against the imposed flow (Figure 5 (H) and Supplementary Video 9). The RL-based local controller was used for these demonstrations without retraining specifically for flow. The mean imposed flow speed was approximately 400 ​ μ ​ m ​ s − 1 400\,\mu\mathrm{m}\,\mathrm{s}^{-1} and the rotating magnetic field was operated at 40 ​ Hz 40\,\mathrm{Hz} . The measured mean microrobot speeds were approximately 164 ​ μ ​ m ​ s − 1 164\,\mu\mathrm{m}\,\mathrm{s}^{-1} downstream and 62 ​ μ ​ m ​ s − 1 62\,\mu\mathrm{m}\,\mathrm{s}^{-1} upstream. The velocity histories in Figure 5 (I) show the local avoidance intervals superimposed on the flow-biased motion. Finally, a long-duration experiment repeatedly alternated upstream and downstream traversal through a flowing red-blood-cell environment (Supplementary Video 10). The microrobot reached multiple sequential targets in both directions while preserving stable closed-loop operation over the full run (Figure 5 (J)). Across the rule-based, RL-based, downstream, upstream, and long-duration experiments, the measured processing and planning time remained within the video-rate operating budget (Figure 5 (K)). Together, these results demonstrate a progression from static path execution to dynamic avoidance and finally to sustained autonomous navigation under flow. Navigation in biologically relevant environments To evaluate magnetic microrobot actuation beyond synthetic microfluidic environments, we performed proof-of-feasibility experiments in post-mortem and ex vivo mouse tissues (Figure 6 ). These experiments assessed whether magnetic microrobots could remain observable and magnetically actuated in biologically relevant environments. The experimental workflow is summarized in Figure 6 (A). We first examined magnetic microrobot motion in ex vivo ocular and cerebral tissues. GFP-labeled magnetic microrobots were introduced into the vitreous humor of isolated mouse eyes and into the microvasculature of thin vascularized cortical tissue sections, respectively. Under rotating magnetic-field actuation, time-lapse fluorescence imaging revealed displacement of the microrobots within both tissue environments (Figure 6 (B,C)). These experiments demonstrate that magnetic actuation remains feasible in heterogeneous biological environments substantially more complex than the transparent microfluidic systems. We next investigated photoacoustic imaging (PAI) as a sensing modality for magnetic microrobots when direct optical observation is limited. Gold-coated 20 ​ μ ​ m 20\,\mu\mathrm{m} magnetic microrobots were introduced into the intraperitoneal cavity of post-mortem mice and imaged at 750 nm while being actuated by a rotating magnetic field (Figure 6 (D)). Magnetic actuation produced a pronounced oscillatory PA response (Figure 6 (E)). Over matched 29.5-s intervals, the mean relative PA signal increased from 0.20% without magnetic actuation to 1.00% during magnetic actuation, while its temporal standard deviation increased from 0.28% to 0.65%, corresponding to a 2.33-fold increase in temporal variability. Spatial analysis further localized the field-associated increase in PA dynamics within the abdominal imaging region (Figure 6 (F)), with the maximum response observed at a lateral position of 1.72 mm and a depth of 11.47 mm. Together, these experiments extend the platform beyond optically accessible microfluidic environments by demonstrating magnetic microrobot actuation in ocular and cerebral tissues and photoacoustic detection of magnetically induced dynamics in the intraperitoneal cavity. Although closed-loop AGP navigation was not performed in these biological settings, the results establish complementary actuation and sensing capabilities that could support future autonomous navigation in more realistic tissue environments. DISCUSSION This work establishes a real-time autonomous navigation framework in which deterministic geometric planning and reactive local control are coupled to microscopy and magnetic actuation. The central design choice is the separation of roles across spatial and temporal scales. AGP continuously supplies a global route using the latest measured geometry, while local controllers intervene only when nearby obstacle motion makes the snapshot-level route temporarily unsafe. This modularity avoids requiring a learned policy to solve the complete navigation problem and preserves an interpretable global plan throughout execution. The static benchmark clarifies the role of AGP within this architecture. Across the 50 paired environments, all four planners succeeded; AGP produced path lengths comparable to RRT* and COP while requiring substantially less planning time than the alternative implementations. The advantage arises from analytic obstacle pruning and local geometric construction rather than exhaustive raster search, stochastic tree expansion, or full geometry-graph construction. Dynamic environments introduce a second limitation of purely geometric replanning. Even a collision-free route computed from the current image can become unsafe before the next control update when obstacles move rapidly. The local escape layer addresses this short-horizon mismatch. The rule-based controller provides a transparent and immediately deployable response, whereas the learned controller uses local position and velocity information to select context-dependent actions, including lateral, reverse, or stationary responses. The simulations and experiments show that local control alone is insufficient for reliable long-range navigation, but coupling it with AGP provides both reactivity and progress to the target. The physical experiments extend this conclusion beyond idealized simulation. The framework operated with image-segmentation error, tracking noise, magnetic-actuation variability, finite control latency, channel boundaries, and imposed flow. The transition from static silica and CHO-cell scenes to dynamic obstacles, flowing red blood cells, and repeated upstream/downstream navigation is particularly important because it tests the closed-loop architecture under progressively less stationary conditions. The measured computation times remained compatible with video-rate operation, indicating that the computational structure of the framework is suitable for online microscopy-guided control rather than offline path generation. The post-mortem and ex vivo experiments further probe whether the actuation and sensing components can be extended beyond optically simple microfluidic environments. GFP-labeled magnetic microrobots remained observable and magnetically mobile in the vitreous humor and vascularized cortical tissue, while gold-coated microrobots in the intraperitoneal cavity produced stronger and more variable PA signals during magnetic actuation than under the no-field condition. The spatial PA analysis localized the largest field-associated increase in temporal variability within the abdominal imaging region. Although closed-loop AGP navigation was not performed in tissue, these experiments demonstrate that magnetic actuation can be retained in heterogeneous biological environments and that PAI can report magnetically induced microrobot dynamics when direct optical observation is limited. A significant gap nevertheless remains before in vivo autonomy can be achieved. The present planner depends on sufficiently reliable microrobot and obstacle localization; missed detections, occlusion, or segmentation latency can reduce the effective safety margin. Optical microscopy further constrains penetration depth and field of view, whereas the present PAI measurements provide sensing rather than full real-time state estimation for closed-loop planning. The biological experiments were also performed post-mortem or ex vivo and therefore do not reproduce physiological motion, perfusion, immune interactions, or other in vivo disturbances. These limitations define the next steps toward autonomous microrobot operation in tissue. Deep-tissue sensing, including photoacoustic localization, must be integrated directly with the perception–planning–control loop; dynamic safety should explicitly account for obstacle motion and uncertainty; and the planning problem should ultimately extend to fully three-dimensional anatomy. Within those constraints, the present results show that fast analytic planning, local learned or rule-based reactivity, real-time magnetic control, and complementary biological sensing modalities can be combined as building blocks toward autonomous microrobot navigation in increasingly realistic environments. Planner benchmarking AGP was benchmarked against weighted A*, RRT*, and circular-obstacle pathfinding (COP). Fifty independently seeded environments were generated, each containing 20 circular obstacles. For each seed, all planners received the same obstacle realization and fixed start–goal pair. Performance was quantified using geometric path length, cumulative absolute turning angle, and wall-clock planning time. DQN training and implementation The DQN was trained in a 2000 × 2000 2000\times 2000 -pixel workspace containing 50 dynamic and five static circular obstacles. Each episode began with the microrobot near the center of the workspace and an initial travel direction sampled uniformly from eight planar directions, emulating a local segment of motion before a safety-region encounter. Dynamic obstacles moved along one of eight discretized directions and reflected from workspace boundaries and other obstacles. The complete environment and motion parameters are summarized in Supplementary Table S1. The Q-network contained two fully connected hidden layers with 512 and 256 units and rectified linear unit activations, followed by nine output values corresponding to the discrete action set. Training used ε \varepsilon -greedy exploration, experience replay, and periodic target-network synchronization. The complete DQN training configuration is provided in Supplementary Table S3. Policy performance was evaluated periodically in an independent validation environment. A checkpoint was retained whenever validation success exceeded the previous best value, with mean return used to break ties. The selected policy was subsequently used in the dynamic-navigation simulations and benchtop experiments without retraining.
