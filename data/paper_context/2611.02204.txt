2 Results 2.1 Problem setting Unidirectional pultruded CFRP composites provide a demanding case study for 3D microstructure reconstruction, with our specimens having a fiber volume fraction of approximately 70% and a mean fiber diameter of about 7 μ ​ m \mu\mathrm{m} Mageira et al. (2026) . As shown in Fig. 1 a, SEM clearly resolves individual fiber boundaries, while the corresponding micro-CT observation retains coarse spatial organization with limited boundary detail. The volumetric CT measurements also retain specimen-specific information about larger-scale fiber architecture and continuity, complementing the fine cross-sectional structure provided by SEM. We formulate specimen-specific reconstruction as a LUPI problem. Each training example consists of a triplet ( x , x ∗ , y ) (x,x^{*},y) , where x x is the micro-CT observation, x ∗ x^{*} is the spatially corresponding SEM observation available only during training, and y y is the fine-resolution fiber-instance label map. Supervision is defined on registered 2D cross-sections, while the student uses adjacent micro-CT slices as a 2.5D input to incorporate volumetric context into the prediction. This design exploits the predominantly unidirectional fiber geometry, where the main reconstruction challenge lies in separating densely packed fibers in the transverse plane. The trained student is then applied section by section throughout the volume to reconstruct the 3D microstructure. 2.2 Overview of the PCRD framework and ACSD PCRD consists of a privileged SEM teacher and a deployable micro-CT student operating on spatially registered observations with an approximately 24-fold difference in pixel size (Fig. 1 b). Before feature extraction, the micro-CT input is interpolated onto the registered SEM grid. Both branches use frozen DINOv3 ViT-B/16 Siméoni et al. (2025) encoders to extract 2D patch-token representations from four backbone depths. The multiscale features are concatenated and projected through a 1 × 1 1\times 1 layer to form fused teacher and student representations. Convolutional upsampling decoders then predict fiber foreground probabilities and flow fields Stringer et al. (2021) that guide the separation of individual fiber instances. During training, ACSD transfers structural knowledge from the SEM teacher to the micro-CT student at the fused representation level. During inference, only the micro-CT student is retained. Architectural and optimization details are provided in Methods 4.2 . ACSD transfers knowledge through the spatial organization encoded within each modality-specific representation (Fig. 2 ). The fused teacher and student feature fields are first flattened into spatial tokens, and a shared set of spatially corresponding locations is selected as anchors. The limited spatial resolution of micro-CT can cause neighboring feature locations to encode similar structural information, making dense anchor supervision potentially redundant. ACSD therefore selects a subset of anchors to reduce redundant constraints and computational cost, while each anchor still establishes relationships with every spatial token in the feature field. Within each modality, cosine similarities between each anchor and all spatial tokens are converted into an anchor-conditioned distribution using masked softmax, which excludes the anchor’s self-similarity. Separate teacher and student temperatures control the concentration of their respective similarity distributions, allowing the relational target and student response to be adjusted independently. ACSD then matches the corresponding teacher and student distributions, transferring the spatial relationships encoded relative to each anchor without directly aligning the underlying SEM and micro-CT feature vectors. The complete formulation of the similarity distributions and distillation objective is provided in Methods 4.3 . Figure 2: Anchored cross-modal similarity distribution distillation. A 4 × 4 4\times 4 token grid is shown schematically for clarity. The fused teacher and student feature grids are flattened into token sequences, with spatially corresponding anchors outlined in purple and marked by purple dots. For each anchor, cosine similarities with the remaining tokens within the same feature space are converted by masked softmax into teacher and student similarity distributions. ACSD minimizes the KL divergence between corresponding distributions across the shared anchor set, transferring spatial organization from the privileged SEM teacher to the CT student. 2.3 Relational transfer improves fiber-instance reconstruction We evaluated the knowledge-transfer strategies on 3,200 spatially registered micro-CT–SEM pairs, with an average of 297 annotated fiber instances per sample. We assessed 2D cross-sectional reconstruction performance using mean average precision (mAP), F1 scores at IoU thresholds of 0.50 and 0.75, and Binary IoU. mAP summarizes the precision–recall performance of fiber-instance predictions across multiple matching thresholds. F1 scores capture the balance between correctly identified, missed and falsely detected fibers at fixed matching criteria, with F1@0.5 assessing instance identification under a more permissive overlap threshold and F1@0.75 requiring closer agreement with reference instances. Binary IoU measures agreement of the overall fiber phase, independent of whether individual fibers are correctly separated. Full evaluation details are provided in Methods 4.4 . To examine how different forms of privileged supervision affect fiber-instance reconstruction, we adapted five existing distillation strategies for comparison with ACSD and direct student training, which uses only ground-truth supervision. All methods share the same student backbone, decoder and training protocol, with feature-based distillation applied at the fused representation level. Prediction distillation aligns the teacher and student foreground and flow predictions, while direct feature alignment constrains corresponding teacher and student fused features to match. Frequency decomposition extracts and aligns low-frequency feature components, which are expected to capture coarse structural patterns shared across the resolution gap Zhang et al. (2024) ; Liu et al. (2026) . Shared-private decomposition factorizes the fused features into modality-shared and modality-specific components, transferring knowledge through alignment of the shared representations Bousmalis et al. (2016) ; van Amsterdam et al. (2023) . Cross-affinity distillation, adapted from a recent cross-modal distillation method for dense prediction Sun et al. (2026) , matches cross-modal teacher–student affinities with student intra-modal affinities to transfer spatial relationships, but still involves direct similarity computation between heterogeneous feature vectors. Implementation details of the adapted baselines are provided in the Supplementary Information. ACSD improved fiber-instance reconstruction from micro-CT inputs across all four evaluation metrics (Table 1 ). Relative to direct training, ACSD increased mAP by 1.66 percentage points and F1@0.75 by 2.65 points, while F1@0.5 improved by 0.61 points. Binary IoU increased by 0.26 percentage points, while the larger gains in instance-level metrics suggest that ACSD improves individual fiber reconstruction beyond overall fiber-phase segmentation. The example in Fig. 1 a further illustrates this improvement, with ACSD achieving higher per-instance overlap than direct student training. In ACSD, selected spatial locations serve as anchors for measuring their similarity to the entire feature field. The ACSD w/o distribution matching variant retains anchor-based supervision but replaces the full similarity distribution with hard relations, where the teacher selects highly similar and dissimilar patch locations for each anchor and supervises the student through a contrastive objective. As shown in Fig. 3 a,b, removing distribution matching reduced mAP by 1.03 percentage points and F1@0.75 by 1.65 points. These results support the importance of preserving graded similarity information, as reducing teacher relations to positive and negative sets discards information about the relative organization of spatial locations. We also tested whether output-level teacher supervision provided additional information beyond the relational objective. Adding prediction distillation to ACSD reduced mAP by 0.20 percentage points and F1@0.75 by 0.10 points (Fig. 3 a,b). These results suggest that prediction-level supervision provides little additional benefit when combined with ACSD in this setting. We next examined the effect of anchor selection on relational transfer (Fig. 3 a–c). Removing anchor selection, which uses all 5,184 spatial locations as anchors, reduced mAP by 0.34 percentage points and F1@0.75 by 0.40 points relative to ACSD. To investigate how anchor density affects reconstruction, we also evaluated smaller anchor sets. Increasing the number of anchors from 1,296 to 2,592 improved mean mAP by 0.19 percentage points, while increasing it to 3,888 reduced mAP by 0.08 points relative to the 2,592-anchor setting. Together with the decline observed when all 5,184 locations were used as anchors, these results show that using more anchors does not necessarily improve reconstruction accuracy. Finally, we examined the role of separate teacher and student temperatures in relational transfer (Fig. 3 d). The teacher temperature controls the concentration of the teacher similarity distribution. A lower temperature produces a sharper distribution, assigning greater probability to spatial locations that are more similar to each anchor. The student temperature independently controls the smoothness of the student distribution, allowing greater flexibility in matching teacher relations across the modality gap. To examine their effects, we varied τ T \tau_{T} over { 0.005 , 0.01 , 0.02 , 0.05 } \{0.005,0.01,0.02,0.05\} and τ S \tau_{S} over { 0.05 , 0.1 , 0.2 } \{0.05,0.1,0.2\} . Relatively low teacher temperatures combined with a moderately higher student temperature generally yielded better reconstruction performance. Increasing τ T \tau_{T} to 0.05 reduced mAP in most settings, while increasing τ S \tau_{S} to 0.2 consistently reduced performance across all teacher temperatures. These results support the use of separate temperatures to maintain a concentrated teacher relational target while allowing the student to accommodate cross-modal representation differences without excessive smoothing. 2.5 3D microstructure reconstruction and validation To assess whether the reconstructed cross-sections reproduce the underlying 3D architecture, we performed an independent volumetric validation using high-resolution synchrotron X-ray CT data as the reference (HR reference). In the CFRP system studied here, SEM resolves fine-scale fiber–matrix interfaces that are poorly resolved in micro-CT images, while micro-CT retains information about the 3D arrangement of the specimen. PCRD uses these fine-resolution 2D observations only during training, with inference relying solely on micro-CT. Across the tested transfer strategies, ACSD produced the largest improvement in fiber-instance reconstruction. These findings support the use of within-modality spatial relationships for knowledge transfer between imaging modalities with different image formation processes. The comparison with other distillation strategies helps explain this result. Teacher predictions provide task-specific supervision, but contain less information than the intermediate SEM representation from which they are generated. Direct feature alignment assumes that corresponding teacher and student features are comparable, an assumption that may not hold across different imaging modalities. Frequency and shared–private decomposition seek to address these differences by imposing additional structure on the representations before alignment, but their improvements remained below those achieved by ACSD. Cross-affinity distillation also transfers spatial relationships, but its use of cross-modal feature similarities may retain sensitivity to differences between the teacher and student representation spaces. ACSD instead constructs similarity distributions independently within each modality and matches the resulting spatial relationships. This allows the teacher and student to retain different feature representations without specifying which feature components should be shared across modalities. The relational analyses provide further insight into the design of ACSD. Replacing the similarity distribution with hard positive and negative relations reduced reconstruction accuracy, indicating that graded similarity information contributes to relational transfer. Using more anchors did not consistently improve reconstruction accuracy. A moderate anchor density may balance coverage of spatial structures against redundant relational constraints, particularly when neighboring micro-CT features encode similar information due to limited spatial resolution. The temperature analysis also showed that the teacher and student distributions benefit from different levels of concentration. A sharper teacher distribution emphasizes the most similar spatial locations, while a moderately smoother student distribution may help accommodate differences between the two representation spaces. The independent synchrotron X-ray CT validation shows that the benefits of PCRD extend beyond individual cross-sections. The reconstructed volume closely reproduced the fiber volume fraction, equivalent fiber diameter and local spacing of the HR reference. PCRD also recovered a greater proportion of the reference centerline length than the interpolated LR, although its position and orientation deviations were slightly higher on the matched segments. The low-resolution volumetric input provides specimen-specific spatial information, while privileged supervision helps recover individual fiber structures that are difficult to resolve from that input alone. The ability to reconstruct fine-scale fiber geometry from lower-resolution volumetric data could support 3D image-based modelling over larger specimen regions, with potential applications in manufacturing process optimization and material property prediction. The formulation of ACSD is not specific to CFRP or the SEM-CT imaging pair and may be applicable to other imaging systems with spatially corresponding observations. More generally, ACSD requires spatial correspondence between the two modalities and informative relationships among locations within each modality. Two limitations remain in the present study. First, the reconstruction strategy improves spatial resolution primarily in the transverse plane, while resolution along the fiber direction remains constrained by the micro-CT sampling. Its ability to recover isotropically high-resolution 3D microstructures has therefore not been established. A possible extension is to reconstruct along multiple directions and combine the resulting predictions, with direction-specific training where required by material anisotropy. Second, ACSD constructs spatial relationships within individual observations. The densely packed fiber system studied here provides many similar local structures, whereas sparse or highly heterogeneous microstructures may offer fewer informative relationships for within-sample distillation. The effectiveness of ACSD in these settings remains to be evaluated. 4 Methods 4.1 Data preparation and cross-modal registration Two unidirectional pultruded CFRP specimens with a vinyl ester matrix were prepared for paired SEM and micro-CT imaging. Each specimen measured approximately 5 × 5 × 10 5\times 5\times 10 mm, with one end face polished for cross-sectional imaging. Volumetric micro-CT data were acquired using a Thermo Scientific HeliScan microCT system at an isotropic voxel size of 2.630 ​ μ ​ m 2.630~\mu\mathrm{m} . Cross-sectional SEM images were acquired using a Thermo Scientific Phenom XL G2 Desktop SEM with a backscattered electron detector at a pixel size of 0.098 ​ μ ​ m 0.098~\mu\mathrm{m} . To establish spatial correspondence between the two modalities, a representative micro-CT end-face image was constructed from the reconstructed volume to account for variations in the polished surface depth. Registration was performed in two stages: an initial affine alignment of the full SEM and micro-CT end-face images, followed by local rigid registration of corresponding image regions. The registered SEM images were resampled to approximately 0.110 ​ μ ​ m ​ px − 1 0.110~\mu\mathrm{m\,px^{-1}} and cropped into 1152 × 1152 1152\times 1152 -pixel patches, each corresponding to a 48 × 48 48\times 48 -pixel micro-CT region on its native sampling grid. All image samples were manually inspected and corrected at the instance level. Each retained fiber cross-section was assigned an independent instance label. The same one-sided input arrangement was used for interior sections during volumetric inference to maintain consistency with training. During student optimization, all teacher parameters remain fixed. 4.4 Experimental setup and evaluation The 3,200 registered SEM-micro-CT pairs were randomly split into training, validation and test sets in an 8:1:1 ratio, stratified by specimen to maintain equal representation of the two specimens in each subset. The split was fixed across all experiments so that all knowledge-transfer strategies were evaluated on the same samples. We compared ACSD with direct student training and five distillation strategies: prediction distillation, direct feature alignment, frequency decomposition, shared-private decomposition and cross-affinity distillation. All methods used the same student backbone, decoder, reconstruction targets and optimization protocol, with method-specific distillation components and objectives described in the Supplementary Information. The SEM teacher was trained first and then fixed during student optimization. Teacher predictions and fused representations required by the different distillation strategies were precomputed and cached for the corresponding training samples. Teacher and student models were optimized with AdamW for 16 epochs using a base learning rate of 7 × 10 − 4 7\times 10^{-4} and a weight decay of 10 − 4 10^{-4} . The learning rate was linearly warmed up for the first three epochs and then decayed using a cosine schedule to 0.1 0.1 times the base learning rate. Training used bfloat16 mixed precision. The foreground and flow loss weights were set to λ fg = 1 \lambda_{\mathrm{fg}}=1 and λ flow = 0.4 \lambda_{\mathrm{flow}}=0.4 , respectively, and ACSD used λ ACSD = 1 \lambda_{\mathrm{ACSD}}=1 . Unless otherwise stated, ACSD used 2,592 uniformly spaced anchors over the flattened token sequence, with teacher and student temperatures of τ T = 0.01 \tau_{T}=0.01 and τ S = 0.1 \tau_{S}=0.1 , respectively. All methods were evaluated using a common set of predefined random seeds, with data-loader initialization controlled for reproducibility. Main comparisons were repeated across five independent training runs, while relational ablations and sensitivity analyses used three runs. Instance masks were recovered from the predicted foreground logits and flow fields using flow-based grouping. Pixels above a foreground-logit threshold of 0 were propagated along the predicted flow field for 200 iterations and grouped according to their converged locations. Reconstructed instances smaller than 1,000 pixels were removed. Ground-truth instances intersecting the image boundary were excluded from instance-level evaluation because only part of the corresponding fiber was observed within the cropped image tile. Predicted instances whose area overlapped an excluded boundary instance by at least 50% were also removed so that correct reconstruction of a cropped fiber was not counted as a false positive. Reconstruction accuracy was evaluated over IoU thresholds from 0.50 to 0.95 in increments of 0.05. At each threshold, predicted and ground-truth instances were matched one-to-one according to IoU. We report F1 scores at IoU thresholds of 0.50 and 0.75, together with COCO-style mean average precision (mAP) computed across the full threshold range using the mean predicted foreground probability within each reconstructed instance as its confidence score. Binary IoU was computed independently of instance identity to quantify agreement of the total reconstructed fiber phase. Hyperparameters, including the number of anchors and the teacher and student temperatures, were selected based on validation-set performance. For each training run, the checkpoint with the highest validation mAP was used for evaluation on the held-out test set. The test set was not used for hyperparameter or checkpoint selection. 4.5 Volumetric inference and structural analysis The trained student was applied section by section to the LR input using the same 2.5D input construction as during training. Each target section was reconstructed from overlapping tiles, with predicted flow fields and foreground logits blended in the overlap regions using cosine-weighted averaging. The stitched predictions were converted into fiber-instance masks and assembled into a 3D volume. The resulting 5952 × 5952 × 248 5952\times 5952\times 248 binary mask stack was downsampled in the transverse plane using pooling and thresholding, followed by resampling along the fiber direction to obtain a 1488 3 1488^{3} isotropic binary volume with a voxel size of approximately 0.438 ​ μ ​ m 0.438~\mu\mathrm{m} for fiber tracking. Fiber volume fraction was calculated as the proportion of the selected region of interest (ROI) occupied by fibers. For the HR reference and interpolated LR, the fiber phase was identified using entropy-based thresholding Kapur et al. (1985) , applied at their respective analysis resolutions. For the PCRD reconstruction, fiber volume fraction was calculated directly from the predicted binary masks. To obtain individual fiber measurements from the HR reference, fiber instances were segmented using Cellpose-SAM and filtered according to the expected fiber morphology. Equivalent fiber diameter was calculated from the cross-sectional area of each instance, and nearest-neighbor distance was defined as the Euclidean distance between each fiber centroid and its closest neighboring fiber centroid. The same measurements were obtained directly from the predicted instances in the PCRD reconstruction. Fiber centerlines were extracted from the three volumes using cylindrical template matching followed by bidirectional tracing guided by local correlation and orientation consistency. The extracted centerlines were evaluated against those of the HR reference. Centerlines were interpolated onto common axial planes and matched using mutual nearest neighbors within 3 ​ μ ​ m 3~\mu\mathrm{m} . Reference centerline coverage was defined as the proportion of the total reference centerline length for which both endpoints of a sampled segment were successfully matched. Position deviation was calculated from the transverse distance between matched centerline locations, while orientation deviation was calculated from the angle between their local tangent directions. Position and orientation deviations were evaluated over the matched portions of each reconstruction independently and reported as reference-length-weighted median [IQR]. Further details of structural characterization, template matching and centerline evaluation are provided in the Supplementary Information.
