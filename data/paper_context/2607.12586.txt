1 Medical Image Segmentation based on Deep Active Contour and Mean Curvature Loss Function Xiao-qiang Zhai1*, Zhi-feng Pang1,2, Peng Zheng2, Ze-wen Li3, Yan-zhe Hou4 1 Henan Provincial Center for Applied Mathematics, Henan University, Kaifeng 475004, China 2 College of Mathematics and Statistics, Henan University, Kaifeng 475004, China 3 Advanced Institute of Finance, Henan University, Kaifeng 475004, China 4 School of Software, Henan Agricultural University, Zhengzhou 450000, China * Correspondence: zhaixiaoqiang19@mails.ucas.ac.cn Abstract Medical image segmentation is an important task in clinical analysis and applications. Although deep learning techniques are widely used in several scenarios, the training at the individual pixel level leads to a lack of geometric prior information. Several studies have proposed integrating the Chan-Vese model into the loss function for training, which can take into account the region and length of the contour inside and outside the segmentation target, thereby improving segmentation performance. However, these methods still lack an effective characterization of the segmented region. To overcome this problem, we introduce the mean curvature as a natural geometric constraint and propose a Deep Active Contour and Mean Curvature (DACMC) loss function, where the convolution kernel is used to approximate the mean curvature to reduce computational cost. We validated the proposed method on liver CT and spleen MRI datasets using U-Net and CE-Net. The experimental results show that the DACMC loss function achieves improvements over the baseline losses across all four evaluation metrics. Introduction Medical image segmentation is a computer vision task whose aim is to partition an image into meaningful regions for further clinical analysis, diagnosis, treatment planning and measurement of disease progression [1]. High-precision segmentation results can help doctors and other medical professionals to better understand or track the changes of the region of interest (ROI). Along with the rapid development of deep learning, many data-driven techniques such as the U-Net [2], the SegNet [3], the VGGNet [4], and the GoogLeNet [5] have been developed for various medical imaging modalities. However, these techniques usually require accurate delineation of structures and regions of interest in complex and noisy images. These delineations require experienced expertise and are time-consuming and costly, which then limits the scalability and applicability of segmentation methods. In addition, data-driven techniques are rather difficult to recognize the object boundary precisely. Furthermore, the ethical and legal constraints associated with the medical data to preserve the privacy and security of sensitive patient information make the data collection and annotation task more challenging. Despite the recent progress of using the deep learning method for medical image segmentation, the practical application still faces challenges due to its poor interpretability and the requirement of large labelled datasets [6], [7]. To improve the interpretability of deep learning strategies, coupling 3 the variational energy methods as network modules, some classical Deep Variation Segmentation Models (DVS) have been incorporated into segmentation networks for end-to-end learning. This mainly uses the fact that the variational energy methods have better mathematical interpretability and also can realize the continuous, closed and smooth representation of target boundary. The DVS can be divided into two categories. One approach is to apply spatial regularization to activation functions by representing activation functions as the solutions of some variational problems [8]. This modified activation function can improve the back-propagation process and the learnable parameters. In addition, this method is also flexible to be applied in any number of activation functions in any layers [9]. In practice, deep learning methods rely on loss functions to measure the discrepancy between the ground truth and the prediction, and the network parameters are optimized by numerical methods to minimize this loss. Based on these facts, another approach is to formulate new loss functions to improve upon the classical loss functions such as the CrossEntropy Loss (CE) [10] and the Dice-Coefficient Loss (DC) [11]. These classical loss functions have two main limitations: they are pixel-wise loss functions that measure the similarity between the ground truth image and the prediction image, but the prior information is not taken into account. In addition, these classical loss functions in image segmentation tasks only consider the information from each individual pixel instead of the relationship between them and often lead to unexpected results in some complicated scenarios. However, the medical images usually contain much contextual information rather than point samples [12]. To obtain more accurate and plausible results [13], some recent approaches have introduced complex geometrical structures such as shape constraints [14], the topology specifications [15], the edge polarity [16], or the adjacency rules among regions [17] to develop more sophisticated loss functions. However, integrating the shape prior still remains one of the most commonly used strategies toward anatomically meaningful predictions. In particular, the exploration of synergies between the FCNs and the Active Contour Models (ACMs) [18] has recently attracted research 4 interest [19]. These synergies utilize the fact that ACMs mainly use an enclosed and smooth curve to signify the target boundary, which is usually accomplished by minimizing the associated energy function through the standard numerical optimization methods or the dynamic programming [12], [20]. By embedding ACMs into deep neural networks, several methods have been proposed to combine the advantages of both data-driven and model-driven approaches [19], [21], [22], [23], [24]. However, these methods still lack an effective characterization of the local geometric properties of the segmented region, such as the mean curvature. To address this limitation, we propose to incorporate the mean curvature as a geometric constraint into the loss function. • We integrate the active contour model with the mean curvature into a loss function, where the mean curvature as a geometric prior guarantees the connectivity and smoothness of the network prediction result. • To reduce the computational cost, we use the mean curvature filter to approximate the mean curvature of the proposed loss function. • The experimental results on two public datasets show that our proposed method outperforms several related deep active contour-based methods. Section III provides the mathematical background on mean curvature and its applications in image processing. Section IV presents the proposed DACMC loss function in detail. Section V describes the experimental setup, datasets, and implementation details. Dataset To evaluate the effectiveness of the designed loss function, experiments were conducted on a two- dimensional liver CT dataset, which is available for download at this website: https://www.kaggle.com/datasets/zxcv2022/digital-medical-images-for--download- resource. This liver dataset comprises 100 images in total, of which 15 are reserved as the validation set and 16 as the test set. In addition, the spleen MRI dataset from CHAOS [36] is also used, and we preprocessed the images by resizing all images into 512 × 384 pixels for the experiment. For this dataset, we selected 120 images as the training set and divided 20 images as the validation set. In addition, 25 images were selected as the test set to assess the performance. B. Network architecture In this study, we utilized the U-Net [2] architecture as a framework for image segmentation. U-Net was initially developed for medical image segmentation but has since been widely used for various other tasks, such as semantic segmentation, denoising, and image generation. The U-Net model is an end-to-end encoder-decoder neural network that incorporates skip connections between upsampling and downsampling layers. This design enables the network to preserve features effectively while producing segmentation outputs efficiently from high-resolution features. Its simplicity and effectiveness make it an ideal choice for tackling complex problems, such as medical image segmentation, where obtaining labeled samples can be challenging. In addition to U-Net, we employed the CE-Net [37] architecture, which features a context extractor that generates more advanced semantic feature maps. This architecture includes a dense cavity convolution (DAC) 14 block and a residual multicore pooling (RMP) module that work together to enhance the network's ability to segment medical images accurately. The encoder-decoder structure of CE-Net, coupled with the advanced semantic feature maps generated by the context extractor, yields strong performance in medical image segmentation. C. Training process All training was done in Python and PyTorch with CUDA, using an Intel Xeon 5218R CPU and an Nvidia RTX5000 GPU. The batch size is set to 4 and the number of epochs is 300, and the models were trained by using the Adam optimizer. To compare these loss functions in a reasonable way, we used a learning rate of 0.0001 for all loss functions. No post-processing techniques were applied to ensure the fairness of the comparison experiments. D. Metrics To evaluate the performance of the segmentation model, we utilized four commonly used segmentation metrics: the Dice Similarity Coefficient (DSC), the 95th percentile of the Hausdorff distance (HD95), the Jaccard Similarity (JS), and the Average Surface Distance (ASD). DSC measures the overlap between the predicted segmentation and the ground truth, ranging from 0 (no overlap) to 1 (perfect match). HD95 is defined as the 95th percentile of the Hausdorff distance, i.e., the distance threshold exceeded by only 5% of the boundary point-to-point distances, making it more robust than the full Hausdorff distance. JS quantifies the intersection-over-union ratio between the prediction and ground truth. ASD computes the mean distance between the boundaries of the predicted region and the ground truth, reflecting the overall boundary alignment accuracy. VI. Experiments A. Ablation Study The ablation study examines the progressive contribution of each loss component by comparing CE, DC, AC, ACE, and the proposed DACMC across both backbone architectures. On the Liver CT dataset with U-Net (Table 1), the cross-entropy baseline achieves a DSC of 0.9257. Replacing it with the Dice loss (DC) improves DSC to 0.9301 but increases HD95, indicating that pixel-wise overlap optimization alone does not guarantee boundary accuracy. Introducing the active contour loss (AC) yields a marginal DSC improvement (0.9306) without substantially reducing HD95. Incorporating the elastic energy term (ACE) produces a notable increase to 0.9370 DSC and reduces HD95 to 4.784 mm, indicating that geometric regularization benefits boundary quality. The complete DACMC formulation, which integrates the CV-based region term with the mean curvature regularization, achieves the best performance across all four metrics: the highest DSC (0.9426), the lowest HD95 (1.305 mm, a 72.8% reduction relative to ACE), the highest JS (0.8956), and the lowest ASD (0.135 mm). On the Spleen MRI dataset with U-Net, DACMC likewise attains the highest DSC (0.8443) and the lowest HD95 (7.339 mm), indicating that the curvature term provides consistent boundary refinement across different imaging modalities. To further validate the generalization of the proposed loss function, the same set of experiments is conducted on CE-Net (Table 2). On Liver CT with CE-Net, DACMC again achieves the highest DSC (0.9446), the lowest HD95 (1.139 mm), and the highest JS (0.895), indicating that the improvement is independent of the network architecture. A notable observation is on the Spleen MRI dataset with CE-Net: the ACE loss performs substantially worse than the CE baseline, with DSC dropping from 0.8125 to 0.7367 and HD95 increasing from 6.352 to 13.639 mm. This degradation suggests that the elastic energy term can become unstable when the segmentation target exhibits significant scale variation. In contrast, DACMC maintains stable performance on this challenging dataset, achieving the highest DSC (0.8481), the lowest HD95 (3.966 mm), and the 19 highest JS (0.7481). These results demonstrate that the explicit mean curvature regularization in DACMC prevents the boundary degradation observed with ACE, providing consistent improvements across both network architectures and imaging modalities. VII. Conclusion This paper proposed a Deep Active Contour and Mean Curvature (DACMC) loss function for medical image segmentation that addresses the lack of geometric prior information inherent to pixel-level supervised learning. The mean curvature is incorporated into the active contour framework derived from the Mumford-Shah energy functional, providing a second-order geometric regularizer that characterizes the segmented region beyond the conventional region and length terms. To circumvent the high-order derivatives and non-smoothness of the variational mean curvature, a convolution-kernel approximation is employed, which preserves differentiability for backpropagation while reducing the computational cost on GPU. The resulting loss couples the region-based discriminability inherited from the Chan-Vese/Mumford-Shah model with boundary regularity through curvature, and requires only one weighting parameter λ to be tuned. Extensive experiments were conducted on two public medical imaging datasets (liver CT and spleen MRI) using U-Net and CE-Net as backbone architectures, with four segmentation metrics (DSC, HD95, JS, and ASD) reported. Compared with the CE, DC, AC, and ACE baselines, DACMC consistently achieved the highest DSC on both datasets (0.9426 on liver CT and 0.8443 on spleen MRI), the latter being the only method exceeding 0.84 on this challenging dataset. Ablation results further demonstrated that each component (the mean curvature loss, the deep active contour regularization, and their synergy) contributes to segmentation accuracy and stability, with the complete DACMC formulation yielding the best balance between accuracy and metric stability. 20 Several limitations remain. First, the convolution-kernel approximation yields a local, single-scale estimate of the mean curvature, which may not fully capture curvature variations across multiple scales or preserve directional curvature information on structures with sharp corners or thin branches. Second, the proposed loss has been validated only on 2-D single-organ datasets; extension to 3-D volumetric and multi-organ segmentation is left for future work. Third, the single weighting parameter λ is currently selected empirically. Future directions include integrating DACMC with transformer-based segmentation architectures, developing learnable or adaptive curvature operators, and extending the framework to multi-modal volumetric medical images.
