We benchmarked our tool to assess how the update time grows with the box count N N and whether it stays within the 33 ms budget ( ≥ 30 \geq 30 Hz) required for interactive editing (Fig. 3 a). We measured in real napari viewers with hidden windows, so no frame is rendered, with both tools in one environment (napari 0.7.0, napari-bbox 0.1.1) on one Apple M2 Pro with 16 GB RAM. Drawing a frame is left to napari and the GPU and depends on the display and its refresh rate, so hidden windows keep the measurement on the plugin code. An automated protocol replays the same seeded mouse drags in the editable X ​ Y XY view of both tools through napari’s mouse dispatch into each plugin’s own drag handler. napari-bbox has no multi-viewer synchronization, so its other views follow every move through its public per-box methods (select, remove, add), with napari’s layer data events deferred to the end of the drag, as napari-bbox’s own drag does. Each drag translates or resizes a box in 60 moves on a 30 Hz schedule, with the X ​ Z XZ and Y ​ Z YZ views placed through the box, and each configuration starts with one untimed drag. Waiting time between moves is excluded and spent busy-waiting. In a control with idle waiting (three runs of ten drags per tool, reported in the repository), napari-TurboBox needed about 8 ms per update in the four-viewer layout, and the speed-up grew from 1.1 × 1.1\times at N = 10 N{=}10 to 2.3 × 2.3\times at N = 10 3 N{=}10^{3} and 19 × 19\times at N = 10 4 N{=}10^{4} . Idle waiting did not change which of these configurations stayed within 33 ms. We record the time from a mouse move until every live 2D view has updated and Qt has processed the pending events. Thumbnails and the 3D view are updated once, at the release, and reported separately. We sweep N N on blank 60 × 256 × 256 60{\times}256{\times}256 -voxel volumes across 50 drags and five replicate runs (separate processes) per tool, since the update cost tracks box and viewer count rather than image content (Fig. 3 f). Moves within one drag are correlated, so the independent unit is the run. We take the median update time within each run and compare the tools on the five per-run medians, with speed-ups as ratios of medians and percentile-bootstrap 95% confidence intervals from 10,000 resamples of the run medians (Fig. 3 d). We call a configuration interactive when its median, 95th and 99th percentile (each taken as the median over the five runs) all stay within 33 ms. Editing latency in the realistic layout. In the four-viewer layout ( X ​ Y XY / X ​ Z XZ / Y ​ Z YZ /3D), the shared store substantially extends the interactive range (Fig. 3 a). napari-bbox stays within the 33 ms budget up to N = 10 3 N=10^{3} (median 18.0 ms, 99th percentile 20.8 ms), exceeds it by its 95th and 99th percentiles at N = 2,000 N=2{,}000 (median 32.4 ms, 95th percentile 33.5 ms, 99th percentile 35.1 ms) and needs 158.7 ms per update at N = 10 4 N=10^{4} . napari-TurboBox needs 1.6–2.0 ms per update from N = 10 N=10 to N = 10 4 N=10^{4} and keeps median, 95th and 99th percentile within the budget up to N = 10 4 N=10^{4} (99th percentile 4.4 ms). Its speed-up over napari-bbox is not a fixed factor but grows with N N , from 2.4 × 2.4\times at N = 10 N{=}10 to 3.2 × 3.2\times at N = 10 2 N{=}10^{2} , 10.5 × 10.5\times at N = 10 3 N{=}10^{3} and 79 × 79\times at N = 10 4 N{=}10^{4} (Fig. 3 d), because each napari-bbox view pays a layer update that grows with N N on every move, while napari-TurboBox changes one shape per view. With 10 4 10^{4} boxes, napari-TurboBox updates all 2D views in 2.0 ms, whereas napari-bbox requires 158.7 ms, i.e. about 6.3 updates per second. Scaling with viewer count. Both tools show a roughly twofold increase in update time for each doubling of synchronized 2D views V V (Fig. 3 b). At N = 10 2 N=10^{2} , scaling V V from 1 to 8 raises the update time from 0.7 ms to 4.1 ms for napari-TurboBox and from 1.4 ms to 15.1 ms for napari-bbox. napari-TurboBox stays 2.0 2.0 – 3.7 × 3.7\times faster across all configurations because each napari-bbox view additionally pays a layer update that grows with N N . The remaining per-view cost, updating each view’s shapes and processing its events, is shared by both tools. Memory, 3D commit, and resolution. At N = 10 4 N{=}10^{4} , napari-TurboBox’s box layer adds about 3 × 3\times less traced memory than napari-bbox’s (13.7 vs 40.0 MiB in one 2D viewer, Fig. 3 c), of which the store and spatial index account for 1.6 MiB. The ratio holds across N N ( 2.7 2.7 – 2.9 × 2.9\times from N = 50 N=50 ). Both tools defer the 3D wireframe update to the end of a drag, but napari-TurboBox triggers it automatically on release where napari-bbox relies on the application to do so, and the napari-TurboBox end-of-drag update (3D view and thumbnails) is 13 × 13\times cheaper at N = 10 3 N{=}10^{3} (10.5 vs 132.1 ms). A box edit never touches the image layer, so image size cannot enter the update time of either tool. We verified this for napari-TurboBox, whose update time holds at 2.1 ms (four 2D views) up to 2048 2 2048^{2} px (Fig. 3 f). Application to real acquisitions. While synthetic benchmarks isolate synchronization overhead, we verified end-to-end usability on real acquisitions of cellular nuclei and transcriptional clusters from confocal, electron, micro-CT, light-sheet and instant-SIM microscopy ( Long et al., 2022 ; Long et al., 2009 ; Weigert et al., 2020 ; Lin et al., 2021 ; Lin et al., 2023 ; Achard et al., 2024 ; Mathis Laboratory of Adaptive Intelligence, 2024 ; Hajiabadi et al., 2022 ; Hajiabadi, 2021 ) . Across all five modalities, 3D boxes derived from the instance labels (for instant-SIM, from a threshold segmentation) passed the plugin’s validation unchanged, stayed valid under a replayed constrained mouse drag in the four-viewer layout and exported directly to COCO and YOLO, with one reproducible notebook per modality in the repository. 4 Conclusion napari-TurboBox makes dense 3D bounding box annotation practical inside napari: an annotator can place and check boxes on volumes with thousands of objects with a plugin update of a few milliseconds per mouse move, whereas napari-bbox leaves the 33 ms budget beyond about 10 3 10^{3} boxes. By sharing a single authoritative state across all viewers instead of an independent box state per viewer, napari-TurboBox removes the update bottleneck at scale while reducing the memory of the box layer about threefold in a single viewer. Because every box is kept axis-aligned and exports directly to COCO and YOLO, the annotations are ready to train detectors or to prompt segmentation models such as nnInteractive without a conversion step. This turns box placement from a bottleneck into a routine part of preparing volumetric training data, and the shared-state architecture could be extended to other prompt types such as points and to direct prompt-response round-trips with foundation models inside the viewer.
