3.2 Failure Mode Definition and Design of Experiments The target failure mode defines the data-generation space and the dependent degradation variable. Corrosion is selected as the governing degradation mechanism because it alters surface morphology, roughness, contact interface, friction, and preload retention. The design of experiments varies factors that influence both condition and response: corrosion exposure, preload level, joint configuration, bolt size, and strength grade. Statistical design-of-experiments principles are used to ensure that these factors span meaningful variability rather than a single narrow degradation path [ 38 ] . The minimum reproducible data path requires repeated or re-localizable observations of the same physical regions. For each exposure/preload condition, multiple specimens are needed to separate specimen variability from exposure effects. Each specimen should include a baseline measurement and at least three post-exposure intervals if PDE discovery is intended. Each time point should include camera images, microscopy patches, roughness or profilometry data, and at least one functional measurement. The first functional target is the friction coefficient μ \mu because it can be derived from torque/clamp-force testing and is directly linked to the mechanical behavior of bolted joints [ 11 ] . Preload loss is introduced as a secondary target when clamp-force measurements are available. Images are not treated as final labels or as direct measurements of a governing law. They are treated as evidence for calibrated degradation fields that can be tested against independent measurements and used in physics-informed prediction. This distinction is important because it prevents visual texture from being interpreted as physics without calibration. The framework also clarifies the meaning of PDE discovery in inspection-oriented corrosion analysis. A discovered equation over c ⁡ ( 𝒔 , t ) c(\bm{s},t) is a candidate effective mesoscale law. It can support prediction if it is stable under perturbation and improves held-out rollout, but it is not a complete electrochemical mechanism unless the required electrochemical variables and boundary conditions are measured. This interpretation keeps the method scientifically bounded while preserving the value of symbolic structure. 5.2 Advantages for Inspection and Circularity The main practical advantage is that the framework begins with feasible inspection data. Camera images, microscopy, roughness measurements, and selected torque/clamp-force tests are more realistic for returned products than full electrochemical characterization. A second advantage is traceability: each functional prediction is linked to surface observations, reconstructed fields, and model assumptions. A third advantage is interpretability: calibrated field definitions and symbolic PDE discovery provide more insight than black-box image regression alone. A fourth advantage is uncertainty awareness, which is essential when degradation histories are unknown. For circularity-oriented engineering, the framework supports a decision chain from observed condition to functional indicator to action. Reuse can be supported when degradation and predicted functional change are low and uncertainty is acceptable. Refurbishment can be supported when degradation is moderate but the functional indicator can be restored. Recycling or disassembly can be supported when degradation is severe, uncertainty is high, or predicted function is unacceptable. The framework therefore provides a methodological basis for using inspection evidence in R-strategy decisions. 5.3 Limitations and Failure Conditions The framework is data-intensive. Time-dependent PDE discovery requires repeated or re-localizable observations of the same surface regions. Cross-modal registration errors can introduce artificial gradients. The surrogate field c c may be predictive without being physically identifiable if it does not correlate with independent roughness, pit, or material-loss measurements. PINN training may become unstable if loss terms are poorly balanced or boundary conditions are not defined. Genetic symbolic discovery can overfit noisy fields without parsimony penalties and perturbation-stability checks. These limitations define the conditions under which the proposed framework is restricted. In particular, it is a structured methodology ready for validation, but it must be tested on specimen-level splits with repeated exposure intervals and independent functional measurements. 5.3 Limitations and Failure Conditions The framework is data-intensive. The framework follows a reproducible sequence: define the corrosion failure mode, generate multimodal inspection and functional-response data, structure the data into descriptors, learn cross-scale similarity between microscopic and camera observations, construct a calibrated corrosion-severity field, discover candidate effective PDEs, and couple corrosion evolution to selected functional indicators. The first functional target is friction coefficient, while preload loss is introduced when clamp-force data are available. The contribution is the methodological architecture connecting image-based degradation information to physically constrained prediction for degraded bolted joints. The framework is intentionally bounded: discovered PDEs are interpreted as candidate effective laws over calibrated degradation fields, not as complete electrochemical mechanisms. Future work will implement the framework on a dedicated experimental dataset with specimen-level splits, repeated exposure intervals, paired microscopy and camera observations, roughness measurements, and torque/clamp-force tests. Synthetic corrosion benchmarks will be used before interpreting real-data PDEs. The central empirical question is whether calibrated image-derived corrosion fields improve held-out prediction of selected functional indicators while producing stable candidate effective laws.
