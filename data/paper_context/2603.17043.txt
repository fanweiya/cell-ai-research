4 Experimental Setup 4.1 Data and Evaluation References We evaluate OpenQlaw using WSe 2 and WS 2 optical microscopy images from a materials laboratory database. Images have a resolution of 3,248 × 2,900 3,248\times 2,900 pixels and were acquired at 25.2 × \times magnification with a recorded scale of 0.1087 μ \mu m/px. Each crystallite entry contains a bounding box, material classification, and human review status. Because individual reviewed entries do not imply exhaustive image-level review, we define an image as fully reviewed only when every associated crystallite entry has been reviewed. These images provide an exhaustive reference for the reviewed material-flake count. However, the reference does not establish a one-to-one correspondence between each returned QuPAINT proposal and a physical flake, so we do not interpret count disagreement as proposal-level detection error. The database contains 7,251 fully reviewed images, including 2,870 with at least one material flake. Of 14,287 reviewed material flakes, 14,218 are labeled thick, and only 69 are mono-, bi-, or trilayer. We therefore evaluate count agreement at scale, while layer-based selection and calibrated measurement are evaluated through tool behavior and refusal conditions. Pilot Set. We use a 20-image WSe 2 pilot for live evaluation of In-Context and State-Grounded OpenQlaw. The pilot supports reference-free measurements including aggregation behavior, tool use, scope selection, and refusal handling. We evaluate count agreement with the reviewed image-level reference only on the fully reviewed cohort described below. Evaluation Cohort. We construct a cohort of 228 fully reviewed images containing 160 WSe 2 and 68 WS 2 images, excluding the pilot. Images are selected deterministically using seed 20260913 and stratified by reviewed material-flake count to cover sparse through densely populated images. 4.2 Evaluation Conditions We evaluate three system configurations and one controlled replay setting. The direct expert condition queries QuPAINT without the OpenQlaw orchestrator and characterizes the relationship between its returned enumeration and summary count. In-Context OpenQlaw represents the original workflow described in Section 2.3 . The orchestrator receives the QuPAINT response as free-form text and performs downstream operations directly in context. State-Grounded OpenQlaw uses the image-scoped records and deterministic operations described in Section 2.4 . We evaluate two live variants. Variant A retains the same orchestrator policy used by In-Context OpenQlaw, while Variant B additionally includes an explicit instruction to use the scientific tool. The matched replay provides a controlled comparison between representation and delegation using identical archived QuPAINT observations. The orchestrator receives either the raw expert response, the same validated observations as a structured record, or the structured record with access to a deterministic count function. Record identifiers are opaque, and record size is withheld, preventing the target count from being exposed directly. Replay uses the same orchestrator model as OpenQlaw but a minimal interaction loop rather than the complete NanoBot workflow. This allows us to compare representation and aggregation while holding the underlying expert observations fixed. 4.3 Metrics and Evaluation Protocol For each trial, we record exact agreement with the requested record count, agreement with the expert enumeration, tool use, record identity and scope, and refusal handling. We additionally compare candidate-list size with the reviewed image-level count on the fully reviewed cohort. Images are the primary evaluation unit, with repeated comparisons summarized by the per-image median. Paired comparisons in matched replay use 10,000 seeded bootstrap samples over images. Prompts and image manifests are frozen before execution, and semantic retries based on response content are not permitted. Live OpenQlaw runs use two NVIDIA L40S GPUs for the orchestrator and one L40S for QuPAINT, while replay uses four NVIDIA A100-40GB GPUs. Candidate-list size strongly tracks the reviewed image-level flake count ( ρ = 0.91 \rho=0.91 ), but the summary count stated in the free-form response agrees with its own enumeration in only 13% of responses where a count is present. Producing candidate observations and aggregating them into an exact summary are therefore distinct operations. In-Context OpenQlaw exhibits the same limitation. On the 20-image pilot, the orchestrator matches the expert enumeration in only 6 of 60 trials and the conclusion count in 2 of 60, while 52 match neither. The median absolute difference from the expert enumeration is 6 observations, and 43% of returned counts are multiples of five. In one trial, for example, QuPAINT returns 97 candidate observations and the orchestrator reports 100. 5.2 In-Context Counting Degradation We isolate representation from deterministic delegation using matched replay. Identical QuPAINT observations are presented as raw text, as a validated structured record, or with access to the deterministic count operation used by State-Grounded OpenQlaw. Each condition contains 684 trials from 228 images across three repeated runs. Raw and structured replay return the exact record count in 40% and 42% of trials, respectively, while State-Grounded OpenQlaw is exact in 684/684 trials. The paired improvement over structured replay is + 0.58 +0.58 with a 95% bootstrap interval of [ 0.52 , 0.64 ] [0.52,0.64] . Their difference decomposes as n ^ − n = ( n ^ − r ) + ( r − n ) , \hat{n}-n=(\hat{n}-r)+(r-n), where n ^ − r \hat{n}-r is the aggregation discrepancy and r − n r-n is the candidate-list/reference discrepancy. State-Grounded OpenQlaw reduces the first term to zero in all 684 matched replay trials, allowing the remaining discrepancy to be examined independently. Candidate-list size remains strongly correlated with the reviewed image-level count ( ρ = 0.91 \rho=0.91 ), although the relationship varies with image density. These differences should not be interpreted as proposal-level detection errors because the reviewed references do not establish a one-to-one correspondence between returned boxes and physical flakes. Distinct overlapping proposals are therefore preserved rather than merged without additional evidence. For the six cohort images in which selected-record size equals the reviewed image-level count, State-Grounded OpenQlaw also returns the reviewed count in 6/6 cases. Candidate enumeration itself remains variable across repeated QuPAINT calls, with a median record-size coefficient of variation of 0.53 and differences of at least 10 observations on 110 of 228 images. This variability remains upstream, while downstream computation over any identified record remains exact. 5.5 Interactive Use The controlled experiments bypass the messaging interface, while the deployed system supports interactive analysis through WhatsApp and Discord. QuPAINT provides rich spatial and material-specific analysis, while In-Context OpenQlaw must still derive exact quantities from the expert’s free-form output. Matched replay shows that restructuring the same observations is not sufficient to resolve this limitation. State-Grounded OpenQlaw instead delegates the operation itself, producing the exact requested record count in all 684 replay trials. This separation also clarifies where remaining disagreement originates. Once aggregation is exact over an identified record, disagreement with the reviewed image-level count can no longer be attributed to an additional counting error. It instead reflects the returned candidate list or the selected scope. Because the reviewed annotations do not establish a one-to-one correspondence between every returned proposal and a physical flake, we do not interpret candidate-list disagreement as proposal-level detection error. Multiple distinct observations may correspond to the same physical region, and OpenQlaw preserves overlapping proposals rather than merging them without additional evidence. Explicit records similarly make scope selection observable. The orchestrator may correctly operate over the narrower conclusion scope when the intended target is the full proposals scope. By binding downstream operations to an identified record and scope, State-Grounded OpenQlaw separates aggregation correctness from scope selection and candidate-list disagreement. This makes each source of discrepancy independently measurable rather than entangled within a single free-form response. Limitations. This work evaluates one general-purpose orchestrator, one QuPAINT checkpoint, and optical microscopy images from one laboratory setting at a single magnification. The reviewed annotations are strongly dominated by thick flakes, so counting is evaluated more extensively than layer-specific selection. Calibrated measurement, ranking, selection, and rendering are verified through tool behavior and refusal conditions rather than large-scale live accuracy studies. Matched replay uses a minimal interaction loop and different hardware from the live OpenQlaw system. It therefore supports controlled comparison of representation and delegation, while the live experiments evaluate tool adoption, scope selection, and refusal behavior. Measurements in this work refer to bounding-box area rather than segmented flake area, and the reviewed references do not provide proposal-level ground truth. 7 Conclusion In this work, we have studied the reliability of downstream operations over rich model-generated scientific observations. We have shown that, although QuPAINT provides physics-aware candidate analysis from optical microscopy images, exact quantities derived directly from longer free-form outputs can become inconsistent with the underlying observations. Controlled replay has shown that structured representation alone does not reliably resolve this behavior, while deterministic delegation has produced the exact requested record count in all 684 cohort trials. To address this limitation, we have introduced State-Grounded OpenQlaw, which converts expert outputs into image-scoped observation records and performs scientific operations over explicitly identified records using deterministic computation and refusal conditions. This formulation has preserved QuPAINT’s original analysis while separating aggregation correctness from scope selection and candidate-list/reference discrepancy. OpenQlaw therefore provides a reliable interface between multimodal scientific reasoning and exact downstream operations. Limitations. Our evaluation has been limited to one general-purpose orchestrator, one fixed QuPAINT checkpoint, and optical microscopy images from a single laboratory setting and magnification. The dataset is strongly dominated by thick flakes, so counting has been evaluated more extensively than selection, ranking, calibrated measurement, and rendering. The reviewed references provide image-level counts rather than proposal-level correspondence. Future Work. Future work will extend State-Grounded OpenQlaw to broader scientific tasks and evaluate deterministic operations, including layer-based selection, ranking, calibrated measurement, and rendering. We plan to study generalization across orchestrators, materials, and imaging conditions while preserving explicit scientific state and deterministic downstream computation.
