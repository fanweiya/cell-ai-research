3.2 CAUSE-ViT Architecture We introduce CAUSE-ViT as a representative transformer-based segmentation model used to instantiate and validate the proposed evaluation framework. The architectural design is intentionally aligned with existing multi-scale ViT paradigms, allowing us to isolate the effect of training strategies and interventions. At the core of CAUSE-ViT lies a multi-scale self-supervised Vision Transformer that follows an encoder–decoder design (Figure 2 ). The key novelty of CAUSE-ViT is a multi-scale encoder composed of independent scale-specific ViT streams with periodic cross-scale feature fusion, enabling structured interaction between representations at different resolutions. Specifically, (1) the encoder consists of multiple parallel ViT streams, each operating at a distinct resolution to capture complementary morphological patterns. Unlike conventional multi-scale designs that rely on shared weights or late fusion, each stream in CAUSE-ViT is independently initialised, encouraging scale-specific feature learning. (2) To promote interaction across scales, we introduce periodic cross-scale feature fusion, where representations are exchanged between streams every two layers. This allows the model to jointly capture fine-grained cellular details and broader tissue context, improving representation consistency across resolutions. Building on this encoder, CAUSE-ViT employs a high-resolution ViT (HRViT)-style backbone to preserve local detail (Figure 2(a) ), while integrating a SETR-MLA-inspired decoder (Figure 2(b) ) to aggregate multi-scale features into coherent global representations. Skip connections, implemented via transposed or standard convolutions, ensure efficient information flow and preserve spatial detail during upsampling. The decoder upsamples encoder features through learnable transposed convolutions, followed by ReLU activations and normalisation, avoiding information loss associated with fixed interpolation methods, as in SegFormer [ 19 ] . Overall, this design encourages representations that are less dependent on scale-specific artefacts and more consistent across resolutions, supporting robust segmentation under domain variability. 3.4 Causality-Inspired Interventional Evaluation We now formalise the proposed counterfactual evaluation framework. Unlike standard evaluation protocols that measure predictive accuracy, our approach explicitly measures how predictions change under controlled interventions on the input. This enables direct assessment of whether models rely on pathology-relevant structures or spurious correlations. While augmentations are commonly used for regularisation during training, we reinterpret them here as explicit interventions at evaluation time; rather than improving performance, these interventions are used to probe model behaviour under controlled distribution shifts. Accordingly, we define a conceptual structural abstraction with foreground tissue structure ( F F ), background context ( B B ), observed image ( X X ), and segmentation output ( Y Y ), with causal relations F , B → X F,B\rightarrow X and X → Y X\rightarrow Y . To disentangle sensitivity to nuisance background variation from sensitivity to pathology-relevant foreground structure, we define the intervention protocol using a matched reference condition. First, we construct a background-normalised image, X r ​ e ​ f X^{ref} , in which background/domain appearance is adjusted while foreground tissue morphology is preserved. We then generate a foreground-perturbed image, X f ​ g X^{fg} , by applying local blurring to the foreground regions of X r ​ e ​ f X^{ref} while keeping the background treatment unchanged. The foreground intervention effect is defined as CE F = 𝔼 ⁡ [ Dice ⁡ ( Y ⁡ ( X r ​ e ​ f ) , M ) − Dice ⁡ ( Y ⁡ ( X f ​ g ) , M ) ] , \mathrm{CE}_{F}=\mathbb{E}\big[\mathrm{Dice}(Y(X^{ref}),M)-\mathrm{Dice}(Y(X^{fg}),M)\big], (1) where M M denotes the ground-truth mask. This measures the performance decrease attributable to degradation of foreground tissue structure under matched background conditions. The background sensitivity term is defined relative to the standard out-of-distribution (OOD) image, X o ​ o ​ d X^{ood} , as CE B = 𝔼 ⁡ [ Dice ⁡ ( Y ⁡ ( X r ​ e ​ f ) , M ) − Dice ⁡ ( Y ⁡ ( X o ​ o ​ d ) , M ) ] , \mathrm{CE}_{B}=\mathbb{E}\big[\mathrm{Dice}(Y(X^{ref}),M)-\mathrm{Dice}(Y(X^{ood}),M)\big], (2) which quantifies the performance difference associated with nuisance background/domain variation. The Causal Reliance Score is then given by CRS = CE F CE B + ϵ . \mathrm{CRS}=\frac{\mathrm{CE}_{F}}{\mathrm{CE}_{B}+\epsilon}. (3) Under this formulation, CRS is interpreted as a relative sensitivity measure with respect to the chosen perturbations, separating the contribution of background/domain mismatch from the incremental effect of foreground degradation. 4 Experiments and Results The goal of the experiments is to demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed counterfactual intervention framework and CRS in revealing model behaviour. 4.1 Datasets The proposed CAUSE-ViT framework is evaluated under both in-distribution (InD) and out-of-distribution (OOD) histopathology settings to assess performance under matched and shifted data conditions. For InD training and validation, we use SNOW (Synthetic Nuclei and annotation Wizard) [ 4 ] , a large-scale synthetic breast histology dataset of 512 × 512 512\times 512 image tiles with paired nuclei segmentation masks, which were resized to 256 × 256 256\times 256 and randomly augmented to support stable model development. For OOD evaluation, we use CryoNuSeg [ 11 ] , a fully manually annotated frozen-section H&E dataset that introduces realistic domain shifts, including organ diversity, staining variability, and frozen-section artefacts. This dataset pairing is intentional: SNOW enables reliable optimisation under controlled conditions, while CryoNuSeg provides a stringent test of whether the model generalises through pathology-relevant features rather than dataset-specific background or staining cues. In total, SNOW contains 18,213 images, which are split into 80% for training and 20% for validation. 4.2 Evaluation Criteria, Interventional Robustness, and Causal Reliance Segmentation performance is evaluated using binary cross-entropy (BCE) loss, Dice score, and mean Intersection over Union (mIoU) across both InD and OOD settings. To assess robustness under causal perturbations, we further evaluate the model under Causal Intervention (1b: Raw OOD input), Causal Intervention (1b: Background-normalised reference), and Causal Intervention (1c: Foreground-blurred condition). Beyond accuracy, we analyse model sensitivity to foreground tissue structure and background/domain variation using the proposed CRS, computed post-hoc on CryoNuSeg from Dice-based interventional contrasts. Specifically, evaluation on CryoNuSeg is performed under three conditions: the raw OOD input, a background-normalised reference condition that preserves foreground tissue structure, and a matched foreground-blurred condition obtained by applying local blurring to the cell regions of the same reference image. In line with the formulation above, the reference condition is used to separate the contribution of nuisance background/domain variation from the incremental effect of foreground degradation. Accordingly, CE B \mathrm{CE}_{B} captures the performance difference between the reference condition and the raw OOD input, whereas CE F \mathrm{CE}_{F} captures the additional performance decrease induced by foreground blurring relative to that same reference. CRS is then computed from these two interventional contrasts. Thus, unlike Dice or mean IoU, CRS cannot be computed from standard predictions alone; it requires evaluation under a controlled perturbation protocol and is therefore intended as an intervention-based companion metric for behavioural auditing on held-out test data. 4.3 Implementation Details All experiments were implemented in Python and conducted on Google Colab using NVIDIA A100 and L4 GPUs. CAUSE-ViT, comprising 74M parameters with a multi-scale patch embedding module, 8 attention heads, and a dropout rate of 0.2, was trained under three experimental settings: supervised learning, generative SSL, and contrastive SSL. The supervised configuration was trained for 25 epochs with a batch size of 32, while both generative SSL and contrastive SSL were trained for 50 epochs, using batch sizes of 32 and 45, respectively. 4.4 Quantitative Results Table 1 summarises the segmentation performance of the CAUSE-ViT variants using BCE loss, Dice, and mean IoU on the SNOW InD dataset. The supervised baseline learns directly from labelled data, which can lead to reliance on dataset-specific appearance cues, while contrastive SSL leverages paired augmentations to enforce representation consistency but may remain sensitive to superficial visual correlations. In contrast, generative SSL, based on masked reconstruction, encourages the model to recover missing structural information, thereby promoting the learning of more intrinsic and pathology-relevant features. As a result, CAUSE-ViT with generative SSL consistently performs best on the SNOW InD dataset, indicating stronger segmentation quality and improved generalisation to unseen hold-out validation data. Table 2 further evaluates the same models on CryoNuSeg under causal intervention conditions. Both the background-normalised and foreground-blurred conditions produce substantially higher Dice scores than the raw OOD baseline, indicating that the raw OOD setting is dominated by nuisance background/domain mismatch and that the principal performance recovery is associated with background normalisation. Foreground sensitivity is therefore assessed relative to the matched reference condition, under which foreground blurring induces only a modest additional Dice decrease. Consistent with this pattern, CRS values are low across all models, indicating that the dominant performance change arises from background/domain variation rather than from the applied foreground perturbation. The Generative SSL variant attains the lowest CRS, reflecting the smallest incremental sensitivity to foreground blurring relative to the much larger background/domain effect. Together with its consistently strongest Dice scores across conditions, this suggests that Generative SSL is the most stable model under the present intervention design. However, because the current foreground perturbation produces only a limited additional decrease, these results should be interpreted as evidence of relative robustness under intervention rather than as a strong demonstration of dominant foreground reliance. Rather than focusing on architectural novelty, we emphasise understanding how model behaviour changes under controlled interventions. Through the proposed CRS and intervention-based analysis, we show that standard metrics such as Dice and IoU are insufficient to explain performance changes under distribution shift. In particular, robust interpretation requires separating nuisance background/domain effects from foreground perturbation effects. Our results demonstrate that models with similar performance can exhibit fundamentally different behaviours under perturbation. We further show that augmentation strategies can be interpreted as implicit counterfactual interventions, providing a bridge between training and evaluation. While demonstrated using a transformer-based model, the proposed framework is model-agnostic and can be applied broadly to audit reliability and robustness in medical image segmentation systems. The proposed framework relies on approximated interventions implemented via image transformations, which may not perfectly correspond to true causal manipulations. Additionally, the CRS is a relative metric and depends on the choice of perturbations. Future work will explore more principled causal formulations and extend the framework to other imaging modalities and tasks.
