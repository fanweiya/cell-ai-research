2 Related Method Computational pathology is often hindered by data scarcity and poor domain generalization. Generative models address these limitations by synthesizing high-fidelity training instances. While early approaches relied on VAEs and GANs, Diffusion Models (DMs) have emerged as the state-of-the-art, generating images via iterative denoising [ 37 ] . Offering superior semantic fidelity and training stability, DMs are increasingly adopted for complex pathology tasks, including report-guided generation, gigapixel WSI synthesis, and rare-label augmentation. 2.1 Video Diffusion for Z-axis Morphology Modeling Compared with a single tissue section, a z-stack captures complementary morphological information across adjacent focal planes. While native 3D imaging techniques such as OTLS and MicroCT provide this information directly, their clinical adoption remains limited, and reconstruction from serial tissue sections requires specialized acquisition protocols that are unavailable in routine pathology [ 27 , 7 ] . Rather than reconstructing physical tissue volumes, our objective is to learn the gradual morphological variation across ordered focal planes. This setting differs from conventional 3D scene reconstruction, where multiple viewpoints are available, such as NeRF [ 25 ] or Gaussian Splatting [ 15 ] , but naturally resembles video generation, where neighboring frames exhibit strong local correlations. We therefore adopt the Latent Video Diffusion Model (LVDM) [ 2 , 1 ] as the backbone and reinterpret the temporal dimension as the z-axis of a pathology z-stack. The spatial-temporal attention mechanism enables the model to capture cross-plane morphological dependencies, which are subsequently transferred to conventional 2D pathology through pseudo z-stack synthesis. 2.1 Video Diffusion for Z-axis Morphology Modeling Compared with a single tissue section, a z-stack captures complementary morphological information across adjacent focal planes. The temporal modules include 3D convolutional blocks and temporal attention layers, designed to capture dependencies across the video frames. In our settings, we reinterpret the temporal dimension as the ordered focal-plane dimension of a pathology z-stack. Thus, the image backbone θ \theta processes each slice as an independent image, while the temporal layers ϕ \phi treat them as a sequence with depth structural correlations along the tissue depth. Directly applying the pretrained model often results in domain-shift artifacts (e.g., stain mismatch). Using the static volume constructed by duplicating a target image N o N_{o} times collapses the depth-wise structural variations. Another option is to fine-tune the spatial layers in θ \theta on target images as in [ 4 ] ; however, we observed that this led to temporal instability, i.e., slice-to-slice inconsistency (flicker), likely because image-only supervision disrupts temporal dynamics learned during z-stack training (Section 4.4 ). Following the joint image-video optimization paradigm adopted in recent video generation models, we adopt a Mixture Fine-tuning strategy for Domain Adaptation (MFDA) that smoothly transfers the model to the target image distribution. Unlike previous methods, our objective is not to improve video generation quality but to preserve the learned z-axis morphological prior while adapting the spatial appearance to conventional pathology datasets. We construct training batches comprising two streams: 1) Z-stack streams ( 𝐗 ∈ 𝒟 z ​ s ​ t ​ a ​ c ​ k \mathbf{X}\in\mathcal{D}_{zstack} ); 2) Target image streams ( 𝐲 ∈ 𝒟 c ​ a ​ n ​ c ​ e ​ r \mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{D}_{cancer} ). The z-stack stream is optimized using the blur-aware objective in Eq. 12 , preserving cross-plane morphological consistency. For the target image stream, only the spatial backbone θ \theta is updated by bypassing the temporal modules ϕ \phi , allowing the model to adapt to the target appearance without disrupting the learned z-axis correlations. The key insight is to uncouple the updates but optimize the parameters jointly for 2D and 3D modules. Similar to Stage I, the VAE is first fine-tuned on the mixed data before optimizing the diffusion model. Then, for the LDM, we use image embedding 𝐜 ( j ) \mathbf{c}^{(j)} of 𝐲 ( j ) \mathbf{y}^{(j)} as an image condition to guide the generation. By combining multiple focal plane slices, z-stack imaging can retain structural details across different depths, as in Fig. 2 (a). To quantify the preservation of cross-plane morphology, we further introduce structure tensor (ST)-based metrics. Specifically, we compute the 3D structure tensor for each z-stack and compare both local orientation and anisotropic tissue organization. Orientation Similarity measures the cosine similarity between the principal eigenvectors of paired structure tensors, while ST Frobenius measures the distance between the corresponding structure tensors. Anisotropy MAE evaluates the consistency of local structural anisotropy. Finally, Vz Wasserstein and ST Evolution corr characterize the statistical evolution of tissue morphology along the z-axis by comparing the distributions of principal orientation components and inter-slice structural changes, respectively. As shown in Table 1 , PathoS2VD consistently outperforms the other two models across all evaluation metrics. Compared with Pix2Pix, PathoS2VD improves SSIM by 7.3% while reducing LPIPS, FID, and FVD by 26.2%, 6.1%, and 77.4%, respectively. Compared with OpenSoraPlan, the improvements are even larger, particularly in FVD (-63.9%), indicating substantially better cross-plane consistency. The ST-based metrics further demonstrate that PathoS2VD preserves biologically meaningful cross-plane morphology. Orientation Similarity reaches 0.9407, indicating that the dominant local structural orientations are well preserved throughout the synthesized z-stack. Meanwhile, the substantially lower ST Frobenius distance (0.77 versus 4.82 and 257.51) suggests that the local spatial organization of tissue closely matches the ground truth. Consistently, the lowest Anisotropy MAE indicates that the generated pseudo z-stacks preserve the directional coherence of tissue microstructures more faithfully than competing methods. Unlike the previous metrics, which compare local structural similarity, Vz Wasserstein and ST Evolution corr evaluate the statistical evolution of morphology along the z-axis. PathoS2VD achieves the lowest Vz Wasserstein distance (0.0234) and the highest ST Evolution corr (0.8949), demonstrating that it more faithfully reproduces the gradual morphological transitions across neighboring focal planes. These observations suggest that the learned z-axis prior captures not only the appearance of individual slices but also their cross-plane morphological progression. It is worth noting that OpenSoraPlan is originally designed for first-frame-conditioned video generation with a temporal compression ratio of 4. In our experiments, we only replaced the first-frame condition with a middle-slice condition while leaving the remaining architecture unchanged. Consequently, its causal design and aggressive temporal compression are not well matched to the symmetric and fine-grained nature of pathology z-stacks. These observations highlight that z-stack synthesis requires task-specific modeling of cross-plane morphology rather than directly transferring generic video generation models. By repurposing an image-to-video latent diffusion model with prior-preserving mixture fine-tuning, PathoS2VD synthesizes plausible pseudo z-stacks that enrich 2D pathology with complementary cross-plane morphological information. Experiments demonstrate realistic z-axis morphology and consistent improvements in downstream cancer classification. Our current study is limited to prostate z-stack data, and the diffusion model is trained only on benign tissue, which may restrict its ability to capture more diverse pathological morphology. Future work will investigate larger and more diverse z-stack datasets across multiple organs and disease types to learn more generalizable z-axis morphological priors.
