2 Proposed Approach Let I t I_{t} denote the input image at time t t , and V t V_{t} the set of candidate cells. The proposed method detects V t V_{t} , assigns correspondence and division scores to candidates in adjacent frames, and determines connections under constraints on the entire graph. Unless otherwise specified, distances and displacements are expressed in physical coordinates in μ \mu m. We refer to annotated cell positions and connections as ground truth (GT). The detector combines heatmaps from 2.5D and 3D models and outputs a position and confidence for each candidate. Dense flow estimates motion between adjacent frames, and cell matching computes correspondence scores from motion-compensated spatial relationships and image features. The same flow is also used to align the inputs of the division-parent model. Scores for pre, parent, and post, corresponding to cells immediately before division, dividing parents, and daughters immediately after division, are used together with motion and correspondence features to calibrate ordinary links and division candidates. Lineage graph optimization integrates these scores and selects nodes, ordinary links, and divisions, followed by gap filling and coordinate refinement. Image models and calibrators are trained in stages, and their predictions are integrated through graph optimization. This design allows downstream components to be compared with detection candidates held fixed. We address offline tracking, where the entire video is available; division identification and coordinate refinement also use future frames. Figure 1 summarizes the inference pipeline. Images, detections, and lineages are schematic. 2.1 Backbone Architecture for 3D Volumes We primarily use a 2.5D U-Net to extract features from 3D images. This architecture integrates information along the depth axis while using an encoder pretrained on 2D images. The basic design follows 2.5D U-Net with Depth Reduction [ 16 ] by Uchida and Fukui. At each encoder stage, z slices are folded into the batch dimension for 2D convolution. The stage output is restored to its 3D arrangement, reduced in depth by average pooling along z, and passed to the next stage. The decoder receives 3D features through skip connections and restores spatial resolution using upsampling and 3D convolutions. This architecture aggregates depth context hierarchically while retaining pretrained 2D encoder weights for initialization. For the 2.5D detection and matching models, each input slice is stacked along the channel dimension with its neighboring z slices, yielding 3 channels for the 2D encoder. This exposes the earliest feature extraction stage to local depth information. These channels represent spatial neighbors and are distinct from the temporal inputs used in Section 2.5 . In the division models, a shared encoder processes each single-channel image separately before temporal information is integrated. We use EfficientNetV2 [ 15 ] and EfficientNet [ 14 ] as 2D encoders. Detection uses EfficientNetV2-L and EfficientNet-B7, whereas matching and division identification use EfficientNetV2-S. The matching model outputs intermediate decoder features, and the detection models output single-channel heatmaps. The architecture is shared across tasks, but the weights are trained independently. For detection, we also use the fully 3D SegResNet [ 11 ] implemented in MONAI [ 2 ] . It has a residual encoder–decoder built from 3D convolutions, 32 initial channels, 4 encoder stages with 1 residual block per stage, and Group Normalization. Its heatmap is included in the ensemble because its feature extraction differs from that of the 2.5D models. The flow model is a separate, compact 3D network described in Section 2.3 . 2.2 Cell Detection We formulate cell center detection as 3D heatmap regression. We therefore estimate dense flow between adjacent 3D images and predict the location of each detected cell in the next frame. Images are downsampled by factors of ( z , y , x ) = ( 1 , 4 , 4 ) (z,y,x)=(1,4,4) , yielding an isotropic grid with 1.625 μ \mu m spacing along each axis. A shared 3D encoder processes the two images, and local correlations are computed between features at half resolution. Correlations over candidate displacements are normalized with a softmax, and their expected displacement provides the initial flow. This flow is interpolated to the input resolution and refined by a residual predicted by a decoder that combines deeper features from both images. The flow model has 3 encoder stages with 32, 64, and 128 channels. Each block consists of 3D convolutions and GELU activations without normalization layers. The output is a displacement vector with 3 components in the ( z , y , x ) (z,y,x) directions at each voxel. This allows motion to vary spatially rather than restricting the entire image to a single translation. The training objective combines self-supervised losses on real image pairs with a supervised loss on synthetic deformations. For real pairs, we use image intensity error after warping, error in the census descriptor of local contrast, forward–backward cycle consistency, spatial flow smoothness, and feature consistency under warping. Weighting by fluorescence signal and local contrast reduces the tendency toward a zero-flow solution that explains only the dominant background. Synthetic pairs are generated by applying known translations and smooth local deformations to real images. Supervision with known displacements complements motion learning in regions where intensity consistency alone is ambiguous. The 199 training videos are shuffled with random seed 42 and split into 179 videos for flow training and 20 for validation, independently of the tracking CV folds. Real training pairs and the source images for synthetic pairs come from the 179-video subset. GT cell links are used only in the 20-video validation subset to assess endpoint error for model selection. The selected checkpoint is epoch 48, which also has the lowest validation endpoint error in the saved training history. The same flow weights are used across all CV folds and for hidden-test inference, without adaptation to hidden-test images. Both frames, downsampled by ( 1 , 4 , 4 ) (1,4,4) , are processed by an EfficientNetV2-S 2.5D U-Net trained independently of the detector. Weights are shared between the frames, and decoder features at stride 2 relative to the input grid are sampled at detection positions using trilinear interpolation. We directly use the 64-channel intermediate decoder output, omitting the subsequent higher-resolution decoder and detection head. Positional encodings are added to each cell feature, and self-attention and cross-attention incorporate context within cell sets and between frames. Learnable role embeddings distinguish the preceding and following frames, encoding temporal direction. Correspondence scores use appearance cosine similarity as a prior score, with a correction derived from the attention features. Candidate matches are selected from motion-compensated neighborhoods. In addition to candidate scores, the matcher outputs a null score for the absence of a correspondence. Forward and backward conditional probabilities distinguish associations supported in both directions from those with strong support in only one direction. Training uses a 16 μ \mu m neighborhood, while the basic downstream candidate set contains edges among the top 5 matcher candidates whose motion-compensated distance is within 12 μ \mu m. Training targets are constructed by matching detections to GT points and transferring annotated edges to pairs of detections. Points with annotated GT connections serve as anchors. Cross-entropy is computed in both directions to select the correct match from a softmax over the candidates and the null option. Unannotated cells remain competing candidates, but anchors without annotated connections are not automatically assigned null targets. A dividing parent has 2 correct daughters. We construct a separate training row for each daughter and exclude the other daughter from the negative candidates in that row. To simulate missed detections, the correct candidate is hidden with a fixed probability and the matcher is trained to select the null option. During training only, missed GT endpoints are added to ensure that positive pairs are available. No GT-based candidates are added during validation or inference. The matcher provides correspondence probabilities, candidate ranks, margins relative to competing candidates, and support in both temporal directions. These outputs are candidate-level evidence rather than final connections. When multiple cells compete for the same match, or ordinary links compete with divisions, selection is determined by the optimization in Section 2.6 . 2.5 Division Identification In a single frame, division can be difficult to distinguish from ordinary cells or nearby cell pairs. If ordinary connections are fixed before divisions are added, candidate daughters may already be assigned to other connections, preventing the correct division from being formed. We address this competition by including ordinary links and divisions in a common optimization problem. Candidate event construction. Ordinary link candidates are the top 5 matcher candidates within a motion-compensated distance of 12 μ \mu m. Basic division candidates are formed from pairs of candidate edges sharing a parent. Candidate generation requires a score of at least 0.1 from the five-input parent model and checks daughter geometry and support from preceding and following correspondence candidates. We additionally use the unaligned three-input parent model and support from preceding and following links to obtain up to 5 new daughter candidates per parent within a radius of 16 μ \mu m. Pairs consisting of a representative daughter from the basic candidates and a new daughter are added as expanded division candidates. Separate calibrators are used for the basic and expanded candidates. The parent-score threshold limits the candidates considered computationally; it is not a probability threshold for accepting a division. The optimization can revise a daughter’s connection even if the initial graph assigns it to another parent. Score calibration. Features include motion-compensated distance, forward and backward matcher scores, candidate ranks, margins relative to competing candidates, detection confidence, and parent, pre, and post scores. Pre scores of predecessor cells are aggregated over input candidate edges, not only over the edges ultimately selected. Examples include the maximum product of a predecessor candidate’s pre score and matcher probability, the pre score of the highest-ranked candidate, and a probability-weighted mean. This makes temporal evidence from multiple possible lineages available before the graph is selected. With sparse annotations, candidates fall into three categories: true positive (TP), false positive (FP), and unevaluated. After optimization, we fill track interruptions caused by temporary missed detections. Collective motion at each frame boundary is estimated from the median displacement of selected links. Accumulated motion compensates the positional difference between a track endpoint and a subsequent track start. Connections are attempted in increasing order of the number of missing frames, g = 1 , 2 , 3 g=1,2,3 , with a distance tolerance of 3 ​ g + 1 3\sqrt{g+1} μ \mu m for a gap of g g frames. For each time point and gap length, endpoints and starts are matched one-to-one using the Hungarian algorithm. An endpoint already used for a connection is not reused, and shorter gaps are resolved first. For each accepted connection, g g nodes are inserted by linear interpolation between the original endpoint coordinates, creating a sequence of edges between adjacent frames. Weakly connected components with fewer than 6 nodes are then removed. Gap filling precedes removal because discarding all short fragments first would also remove fragments that could be connected. Only detection and interpolated-node positions are refined; node counts, time points, and connections remain fixed. 2.1 Backbone Architecture for 3D Volumes We primarily use a 2.5D U-Net to extract features from 3D images. This architecture integrates information along the depth axis while using an encoder pretrained on 2D images. The basic design follows 2.5D U-Net with Depth Reduction [ 16 ] by Uchida and Fukui. At each encoder stage, z slices are folded into the batch dimension for 2D convolution. The stage output is restored to its 3D arrangement, reduced in depth by average pooling along z, and passed to the next stage. The decoder receives 3D features through skip connections and restores spatial resolution using upsampling and 3D convolutions. This architecture aggregates depth context hierarchically while retaining pretrained 2D encoder weights for initialization. For the 2.5D detection and matching models, each input slice is stacked along the channel dimension with its neighboring z slices, yielding 3 channels for the 2D encoder. This exposes the earliest feature extraction stage to local depth information. These channels represent spatial neighbors and are distinct from the temporal inputs used in Section 2.5 . In the division models, a shared encoder processes each single-channel image separately before temporal information is integrated. We use EfficientNetV2 [ 15 ] and EfficientNet [ 14 ] as 2D encoders. Detection uses EfficientNetV2-L and EfficientNet-B7, whereas matching and division identification use EfficientNetV2-S. The matching model outputs intermediate decoder features, and the detection models output single-channel heatmaps. The architecture is shared across tasks, but the weights are trained independently. For detection, we also use the fully 3D SegResNet [ 11 ] implemented in MONAI [ 2 ] . It has a residual encoder–decoder built from 3D convolutions, 32 initial channels, 4 encoder stages with 1 residual block per stage, and Group Normalization. Its heatmap is included in the ensemble because its feature extraction differs from that of the 2.5D models. The flow model is a separate, compact 3D network described in Section 2.3 . 3 Experimental Results 3.1 Experimental Setup The training data comprise 71 videos from embryo 44b6 and 128 videos from embryo 6bba. Training and test data come from different embryos. Each video contains 100 frames of 64 × \times 256 × \times 256 voxels. Voxel spacing is 1.625 μ \mu m along z and 0.40625 μ \mu m in the xy plane. Across all videos, annotations contain 133,318 nodes, 128,883 edges, and 151 divisions, counting annotations in overlapping crops separately. The organizer-provided estimates of total node counts sum to 4,725,117. Dividing the annotated node count by this estimate gives the approximately 2.8% ratio reported in Section 1 . We compare configurations using video-level 5-fold cross-validation (CV). Unless otherwise specified, scores in the main text are CV results aggregated over all 199 videos. Video IDs are sorted within each embryo and assigned to the 5 folds in round-robin order, so every fold contains videos from both embryos. All frames of a video belong to the same fold; training and validation are not split within a video. The detectors, matcher, and division models generate out-of-fold (OOF) predictions by excluding each video’s fold from training. For CV evaluation, each model uses one set of weights corresponding to the video’s held-out fold. Candidate features are constructed from these predictions, and calibrators are also trained without the evaluation fold. Prior rates for total cells, annotated edges, and divisions are estimated from the calibrators’ training videos. Dense flow uses the separate training and model-selection procedure described in Section 2.3 and does not follow the same 5-fold OOF scheme. We use stacking, with calibrators trained on OOF image-model outputs, rather than nested cross-validation that retrains all image models for each outer validation fold. Some crops from the same embryo overlap in space and time; the existing split contains 37 overlapping pairs that cross fold boundaries. Shared optimization prices are computed by pooling the expected counts from all 199 OOF predictions, with each video’s calibrators and count priors fitted outside its fold. The optimization is therefore performed jointly over the CV pool rather than separately within each evaluation fold. The cumulative evaluation and ablations share the detection candidates from the ensemble in Section 2.2 . The initial candidate count is 5,320,079 across all 199 videos. Section 3.4 replaces the detector to evaluate transfer of the downstream models. Table 1 lists the main training settings. The 2.5D detectors use pretrained encoders, while the fully 3D detector is initialized randomly. Detector evaluation uses the EMA weights from the final epoch. The five-input parent model also uses EMA. Among checkpoints whose median scores for validation negatives and unmatched detections in the evaluation fold are below 0.05 and 0.15, respectively, we select the checkpoint with the highest AUC computed from labels in that fold. If no checkpoint satisfies these criteria, the final epoch is used. Scores of unmatched detections are used for this selection diagnostic but are not included in the parent model’s negative loss. On fixed candidates, improvements are observed from joint selection of ordinary links and divisions, the calibration ensemble relative to a linear model, and auxiliary information after division. The final-output comparisons do not establish clear improvements from learned Exposure, an explicit node price, repeated price updates, auxiliary information before division, or flow-based input alignment. Detection and downstream tracking. Cell detections are the shared input to correspondence estimation and division identification. Downstream classification alone cannot recover divisions with missing daughter detections or daughters outside the candidate search range. We evaluate the complete pipeline using the 2.5D and 3D detector ensemble trained for sparse annotations as a common foundation, and compare downstream components on fixed candidates. We do not individually ablate the detection mask, loss normalization, or ensemble members. Scope. Because the official metric relies on annotated lineages, it does not comprehensively evaluate tracking accuracy in unannotated regions.
