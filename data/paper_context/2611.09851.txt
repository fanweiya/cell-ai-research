2 Methods Study design and cohort This retrospective cohort included all available digitized routine diagnostic H&E WSIs from the Mount Sinai Health System (MSHS) through October 2025, together with linked pathology reports and clinical metadata. Cases without digitized H&E WSIs were excluded. The finalized cohort comprised 257,322 patients, 327,483 pathology cases, 731,972 specimens, and 1,302,440 WSIs. The data followed a hierarchical structure: each patient could contribute one or more cases, each case with one or more specimens, and each specimen with one or more WSIs. De-identified identifiers preserved these links. Routine staining and scanning were centralized within MSHS. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board at the Icahn School of Medicine at Mount Sinai (Protocol STUDY-20-00338). Informed consent was waived under the approved protocol. Report extraction and organ grouping Model development and evaluation were stratified by specimen organ. Pathology reports were linked to cases and parsed at the specimen level using OpenAI GPT-5 mini 36 , accessed through Azure OpenAI. Schema-constrained outputs recorded each specimen’s anatomic source, normalized organ label, and verbatim diagnosis text. The extraction protocol is summarized in Supplementary Note Diagnosis extraction . Extracted organ labels were normalized into 25 groups selected for clinical interpretability and sufficient data for model training and evaluation. Closely related anatomic sites were consolidated where appropriate, including colon/rectum, bone/joint, and soft tissue. Specimens not mapped to these groups were assigned to an Other category and excluded from pan-organ analyses. The final organ set included bladder, bone marrow, bone/joint, brain, breast, cervix, colon/rectum, esophagus, gallbladder, gastric, kidney, liver, lung, lymph node, mouth, ovary, pancreas, placenta, prostate, sinus, skin, small bowel, soft tissue, thyroid, and uterus. For the cohort overview, specimen-level diagnosis text was visualized using UMAP and classified into four broad categories (neoplastic, normal, inflammatory, and other) using rule-based text mapping (Fig. 1 B). Details are provided in Supplementary Note Diagnostic spectrum embedding . Demographic labels Eligibility was determined separately for each demographic target based on the availability of a valid label. Sex was recorded at the patient level and harmonized to female and male; indeterminate or unknown labels were excluded. Sex prediction was evaluated across 19 organ groups, excluding prostate, breast, cervix, ovary, uterus, and placenta. Self-reported race was recorded at the patient level and harmonized to five mutually exclusive categories: White, Black or African American, Hispanic or Latino, Asian, and Other. Other included Native Hawaiian or Other Pacific Islander, American Indian or Alaska Native, and Other Race; missing or unmappable values were excluded. Age at the pathology encounter was calculated as case receive year minus birth year and grouped into 20–34, 35–44, 45–54, 55–64, 65–74, and ≥ \geq 75 years. Cases with missing age or age below 20 years were excluded from age-group analyses. Diagnosis embeddings and clustering Diagnosis text embeddings were used to group specimens with similar diagnostic contexts for stratified evaluation of demographic predictability and the construction of cluster-constrained training groups. Specimen-level diagnosis text was encoded using OpenAI text-embedding-3-large , producing 3,072-dimensional embeddings. Embeddings were computed at the specimen level because a single pathology case may contain multiple specimens from different anatomic sites with distinct diagnoses. For each organ, we applied k k -means clustering to the diagnosis embeddings. The number of clusters was selected separately from an organ-specific candidate grid using silhouette scores. K K -means was fit once per organ, over all eligible specimens for that organ. The resulting assignments were fixed across all Monte Carlo cross-validation (MCCV) folds. Controlled training regimes The overall study design is summarized in Fig. 2 A. Observed-distribution training (S1) sampled cases according to the observed demographic-class distribution. Demographic-balanced training (S2) used case-level resampling to balance demographic-class exposure, using the S1 epoch-size target. Diagnosis-context-constrained grouped training (S3) constructed demographic-balanced groups within diagnosis clusters in each fold’s training pool. Retained classes were oversampled to the largest class before assembling groups containing one case from each retained class. For group construction, specimen-level cluster assignments were mapped to cases by majority vote, with ties resolved by the first tied cluster encountered in specimen row order. The random grouped-training control (S3 RND {}_{\text{RND}} ) applied the same demographic balancing procedure to the full training pool without diagnosis-context constraints. This comparison assessed the additional association of diagnosis-context constraints under grouped training; it did not isolate data size or guarantee identical training exposure. Detailed group-construction, class-retention, and epoch-exposure rules are provided in Supplementary Method 1.1 Grouped training and epoch exposure . All regimes shared the same model architecture, prediction unit, and cross-validation design. Model development For each case and organ target, eligible H&E slides from organ-matched specimens were combined into one case-level MIL bag, capped at 30 slides and 100,000 tiles. For cases exceeding the 30-slide cap, slides were randomly subsampled at each training epoch using a seeded random procedure. At least one organ-matched specimen with linked H&E WSIs was required for inclusion. GrandQC 37 excluded background, pen marks, and artifacts before tile extraction at 20 × \times magnification. The in-house small vision transformer (ViT-small) pathology foundation model SP22M 38 generated 384-dimensional tile embeddings without task-specific fine-tuning. Gated multi-head attention MIL used class-specific attention heads 39 , 40 . Models were trained with AdamW, a learning rate of 10 − 5 10^{-5} , 10 epochs of linear warm-up, and cosine decay to 10 − 6 10^{-6} over 50 epochs. S1 and S2 processed one case at a time and computed case-level losses, whereas S3 and its random grouped-training control summed case-level losses across group members before gradient accumulation and optimizer updates. Detailed regime-specific loss aggregation, epoch-exposure formulas, and group caps are provided in Supplementary Method 1.1 Grouped training and epoch exposure . Evaluation and statistical analysis Performance was estimated using 10-fold MCCV, with an 80/20 train-validation split per fold. Sex and self-reported race used patient-level splits, whereas age group used case-level splits to account for age variation across encounters. Evaluation was performed at the case level. All models were evaluated at the final training epoch (epoch 50). The primary metric was macro one-vs-rest area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC), reported as mean ± \pm standard deviation (SD) across folds. Regime comparisons emphasized paired AUROC differences and their consistency across organs and folds. Paired t t -tests were performed across matched MCCV folds, with Benjamini–Hochberg (BH) adjustment applied separately within each demographic target and regime comparison. Statistical results are provided in Supplementary Table S10 . As an additional evaluation analysis, the existing S1 and S2 predictions were evaluated within diagnosis-text clusters using the same matched validation cases as pooled evaluation. Within-cluster AUROCs were aggregated using case-count weighting and compared with pooled AUROC. This analysis introduced neither a new training regime nor a prediction target and was distinct from diagnosis-context-constrained S3 training. Clusters containing fewer than two target classes were excluded. The same evaluation procedure was applied across all organ and demographic combinations, including the breast case study. Detailed definitions and eligibility criteria are provided in Supplementary Method 1.2 Cluster-stratified evaluation . Literature searching and hypothesis generation Targeted large language model (LLM)-assisted literature searching was conducted for the three selected organ–demographic combinations to identify potential histomorphologic and clinical explanations for residual demographic predictability. Using OpenAI gpt-6-astra (accessed 24 September 2026), a standardized prompt was used to identify relevant studies, including supporting, contradictory, and null findings. Candidate explanations were checked in a separate model-assisted pass against retrieved abstracts or accessible full texts for citation accuracy, evidence support, and applicability to the study population and tissue context. The resulting literature-supported hypotheses were summarized to guide subsequent morphologic investigation. The search protocol and source verification procedures are provided in Supplementary Method 1.3 LLM-assisted literature searching and hypothesis generation . Literature-guided hypotheses for selected signals We developed the public SAIF Hypothesis Explorer to organize candidate explanations and supporting, contradictory, and limiting literature, and to facilitate further investigation and community discussion. Candidates were generated through targeted LLM-assisted literature searching followed by source checking ( Literature searching and hypothesis generation ). Sex prediction in gastric tissue, age-group prediction in breast, and prediction of self-reported race in skin received detailed attention because of their substantial residual performance and value as representative settings for further histomorphological investigation. For sex prediction in gastric tissue, selected gastric cancer cohorts support sex differences in subtype composition 42 , 43 , while studies of Helicobacter pylori -associated and benign gastric disease suggest context-dependent mucosal differences related to infection, subsite, and eradication history 44 , 45 . These findings identify cancer subtype composition and mucosal disease context as candidate explanations for the retained signal. For age-group prediction in breast, retained performance motivated review of age-associated tissue changes. Terminal duct lobular unit (TDLU) involution 46 , 47 and H&E morphometry 48 support differences in epithelial cellularity and stromal/adipose composition as candidate correlates. Reported associations between parity and lobular measures indicate that such features are not specific to chronological age 49 . These observations are cross-sectional and do not establish that the model uses these features. For prediction of self-reported race in skin, substantial retained predictability motivated investigation of both tissue and sampling context. Epidermal pigmentation emerged as a candidate tissue correlate, supported by studies of epidermal melanin and melanosome distribution 50 , 51 . Some supporting evidence used modalities or population definitions that do not map directly to routine H&E or the categories used here; anatomical-site and exposure differences remain contextual alternatives, and measured ultraviolet sensitivity has been reported to predict photodamage better than racial or ethnic origin 52 . Differential biopsy practice is a further contextual candidate: within psoriasis, biopsy frequency has been reported to differ by race and ethnicity 53 , and selected psoriasis biopsy series document a broad histological spectrum 54 , so diagnosis- and indication-specific sampling could alter the lesion mix across label groups. 4 Discussion This study systematically mapped demographic-associated information recoverable from routine clinical histopathology across 25 organs and 69 organ–demographic combinations. Using deep learning models as analytical tools, we found that information associated with sex, age group, and self-reported race was broadly detectable, although its recoverability varied substantially across organs and demographic attributes. Demographic-class balancing produced relatively small changes in model performance, and additional diagnosis-context analyses showed modest attenuation while leaving substantial signals in multiple tissues. Together, these findings establish a pan-organ reference for investigating demographic-associated variation in histopathology and its implications for computational pathology research. Demographic-associated information in clinical histopathology may reflect differences in demographic representation and diagnostic composition, in addition to tissue-associated characteristics. To examine the sensitivity of the observed signals to these factors, we compared observed-distribution training (S1) with demographic-balanced training (S2), which produced relatively small changes in performance. Evaluation within diagnosis-text clusters further showed modest attenuation relative to pooled evaluation. Under grouped training, introducing diagnosis-context constraints (S3) yielded additional, generally modest attenuation compared with random grouping without these constraints (S3 RND {}_{\text{RND}} ). Notably, substantial demographic-associated signals remained across multiple organs under the evaluated controls, motivating further investigation of tissue-associated information and clinical or contextual factors not captured by these analyses. The heterogeneity of demographic-associated information across organs raises questions about the histological and contextual features underlying these signals. Such information may be localized to specific tissue compartments, reflected in cellular morphology, or distributed across broader tissue regions. To facilitate further investigation, we selected three representative settings—sex in gastric tissue, age group in breast, and self-reported race in skin—and used literature-guided searching to identify plausible histomorphological and clinical correlates. Our previous dermatopathology study, in which attention and ablation analyses highlighted the epidermis as an important region for self-reported-race classification, illustrates how spatial analyses can help investigate candidate sources of information 27 . The hypotheses assembled here provide starting points for targeted pathology review, quantitative feature measurement, and diagnosis-matched analyses, while inviting further investigation of alternative explanations and previously unrecognized tissue-associated patterns. The widespread availability of demographic-associated information in histopathology has implications for computational pathology model development and clinical translation. Such information may provide useful context for certain clinical tasks, but it may also become a source of shortcut learning when models exploit demographic correlations that do not generalize across populations or care settings 28 , 29 . Whether these associations are informative or problematic depends on the intended task, the model’s use of the information, and the population in which it is applied. Our findings therefore support more systematic characterization of patient populations and demographic subgroup performance during model development and evaluation 26 , 31 . Where data permit, studies should report performance across relevant demographic groups, including self-reported race, sex, and age. At a minimum, clear reporting of cohort demographic composition and population restrictions can help researchers assess the applicability of a model to new clinical settings. The pan-organ map presented here may help prioritize organ-specific investigations, subgroup validation, and assessments of potential demographic shortcuts in downstream pathology models. Several considerations remain important for interpreting these findings. Self-reported race may serve as an imperfect proxy for genetic ancestry while also reflecting social, environmental, and healthcare-related factors. Future studies incorporating ancestry-informed measures could provide complementary perspectives on the sources of demographic-associated information in histopathology. The single-health-system cohort provided a relatively consistent clinical and imaging environment, although independent cohorts will be important for evaluating the reproducibility of organ-specific patterns. Diagnosis-text clustering captured broad diagnostic contexts but could not fully account for disease heterogeneity, clinical history, sampling practices, or other potential confounders. The present study also did not directly localize the histological features underlying the observed signals. Future investigations combining more granular clinical stratification with tissue-region, tile-level, and cellular analyses may help identify the morphological and contextual sources of demographic-associated information. By providing a systematic map and literature-informed candidate hypotheses, this study offers a foundation for further investigation of these signals and their relevance to computational pathology. Supplementary Methods 1.1 Grouped training and epoch exposure Grouped balancing for S3 and S3 RND {}_{\text{RND}} operated on unique pathology cases, with each case counted once in the candidate training pool. Within a grouping pool p p , let n p ​ g n_{pg} denote the number of cases in demographic class g g . The balancing target was the largest available class count, n p max = max g ∈ { 1 , … , G } ⁡ n p ​ g n_{p}^{\max}=\max_{g\in\{1,\ldots,G\}}n_{pg} . A class was retained only when n p max / n p ​ g ≤ 50 n_{p}^{\max}/n_{pg}\leq 50 ; classes requiring greater oversampling were dropped, and a pool with fewer than two retained classes was excluded from group construction. Classes below n p max n_{p}^{\max} were oversampled by cycling through shuffled case lists to that target; the largest class was retained in full, without subsampling majority-class cases. The balanced class lists were assembled into training groups containing one case from each of the G G retained demographic classes. Each case was then represented by its full case-level MIL bag. S3 and its random grouped-training control used this formulation. S3 RND {}_{\text{RND}} applied grouped demographic balancing once to the full, undifferentiated training pool; it did not use diagnosis clusters or randomized cluster assignments and served as the non-semantic global grouped-balancing control. S3 applied the same formulation separately within the diagnosis-context clusters defined above. Clusters with fewer than two retained demographic classes were excluded, and groups from the retained clusters were combined for training. S3 RND {}_{\text{RND}} is reported as a supporting control to S3. S1 and S2 processed cases individually and computed loss case by case. Each S3 or S3 RND {}_{\text{RND}} training example was a demographic group; the case-level cross-entropy losses were summed across group members before gradient accumulation and the optimizer step.
