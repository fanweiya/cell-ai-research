1 A Geometry-Guided Feature-Space Framework for Interpretable Unsupervised 4D-STEM Analysis of Complex Multiphase Materials Arda Genc1*, Daniel A. Bolden2, Albert Nguyen3, Daniel S. Gianola1, Ravit Silverstein2* 1 Materials Department, University of California Santa Barbara, Santa Barbara, CA, USA 2 Department of Materials Science and Engineering, University of Florida, Gainesville, FL, USA 3 Computer Science Department, University of California Santa Barbara, Santa Barbara, CA, USA *Corresponding authors. ardagenc@ucsb.edu, rsilverstein@ufl.edu Abstract Rapid, high-fidelity analysis of four-dimensional scanning transmission electron microscopy (4D-STEM) datasets from heterogeneous materials remains challenging because coexisting phases, orientation variants, local strain gradients, and intrinsic TEM thin-foil effects produce complex, overlapping diffraction signatures. Here, we present a machine-learning (ML) framework that combines physics-informed geometric descriptors with weighted high-dimensional embeddings to identify latent structural patterns with minimal prior knowledge. By constructing a regularized descriptor space before embedding and density-based clustering, the method improves cluster stability, suppresses noise-driven fragmentation, and reduces sensitivity to embedding hyperparameters. Spatial weighting produced the largest stability gain, increasing median cluster persistence from 0.04 to 0.55 while reducing the noise fraction from approximately 22% to 0%. Rotation weighting increased DBCV from 0.80 to 0.92, although the noise fraction rose from 0.2% to 1.0%, highlighting a trade-off between improved density separation and noise rejection. We validate the framework on two benchmark systems: Si/ SixGe1-x epitaxial layers with continuous lattice variation and deformation, and multiphase Ti–Nb alloys containing distinct phases and orientation variants. Comparisons with a pretrained vanilla autoencoder and model-free pattern vectorization show that the physics-informed geometric descriptor provides higher cluster fidelity, greater interpretability, and substantially lower dimensionality. Introduction 4D-STEM generates vast, information-rich datasets, driven by rapid advances in high-throughput detector architectures 1. In this modality, the electron beam records a series of diffraction patterns that encode highly correlated information about a material’s phase, deformation, and orientation structure 2–6. Data volume increases further in 5D-STEM with the addition of a temporal axis, resulting in terabytes of memory usage per user session. The key challenge is no longer data acquisition, but rapidly and reliably navigating these complex datasets and transforming them into physics- informed, readily interpretable visualizations and analyses necessary to derive deep materials inferences 1. Current analysis pipelines remain highly parameterized, sequentially complex, computationally demanding, and domain-specific, and they often fail to resolve the latent relationships that govern material behavior. For example, the widely used template-matching approach for phase and orientation analysis requires prior crystallographic models and extensive template libraries, which become increasingly difficult to construct and search for complex multiphase systems, orientation variants, strained regions, or previously unknown phases 5,7,8. Several 4D-STEM workflows use unsupervised ML and clustering to improve analysis while preserving the important details of large diffraction datasets 9–14. Prior studies have combined clustering with principal component analysis (PCA) for linear dimensionality reduction, while others employ uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) for nonlinear embedding and visualization 9–18. Notable examples include latent-space mapping of atomically-resolved electric fields in Si-doped graphene from atomic-resolution STEM data 12 and a high- dimensional diffraction-vector workflow that uses guided multi-stage clustering to delineate phase, rotation, and bend contour effects in 2D materials and relatively thin TEM samples 11,17,18. In more complex, thicker TEM samples, clustering robustness is often improved through preprocessing steps. Common strategies include masking to suppress the dominant central beam or Fourier-transform-based treatments to reduce tilt and thickness effects before flattening diffraction patterns into high-dimensional 2 intensity descriptors 14. More recently, energy-dispersive spectroscopy chemical maps have been incorporated to enable multimodal clustering approaches 19–22. In 4D-STEM, physically meaningful analysis requires a structured representation that preserves both diffraction- pattern signatures and their spatial context before applying neighborhood-based learning, manifold embedding, and clustering 9,23,24. The goal is not merely dimensionality reduction but organizing diffraction-derived descriptors into a form that makes interpretation easier and robust. A central challenge in analyzing these large, information- rich datasets is the curse of dimensionality. For example, a single 128x128-pixel diffraction pattern can be flattened into a 16,384-dimensional feature vector, where each dimension corresponds to the intensity recorded at a single detector pixel. As the dimensionality of the feature spaces expands, as in raw diffraction pattern representations, the data become increasingly sparse, local neighborhoods lose contrast, and the distinction between near and far points weakens (Aggarwal et al., 2001; Beyer Kevin and Goldstein, 1999; Donoho, 2000). These effects reduce the effectiveness of distance-based similarity measures and nearest-neighbor searches, increase sampling requirements, and make it more difficult to construct stable and physically interpretable low-dimensional representations of the data 19,28. At the same time, many high-dimensional datasets contain fingerprint information that can be represented in far fewer dimensions than their raw measurements suggest 29. These limitations motivate an approach in which feature-space construction is treated as a primary part of the analysis rather than as a preprocessing detail. In this work, we present a feature-engineering-focused machine learning framework for 4D-STEM analysis that emphasizes deliberate construction of the descriptor space before embedding and clustering. By using feature extraction to control dimensionality, the framework accelerates interpretation. It enables robust, physics- informed analysis of complex structural materials without labeled training data or prior phase assignments. Our central premise is that the main challenge in unsupervised 4D-STEM cluster analysis is not only selecting an embedding or clustering algorithm but also designing and regularizing an interpretable feature space that preserves the underlying crystallographic relationships within the data. While existing workflows often emphasize diffraction-pattern preprocessing, black-box approaches to feature extraction, such as autoencoders, or downstream dimensionality reduction and clustering, comparatively less attention has been devoted to the physics-informed design and regularization of diffraction feature representations that preserve crystallographically meaningful information 9–16,19– 21,28,30. Feature-space construction and embedding The workflow begins by constructing a physically interpretable feature space, referred to here as physics- informed descriptors (PIDs), from diffraction-disc centers detected by our neural-network object-detection model (Fig. 1) 31. Before disc detection, each diffraction pattern is log-normalized and processed using Fourier-transform- based autocorrelation. This autocorrelation step reduces the influence of thickness- and tilt-related dynamical effects, similar to the robustness sought in cepstrum-based diffraction representations and improves the Friedel symmetry of the patterns 14,31,32. The resulting descriptor representation is then passed to unsupervised embedding and clustering. As shown in Fig. 1, each diffraction pattern is encoded using canonical triplet invariants, in-plane lattice rotation, and 2D scan coordinates. These descriptors are scaled and embedded before hierarchical density-based clustering assigns labels for 2D spatial mapping and 3D visualization. For 4D-STEM datasets, the latent space is defined by three coupled approaches: the selected diffraction features, their relative weighting, and the embedding used before density-based clustering. Feature selection determines which physical distinctions the clustering can capture. While raw diffraction intensities preserve the full signal, they are dominated by the central beam and are sensitive to background variation and detector noise. In thicker samples, the background contribution is even larger, potentially overwhelming the clustering algorithm unless additional preprocessing is applied 11,19,24,28. In contrast, hand-crafted descriptors such as canonical triplets of Bragg vectors — together with explicit in-plane rotation and spatial-coordinate channels — compress each pattern into a compact fingerprint, rather than entangling them as in pixel-wise intensity representations. Spatial context is widely used in computer vision segmentation tasks to preserve image structure and geometry, including in unsupervised ML frameworks such as SLIC superpixels and autoencoders 33–35. Because feature descriptors combine heterogeneous quantities, including reciprocal-space lengths, angles, and scan positions, they must be brought onto a common scale before any distance metric can be interpreted. Per-channel z-scoring removes unit imbalance, while explicit channel weights encode the relative importance of lattice geometry, rotation, and spatial coherence in the final metric. This weighted representation provides the foundation for the manifold embedding and clustering steps that follow. To address this, the weighted feature space is embedded into a manifold that preserves local neighborhood structure while making high-density regions more accessible to the clustering algorithm. In this pipeline, feature design determines which physical attributes are represented, scaling controls the relative contribution of each channel, and embedding sets the effective resolution at which hierarchical density-based spatial clustering with noise (HDBSCAN) resolves structure 18. Together, these steps enable pixel-resolved phase, rotation, and lattice deformation mapping from otherwise unlabeled diffraction data without requiring prior phase assignments. Results and Discussion Strain and lattice analysis of pseudomorphic Si/ SixGe1-x epitaxial layers To evaluate the fidelity of our structural analysis workflow, we used a Si/SixGe1-x epitaxial-multilayer TEM sample (dataset I) in 4D-STEM analysis. This traceable standard consists of alternating pure Si and SixGe1-x alloy layers deposited on a single-crystal Si substrate. It contains five SixGe1-x layers, each about 10 nm thick, separated by pure Si layers about 13 nm thick, for a total superlattice thickness of approximately 100 nm. The alternating Si and SixGe1-x layers produce the light and dark contrast seen in the virtual bright-field (vBF) image in Fig. 4(a). The TEM foil thickness was approximately 70 nm, as determined by electron-energy-loss spectroscopy (EELS). We acquired XEDS-STEM profiles across the multilayer stack and measured the average Si-Ge composition using the Ge K- line at 9.874 keV and the Si K-line at 1.739 keV. The layers contained approximately 87 at. % Si (x = 87) and 13 at. % Ge. From this composition, we estimated a maximum expected out-of-plane (growth direction) strain, 𝜀⊥ , of about 0.85% using the approach of Munshi et al. 43. The strain maps 𝜀⊥and 𝜀∥ in Fig. 4(b) and (c), respectively, represent the out-of-plane and in-plane (parallel to interface) strain fields, respectively, referenced to the two reciprocal-space directions used in the analysis, corresponding to the ⟨100⟩ and ⟨110⟩ reflections. The strain fields were calculated by tracking diffraction-disc centers with the center-detection model and measuring their displacements relative to a strain-free region in the Si substrate using polar decomposition (Supplementary material: polar decomposition algorithm). Oscillations in the 𝜀⊥map indicate tensile strain along the growth direction, with a mean variation of approximately 1%, in good agreement with the expected theoretical maximum for the Si87Ge13 layers. This behavior reflects an inherent limitation of the clustering approach, which assumes that clusters occupy high-density regions separated by lower-density gaps. In 7 this case, the continuous deformation linking the bend contour to the Si87Ge13 layers forms a smooth gradient without well-defined boundaries, thereby precluding distinct cluster partitioning. Two principal UMAP hyperparameters are the number of neighbors and the minimum distance. The number of neighbors sets the local neighborhood size used to construct the fuzzy graph, whereas the minimum distance controls how tightly points may pack in the low- dimensional embedding. In this study, the number of neighbors was set to 100 and the minimum distance to 0.15 based on empirical optimization, as these values produced a stable manifold (Supplementary Fig 1) with well-defined boundaries. For the weighted embedding, the spatial weight was set to 0.03, imposing a modest spatial prior. The weighted clustering resolves the lattice difference between the Si and Si87Ge13 layers and identifies the regions strained by bending. These bend contours are also visible in the virtual bright-field image as a broad alternating band of high and low intensities (Fig. 4(a)) (Supplementary Fig. 4(a-d)). Bending of the thin TEM foil is also evident in the strain maps. In the 𝜀∥ map, Fig. 4(c), it produces continuous strain modulation of about 0.1–0.2% along the nominally strain-free ⟨110⟩ direction in the Si substrate. The spatial cluster map complements the strain analysis by providing structural context, reducing the risk of misinterpreting bending-related effects. Fig. 4(d–g) illustrates how feature-space weighting shapes the UMAP-fuzzy graph. With spatial weighting in the 6D embedding (Fig. 4d, e), the manifold remains well defined, and clustering resolves deformation and lattice variation. In the absence of this spatial prior in the 4D embedding (Fig. 4 (f, g)), the structure fragments into noise, evident as gray over-fragmentation in the 3D projection and dark noise pixels in the spatial cluster map, leaving sparse, weakly connected regions that HDBSCAN predominantly classifies as noise. These results show that weighting reshapes the UMAP topology and facilitates k-nearest neighbors (kNN) graph construction by reorganizing local neighborhoods and rescaling feature axes in high- dimensional space. As a result, HDBSCAN identifies more stable clusters. To assess embedding behavior across the UMAP hyperparameter space, we examined 3D projections generated without any spatial regularization; these showed greater noise, weaker cluster boundaries, and reduced stability (Supplementary Fig. 2). We then evaluated the effect of spatial channel weighting by varying it over four orders of magnitude for the Si/Si87Ge13 epitaxial layers (dataset I; Table 2). Cluster stability was quantified using two complementary metrics: median per-cluster persistence (P̃) from the HDBSCAN condensed-tree hierarchy and the density-based cluster validity (DBCV) score 44, both defined in the methods section. At wp = 0, the geometric descriptor produced 14 candidate clusters with P̃ near the noise floor (0.04), indicating over- fragmentation from per-pixel descriptor noise, with approximately 22% of pixels classified as noise, consistent with Fig. 4(f, g). A modest regularization, wp = 0.03, consolidated these into five density-supported clusters (P̃ 0.55, DBCV 0.44, noise 0%) while preserving fine continuous deformation features such as crystal bending, as shown in Fig. 4(d, e). As the spatial weight increased, these features were progressively absorbed into compact, spatially localized clusters, with a corresponding loss of manifold detail (Supplementary Fig. 3c–d). Phase and rotation analysis in multiphase metallic alloy We next evaluated our workflow on the more challenging task of resolving complex heterostructure in a Ti-50Nb metallic alloy (dataset II). Refractory alloys often exhibit intricate chemical variations and associated multiphase distributions together with crystallographic variants and in- plane rotations 46. The spatial cluster map in Fig. 5(a) provides a phase-level interpretation of dataset II. Clustering is governed primarily by the rotation-invariant geometric descriptor and spatial coherence; therefore, variants with similar reduced-cell geometry remain grouped even when they differ in in- plane orientation. The orange background corresponds to the body-centered-cubic (BCC) β-Ti matrix viewed along the [110]β direction., while the red and blue precipitate regions correspond to hexagonal-closed-pack (α-HCP) and orthorhombic O/α″ domains (distinguished by the level of tetragonal distortion of the BCC phase), respectively. In the 3D projection of the embedding in Fig. 5(b), these precipitate-related clusters are separated mainly by differences in lattice metric in Ti-based alloys 47–50. Cluster 4 traces the β/α″ phase boundaries: it inherits the 𝛹2 orientation of Cluster 0 but carries a distorted metric (α = 46.6°) arising from the double diffraction effect at the overlapping interfaces and is not a crystallographic distinct phase. The observed phase geometry is consistent with the orientation relationship [110]β // [001]α″ // [0001]α-HCP, which places the β matrix, orthorhombic α″ martensite, and α-HCP phase along aligned zone axes and provides a crystallographic basis for the variant selection observed in the cluster map. α‑HCP (Cluster 3) appears as elongated laths localized almost entirely within the lower β‑twin domain (Cluster 2), indicating preferential nucleation on one twin variant rather than uniform distribution — a variant‑selection effect consistent with a fixed β→α orientation relationship. Here, UMAP parameters matched dataset I (100 neighbors, minimum distance 0.15); rotation and spatial weights were 𝑤𝑟 = 0.25 and wp = 0.03. A one‑way ANOVA across clusters ranks the descriptor channels by discriminability (Fig. 6 c,d). The lattice‑metric edges and the 𝛹2 channels dominate — s₁ (F = 24640, η² = 0.97), s₃ (F = 24131, η² = 0.97), Im 𝛹2 (F = 21962, η² = 0.97), s₂ (F = 12914, η² = 0.95) and Re 𝛹2 (F = 11964, η² = 0.95) — confirming that metric and dominant two‑fold orientation separate the β and α″ families. The 𝛹6 channels rank lowest overall (Re 𝛹6: F = 1350, η² = 0.67; Im 𝛹6: F = 942, η² = 0.58) because only one cluster is six‑fold coherent: they are the sole channels that isolate the α‑HCP grain (Cluster 3), which is otherwise metric‑ and 𝛹2‑ambiguous. Global channel importance and phase‑resolving power are therefore distinct — a low‑variance channel can still be indispensable for a minority phase, which is precisely the advantage of retaining multiple coherence‑gated orders. Complex orientational order provides a direct check that the 𝛹2 and 𝛹6 channels encode meaningful in-plane symmetries. The cluster‑average orientation θ and coherence r (Fig. 7b) make the variant structure explicit. We interpret wᵣ as an angular‑resolution control, with the weight required to resolve two metric‑identical variants scaling approximately as 𝑤𝑟 ∝ 1/Δθ. The dominant β twin (Δθ ≈ 12 72° in‑plane) is resolved at the lowest weight, where DBCV peaks (𝑤𝑟 = 0.03). Small precipitate rotations require more weight: Clusters 0 and 7 are both α″ with an identical cell (s ≈ 0.41, 0.27, 0.25 nm; α ≈ 83°) and differ by only ≈ 13° in 𝛹2 orientation, so they merge into a single mode for 𝑤𝑟 < 0.25 and separate into distinct density peaks only at 𝑤𝑟 = 0.25. The rotation weight should therefore be chosen according to the angular scale of the feature of interest — coarse textures at low weight, few‑degree orientation variants at higher weight — up to the point where noise degrades the partition ( 𝑤𝑟 ≳ 0.3). The two datasets, dataset I (Si/ Si87Ge13) and dataset II (Ti- Nb multiphase alloy), pose fundamentally different clustering problems due to their intrinsic structures. Dataset I is dominated by continuous, non-linear lattice deformation: the per-pixel descriptors form a single, connected manifold with no intrinsic density gaps, so there is no discrete set of classes to be recovered. Clustering here is therefore better understood as a coarse-graining of a continuum than as class discovery, where the spatial weight 𝑤 𝑝 acts as a granularity control, trading a finer, manifold-preserving partition for a coarser set of spatially compact regions, with the operating point chosen to match the length scale of interest. dataset II, by contrast, contains discrete phases together with twin and precipitate variants that occupy genuinely distinct, well-separated density modes. Here, clustering is well-posed and recovers physically meaningful classes: a small rotation weight (𝑤𝑟 = 0.03) suffices to separate the dominant twin variant (~72° misorientation) while keeping the cluster density stable, whereas excessive weighting over-fragments the map. The same descriptor-and-HDBSCAN pipeline thus behaves very differently across datasets: in dataset I, it segments a continuous deformation field, while in dataset II, it resolves discrete phase and rotation variants. Comparison of descriptors Using dataset II, we benchmarked the geometric descriptor against two data-driven alternatives—a pretrained convolutional-autoencoder and a model-free flattened descriptor—using the same UMAP→HDBSCAN pipeline. The 130-dimensional autoencoder resolved the dominant twin orientation by splitting the cluster into two low-density clusters, as shown in the 3D projection in Fig. 8 (c) (DBCV = -0.46, 𝑃̃ = 0.31) and in Table 4. The HDBSCAN condensed trees in Fig. 8 (b) and Fig. 8(d) highlight this distinction: the autoencoder and model-free descriptors produce only a few low-density modes within an otherwise continuous manifold. Additionally, we plan to curate data to expand the training datasets to include diffraction patterns acquired over a broader range of camera lengths, spot spacing, pattern geometry, and intensity distributions for both autoencoder and neural network object detection models. 14 Conclusion In this work, we present a parallelized post-processing workflow for large-scale 4D-STEM datasets that combines a neural network-based object detection model for feature extraction with feature-regularized unsupervised ML clustering. The framework enables rapid classification of diffraction features and supports accurate crystallographic analysis (assigning both phase and crystal orientation) without labeled training data or prior phase assignments. This approach proved to be successful in disentangling the various components of a complex and multiphase alloy with nanoscale features and double-diffraction effects. By accelerating interpretation across varied experimental conditions, this approach can help streamline materials discovery and understanding in systems ranging from semiconductors to metallic alloys. HDBSCAN condense tree visualization Phase clusters were identified with HDBSCAN and visualized using its condensed cluster tree. In this representation, the vertical axis is the density parameter λ = 1/dmr, where d is the mutual-reachability distance; larger λ corresponds to higher density (smaller distance scale). Each branch ("icicle") denotes a candidate cluster, and its width represents the number of constituent points at the given λ. As λ increases, points progressively drop out of a cluster until it either splits into child clusters (when both children meet the minimum cluster size) or dissolves into noise; the persistence of a branch over a range of λ defines its stability. Branch color encodes cluster size as log (number of points), a logarithmic scale used so that clusters spanning several orders of magnitude in population remain distinguishable. The clusters retained by HDBSCAN's excess-of-mass selection are outlined and assigned the phase color palette used throughout in the spatial cluster maps. As a learned alternative to the hand-crafted descriptors, we used a convolutional autoencoder as a fixed feature extractor. The network is a four-stage stride-convolution encoder (32→64→128→256 channels, each Conv– BatchNorm–ReLU) feeding a 128D-channel bottleneck, mirrored by a transposed-convolution decoder; it was pretrained self-supervised (reconstruction loss) on 99 diffraction patterns — the same image domain used to train the diffraction peak center detector (neural network object detection model)—. The convolutional autoencoder was trained to minimize MSE reconstruction loss using the AdamW optimizer with an initial learning rate of 0.001, a weight decay of 0.0001, and a cosine annealing learning- rate scheduler. The model was trained for a maximum of 60 epochs with a batch size of 8. A 5% validation split and early stopping with a patience of 20 epochs were used to 16 mitigate overfitting. The checkpoint with the lowest validation loss was retained for downstream analysis. Its weights were held fixed during clustering. At inference, each scan position's diffraction pattern was converted to its log-autocorrelation, per-image min–max normalized to [0,1], and contrast-inverted to match the polarity of the training images (bright background, dark peaks), then passed through the encoder. The bottleneck feature map was globally average-pooled and L2-normalized to yield a 128-dimensional embedding per pixel. As with the geometric descriptor, the two scan-position channels were then concatenated, yielding a 130-dimensional feature vector that was fed into the common pipeline (per-channel z-scoring, UMAP, HDBSCAN). Model-free descriptor As a model-free baseline to the geometric descriptor, each scan position was represented by its diffraction pattern autocorrelation, computed as the inverse Fourier transform of the power spectrum (Wiener–Khinchin), normalized by its central peak, and log-compressed. Each autocorrelation was area-downsampled to N×N pixels (N = 128), its central self-correlation peak excised with a circular mask of radius f·(N/2) (f = 0.1), and the retained pixels were max-normalized per pattern and flattened into a feature vector. These vectors were then fed to the common pipeline (per-channel z-scoring, UMAP, HDBSCAN). This descriptor is translation-invariant but rotation-variant and is therefore sensitive to in-plane lattice rotation (twin/orientation variants) as well as lattice spacing.
