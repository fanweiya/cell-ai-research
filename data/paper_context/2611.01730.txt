3 Review Methodology We used a structured search and screening process; this is a narrative survey rather than a full systematic review or meta-analysis. We searched PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, IEEE Xplore, and arXiv from January 2010 to October 2026. The search combined cervical cytology terms (“cervical cytology”, “Pap smear”, “liquid-based cytology”, “ThinPrep”, “TCT”), method terms (“deep learning”, “convolutional neural network”, “transformer”, “foundation model”, “multiple-instance learning”, “self-supervised”), and task terms (“segmentation”, “classification”, “detection”, “whole-slide image”). We included peer-reviewed articles and preprints that (i) addressed cervical cytology image analysis, (ii) reported a quantitative evaluation on cytology data, and (iii) described a method, dataset, or benchmark relevant to at least one data scale (cell, patch, or WSI). We excluded records that (i) were not cytology-based (colposcopy, histopathology, or HPV testing without a cytology component), (ii) were of low quality (no quantitative evaluation or insufficient methodological detail), or (iii) were reviews without primary data. English-language publications were included; regulatory documents were retrieved separately from the FDA and NMPA databases. One author (Y.J.) screened all titles and abstracts and assessed records with sufficient textual information in full text. Data were extracted for study design, data scale, task, dataset, supervision strategy, evaluation metrics, external validation, and code availability. Risk of bias was assessed by the same author with QUADAS-2 for diagnostic accuracy studies and CLAIM for AI studies; reporting completeness was checked against TRIPOD-AI where applicable. Because study designs, datasets, and metrics were heterogeneous, evidence was synthesized qualitatively; no meta-analysis was performed. The search and screening flow is summarized in Table 1 . Of 163 records identified, one duplicate was removed, leaving 162 records for title/abstract screening. Twenty records were excluded at this stage because they lacked sufficient textual information or were clearly out of scope. The remaining 142 records were assessed in full text; 9 were excluded as non-cytology, 5 as low quality, and 3 because the full text could not be obtained. The final corpus comprised 125 records (primary studies, datasets, and regulatory documents) for qualitative synthesis. Single-author screening and quality assessment are limitations of this review. We propose a hierarchical, three-axis taxonomy for cervical cytology screening methods. The primary axis is data scale , reflecting the spatial granularity at which analysis is performed; the secondary axis is functional category , describing the computational task; the third axis is supervision , describing the label regime required for training. This taxonomy captures the technical evolution of the field, the clinical diagnostic hierarchy, and the annotation economics that determine deployability. Data Scale Axis (Primary): 1. Cell-level: Analysis of individual cervical cells (typically 64 × \times 64 to 128 × \times 128 pixel crops). Tasks include nucleus and cytoplasm segmentation, morphological feature extraction, and cell-level classification into Bethesda categories. Input scale: single cells. Clinical application: differential diagnosis at cellular resolution. 2. Patch-level: Analysis of image regions containing multiple cells (typically 256 × \times 256 to 1024 × \times 1024 pixels). Tasks include instance segmentation (delineating and classifying each cell within the patch) and object detection (localizing and classifying abnormal cells via bounding boxes). Input scale: multi-cell image regions. Clinical application: regional abnormality screening where cell context and spatial relationships matter. 3. WSI-level: Analysis of gigapixel whole-slide images. Tasks include slide-level abnormality classification, risk stratification, and lesion localization through aggregation of cell-level or patch-level predictions, or through end-to-end slide-level models. Input scale: 15,000 × \times 15,000 pixels or larger. Clinical application: slide-level triage and diagnostic decision support. Functional Category Axis (Secondary): • Segmentation: Delineation of nucleus and cytoplasm boundaries. Methods span classical approaches (watershed, active contours, graph cuts) to deep learning (U-Net, FCN, Mask R-CNN, Transformer- and Mamba-based architectures). • Feature Extraction: Derivation of discriminative representations. Categories include handcrafted features (morphological: area, perimeter, eccentricity; textural: GLCM, LBP, Haralick; chromatic: brightness, color moments), deep features extracted from pretrained CNNs (VGG, ResNet, Inception), and hybrid features fusing both paradigms. • Classification: Assignment of cells or patches to Bethesda diagnostic categories. Classical classifiers (SVM, ANN, random forest) operate on extracted features, while deep classifiers (CNN, ViT, ensemble methods) learn end-to-end from raw images. • Detection: Simultaneous localization and classification of cells. Approaches include two-stage detectors (Faster R-CNN, Cascade R-CNN), one-stage detectors (SSD, YOLO, RetinaNet), and query-based detectors (DETR variants). • Quality Control and ROI Selection: Detection of unsatisfactory slides, artifacts, staining problems, and selection of diagnostically relevant regions before or alongside diagnosis. This function is often omitted from benchmarks but is critical in deployment. • Report Generation and Human-AI Interaction: Structured diagnostic reports, confidence and uncertainty estimates, and review queues that integrate model outputs into cytopathologist workflow. Supervision Axis (Third): Methods also differ in their label requirements: fully supervised, weakly supervised (slide-level labels), semi-supervised (small labeled plus large unlabeled sets), self-supervised (pretraining without labels), few-shot/zero-shot, and active learning. This axis is clinically decisive because annotation cost, rather than architecture, often determines whether a method can be deployed. We therefore report the supervision regime for each method family in Sections 6 – 8 and synthesize the paradigms in Section 9 . The four processing pipelines are illustrated in Fig. 4 . The field has evolved through four technical generations. The key technical transition from each generation to the next is driven by a reduction in manual engineering: Generation 1 relies on handcrafted features and classical classifiers; Generation 2 replaces individual pipeline stages with deep networks; Generation 3 eliminates the explicit segmentation step through end-to-end detection; Generation 4 introduces foundation model pretraining and label-efficient learning to address annotation scarcity and domain shift. Fig. 5 provides a detailed functional taxonomy of deep learning methods. It focuses on the four core computational branches; classical segmentation and classification methods are discussed in the text but omitted from the figure, and quality control and report generation are workflow functions discussed in Section 4.1 . Segmentation methods have evolved from classical approaches (watershed, level sets, graph cuts) through deep encoder-decoder architectures (U-Net, FCN, DeepLab) to attention-enhanced and state-space models. Feature extraction has progressed from manual engineering of 29-dimensional morphometric vectors to deep features from VGG, ResNet, and Inception families, and more recently to self-supervised representations. Classification has advanced from SVM and ANN operating on handcrafted features to CNN ensembles achieving over 99% accuracy on curated single-cell benchmarks. Detection has transitioned through two-stage (Faster R-CNN, Cascade R-CNN), one-stage (SSD, YOLO, RetinaNet), and query-based (DETR) paradigms. The taxonomy is structured in three tiers. Tier 1 (Cell-level): Segmentation methods (classical and deep learning) extract nucleus and cytoplasm boundaries; feature extraction methods derive discriminative representations; classification methods assign Bethesda categories. Tier 2 (Patch-level): Instance segmentation methods simultaneously segment and classify each cell within a patch; object detection methods localize and classify cells via bounding boxes, using two-stage (Faster R-CNN, Cascade R-CNN), one-stage (SSD, YOLO, RetinaNet), or query-based (DETR) architectures. Tier 3 (WSI-level): WSI confidence methods aggregate patch-level or cell-level prediction scores to produce slide-level diagnoses; cell detection methods perform end-to-end detection across the entire slide, providing per-cell localization and classification. This taxonomy reflects both the increasing data scale and the clinical workflow: pathologists first survey the entire slide (WSI-level), then examine suspicious regions (patch-level), and finally classify individual cells at high magnification (cell-level). Classification is assessed using accuracy, precision, recall, F1 score, sensitivity, and specificity. For instance segmentation, Panoptic Quality (PQ) provides a unified metric jointly measuring segmentation and recognition quality. WSI-level screening uses the Area Under the ROC Curve (AUC) for slide-level abnormality assessment. We therefore recommend that studies report a minimum set of prevalence-aware measures: sensitivity at fixed specificity (e.g., 90% and 95%), PPV and NPV at a stated prevalence, the full ROC curve and AUC with 95% confidence intervals, and calibration (reliability diagram and expected calibration error). For segmentation and detection, authors should state the metric definition and IoU threshold, and report per-class results for clinically critical categories (ASC-US, ASC-H, AGC, AIS) rather than only aggregate scores. Comparisons should use patient-level or slide-level splits, and must state whether patches from the same slide or patient appear in both training and test sets. Paired comparisons should use McNemar’s test for classification and paired bootstrap for detection or segmentation metrics; AUC differences should be tested with DeLong’s method. External validation should report the number of centers, scanners, staining protocols, and the target population. We recommend CLAIM, TRIPOD-AI, and STARD-AI for reporting, and QUADAS-2 for risk-of-bias assessment. Table 4 summarizes the minimum reporting checklist. The most widely used benchmarks—Herlev (917 cells, 2005) and SIPaKMeD (4,049 cells, 2018)—are orders of magnitude smaller than ImageNet (1.4M images). Table 7 compares 16 publicly available or publicly documented cytology datasets spanning cell-level to WSI-level scales. CAISHI, a histopathology dataset for adenocarcinoma in situ, is discussed separately because it is not a cytology benchmark. The Herlev dataset Jantzen et al. (2005) (917 single-cell images, 2005) provides nucleus and cytoplasm segmentation masks and 7-class Bethesda labels. It remains the most widely used benchmark for cell-level segmentation and classification, despite its small size and single-center origin. The ISBI 2014/2015 challenge datasets Lu et al. (2015) ; Lu et al. (2017) contain overlapping cervical cells in Extended Depth of Field (EDF) format—ISBI14 includes 16 EDFs with 945 synthetic patches, ISBI15 includes 17 EDFs with 340 fields of view—with pixel-level nucleus and cytoplasm annotations. CERVIX93 Phoulady and Mouton (2018) adds 93 EDFs (approximately 1,860 fields of view at 20 fields per EDF) with nucleus-only segmentation labels. The CRIC dataset Rezende et al. (2020) provides 400 patches and 3,233 annotated cells with 6-class Bethesda labels, bridging cell-level and patch-level analysis. The SIPaKMeD dataset Plissiti et al. (2018) contains 996 patches and 4,049 single-cell images with 5-class labels, designed for patch-level classification. The Tianchi dataset shijian (2021a) (1,000 WSIs from 2019, with 800 training and 200 test slides) is among the first public WSI-level benchmarks, providing ROI annotations for abnormal regions. The ComparisonDetector dataset Liang et al. (2021b) includes 6,666 patch-level images with 11-class labels for cell detection. Several recent datasets address specific limitations. Cx22 (1,320 images) provides full instance-level annotations—bounding boxes, segmentation masks, and category labels—for every cell, enabling both detection and instance segmentation Liu et al. (2022) . HiCervix (40,229 cells, 29-class hierarchy) is the largest annotated single-cell cervical dataset, with a three-level hierarchical classification scheme and both nuclear and cytoplasmic boundaries Cai et al. (2024) . APACC provides 103,675 annotated cell images from 107 conventional Pap smear whole-slide images, with labels from three expert cytopathologists Kupas et al. (2024) . RIVA contains 959 conventional Pap smear patches (15,949 unique cells) with up to four independent expert annotations per cell, enabling inter-observer agreement analysis Perez Bianchi et al. (2025) . Zhang et al. released 8,037 annotated TCT images from 129 slides specifically for detection benchmarking Zhang et al. (2025) . SCAC provides a large unlabeled cervical cytology collection for semi-supervised abnormal-cell detection Zhang et al. (2024) . CCS-127K (127,471 WSIs from 48 clinical centers) is the largest WSI-level cervical screening collection Jiang et al. (2025) . CCBench (773 QA + 420 VQA pairs) provides a benchmark for evaluating vision-language models on cervical cytology tasks Hong et al. (2025) . CAISHI provides 2,240 H&E-stained histopathology images for adenocarcinoma in situ; because it is not a cytology dataset, it is discussed separately rather than pooled with cytology benchmarks Yang et al. (2024) . Despite this progress, a critical gap persists: no large-scale, multi-center, publicly accessible WSI-level benchmark with standardized train/validation/test splits and longitudinal clinical outcomes exists for cervical cytology—analogous to TCGA in cancer genomics or CAMELYON in digital pathology. APACC, RIVA, and the TCT detection set partially address the scale and annotation gaps, but they remain single-country collections without outcome labels or standardized external test splits Kupas et al. (2024) ; Perez Bianchi et al. (2025) ; Zhang et al. (2025) . The construction of a multi-center benchmark with fixed splits and longitudinal outcomes remains an urgent priority for reproducible and clinically meaningful research. Task-specific 6 Cell-level Methods We analyze cell-level cervical screening methods according to three functional categories: segmentation methods (delineating nucleus and cytoplasm), feature extraction methods (deriving discriminative representations), and classification methods (assigning Bethesda diagnostic categories). 6.1 Segmentation Methods Cervical cell segmentation delineates the nucleus and cytoplasm boundaries from microscopy images, forming the foundation for downstream quantitative analysis and classification. Accurate segmentation is challenging due to high cell density, severe overlap and occlusion, substantial variation in cell morphology across the Bethesda spectrum, and inconsistent staining across samples. We categorize segmentation methods into three classes: (1) classical unsupervised methods (watershed, active contours, graph cuts, thresholding, superpixel-based), (2) deep learning methods (U-Net, FCN, Mask R-CNN, and more recent Transformer and Mamba architectures), and (3) hybrid methods combining deep feature extraction with classical refinement. Classical methods are included for context; the remainder of this section focuses on deep learning architectures. Table 8 presents a quantitative comparison. Deep convolutional neural networks have substantially advanced cervical cell segmentation. Early approaches adapted generic architectures; recent work has introduced cervical-specific designs. U-Net Variants: Zhao et al. Zhao et al. (2019a) proposed PGU-net+, a progressive U-Net that learns shape residuals between coarse and fine annotation scales, achieving a DSC of 0.925 and precision of 0.901 on Herlev. Mahyari et al. Mahyari and Dansereau (2021) designed URestNet, a two-stage U-Net with residual blocks and skip connections for iterative refinement. Wan et al. Wan et al. (2019) combined TernausNet for single-cell extraction with DeepLabV2 for cytoplasm segmentation. Ke et al. Ke et al. (2019) used a U-Net with VGG16 encoder and HSDC (Hybrid Standard and Dilated Convolution) decoder modules for joint nucleus-cytoplasm-background segmentation. Zhao et al. Zhao et al. (2019b) introduced D-MEM (Deformable Multi-path Ensemble Model), employing U-Net with dense blocks as a backbone, achieving recall of 0.968. FCN: Harangi et al. Harangi et al. (2019) ensembled FCN-8 and FCN-16 architectures for cervical cytology segmentation, achieving a DSC of 0.93 on single cells but only 0.534 IoU on multi-cell images, highlighting the difficulty of overlapping cell segmentation. Sabeena et al. Sabeena et al. (2020) applied FCN-based approaches achieving 86.67% accuracy on multi-cell segmentation. Mask R-CNN: Kurnianingsih et al. Kurnianingsih et al. (2019) applied Mask R-CNN for contour-level segmentation without classification, achieving precision of 0.92 and recall of 0.91. Zhou et al. Zhou et al. (2019) proposed IR-NET, a Mask R-CNN variant incorporating an instance relation module with self-attention for inter-cell information transfer and a sparse-constrained duplicate removal module. IR-NET achieved cytoplasmic AJI of 0.7185 and nuclear AJI of 0.5496 on multi-cell images, underscoring the challenges of overlapping cell segmentation. Other Architectures: Yang et al. Yang et al. (2020) introduced the Interactive Convolutional Pyramid Network (ICPN) with dual aggregation and selection paths, using Interacting Convolutional Modules (ICM) and Internal Pyramid Resolution Complementing Modules (IPRCM) to capture nuclear size and shape variations. Recent Advances: The segmentation landscape has expanded beyond CNN encoder–decoder architectures. Recent reviews document growing use of attention-enhanced U-Nets, transformer variants, and hybrid designs Fang et al. (2024) ; Valles-Coral et al. (2026) . State-space models have also been applied to cervical nuclei and cytoplasm segmentation; a recent VMamba-based model targets accurate boundary delineation, motivated by the linear complexity of SSMs relative to attention Zhang et al. (2026) . However, most recent reports evaluate on different datasets, class definitions, and splits, so their reported Dice values are not directly comparable. Standardized overlapping-cell benchmarks remain the main bottleneck for measuring progress. Hybrid Methods: Several approaches combine deep feature extraction with classical refinement. Song et al. Song et al. (2014) ; Song et al. (2015) ; Song et al. (2016) integrated multi-scale CNNs with superpixel segmentation (SLIC) and Graph-Cut for cytoplasm and nucleus segmentation, and with level set methods for boundary refinement. Wang et al. (2017) combined FCN with graph model methods for nucleus segmentation. Liu et al. Liu et al. Feature extraction approaches fall into three categories: handcrafted features, deep learning-based features, and hybrid fusion. We summarize handcrafted features briefly as background; the main focus is deep representation learning. Handcrafted Features: Classical morphometric features quantify the structural changes characteristic of cervical neoplasia. Morphological features include area, perimeter, roundness, eccentricity, elongation, compactness, nuclear-to-cytoplasmic (N/C) ratio, nuclear relative position, and long/short diameters of both nucleus and cytoplasm—the N/C ratio being the single most diagnostic morphological parameter in the Bethesda System. Color features encompass brightness, gray value, color intensity, minimum/maximum brightness, average color value, N/C brightness ratio, saturation, and optimal density. Texture features include LBP, GLCM, Haralick features, Laws texture energy, Tamura features, HoG, MRF, roughness, histogram statistics, and frequency-domain texture and energy descriptors. Table 9 provides a comprehensive taxonomy of handcrafted features organized by category and cellular compartment. Deep Learning Features: CNNs pretrained on ImageNet serve as effective feature extractors for cervical cells through transfer learning. Gv et al. Gv and Reddy (2019) extracted features from ResNet34 to train an SVM classifier, achieving 99.63% accuracy on 5-class SIPaKMeD classification. Mousser et al. Mousser and Ouadfel (2019) compared features from VGG16, VGG19, ResNet50, and InceptionV3, finding that deeper architectures capture increasingly abstract representations of nuclear morphology. Khamparia et al. Khamparia et al. (2020) evaluated InceptionV3, VGG19, SqueezeNet, and ResNet50 features. Plissiti et al. Plissiti et al. (2018) used VGG19 features for SIPaKMeD classification. Rahaman et al. Rahaman et al. (2021) proposed a deep feature fusion network concatenating features from VGG16, VGG19, ResNet50, and XceptionNet, achieving 99.14% accuracy on 5-class classification. Nguyen et al. Nguyen et al. (2019) ; Nguyen et al. (2018) fused InceptionV3, ResNet152, and InceptionResNetV2 features. Hybrid Feature Fusion: Combining handcrafted and deep features leverages complementary strengths—the clinical interpretability of morphometric features and the representational power of deep features. Dong et al. Dongyao Jia et al. (2020) combined GLCM, Gabor, and CNN features. Dong et al. Dong et al. (2020) fused InceptionV3 features with color, texture, and morphological features. Rohmatillah et al. Rohmatillah et al. (2018) combined LeNet features with LDA and PCA projections. Deep learning has eliminated manual feature engineering from state-of-the-art pipelines. However, handcrafted features retain value for two reasons: they are inherently interpretable, mapping directly to Bethesda System diagnostic criteria (N/C ratio, nuclear membrane irregularities, chromatin texture), and they complement deep features in hybrid approaches, particularly for rare cell types where deep models overfit. The principal limitation of deep feature extraction is domain shift: features from ImageNet-pretrained networks may not optimally represent the distinctive texture and chromatic characteristics of Papanicolaou-stained cytology images. Self-supervised pretraining on cervical-specific image collections represents a promising avenue for learning domain-adapted feature representations. Accurate segmentation is challenging due to high cell density, severe overlap and occlusion, substantial variation in cell morphology across the Bethesda spectrum, and inconsistent staining across samples. We categorize segmentation methods into three classes: (1) classical unsupervised methods (watershed, active contours, graph cuts, thresholding, superpixel-based), (2) deep learning methods (U-Net, FCN, Mask R-CNN, and more recent Transformer and Mamba architectures), and (3) hybrid methods combining deep feature extraction with classical refinement. We categorize these methods into instance segmentation (simultaneous segmentation and classification of each cell) and object detection (bounding-box localization and classification). Both approaches represent end-to-end deep learning paradigms that eliminate the explicit segmentation step required by cell-level pipelines. Framing cervical cytology screening as an object detection or instance segmentation task transforms the problem from a sequential pipeline into an end-to-end optimization. These methods require bounding box, segmentation mask, or category annotations for training, incurring higher annotation cost but delivering simultaneous localization and classification. 7.1 Instance Segmentation Instance segmentation simultaneously segments individual cells and assigns class labels, providing the most information-rich output at the patch level. Mask R-CNN serves as the dominant architecture. Sompawong et al. Sompawong et al. (2019) applied Mask R-CNN for end-to-end cervical cell classification, localization, and segmentation, achieving mAP of 0.578, accuracy of 91.7%, sensitivity of 91.7%, and specificity of 91.7%. The relatively low mAP (0.578) compared to classification accuracy (91.7%) reveals that the detection and segmentation components lag behind the classification component—a pattern consistent across cervical cell analysis pipelines. Long et al. Long et al. (2021) enhanced Mask R-CNN with NAS-FPN (Neural Architecture Search Feature Pyramid Network) for multi-scale feature fusion and soft-NMS to improve recall on overlapping cells. Zhou et al. Zhou et al. (2020) proposed MMT-PSM, a semi-supervised knowledge distillation approach using dual Mask R-CNN models with K-fold data augmentation on the teacher model, enforcing feature knowledge distillation in foreground regions through predicted segmentation masks. This approach achieved AJI of 0.6694 and mAP of 0.4052, demonstrating that semi-supervised learning can partially mitigate annotation requirements. Recent Advances in Instance Segmentation: Methodological innovations include boundary-refinement modules such as PointRend, query-based mask transformers derived from DETR, Mask2Former-style universal segmentation, and adaptations of the Segment Anything Model and CellViT for cell-instance tasks. These approaches are promising for overlapping cervical cells because they model long-range context and refine boundaries. However, cervical-specific evidence remains limited: most reports use private datasets with different annotation protocols, and direct comparisons against Mask R-CNN baselines on public overlapping-cell benchmarks are scarce. Standardized instance-level annotations, such as those in Cx22, are a prerequisite for rigorous comparison Liu et al. (2022) . 7.2 Object Detection Object detection localizes and classifies abnormal cells via bounding boxes. Two architectural paradigms dominate: two-stage detectors (Faster R-CNN, Cascade R-CNN) that first propose regions then classify them, and one-stage detectors (SSD, YOLO, RetinaNet) that directly predict bounding boxes and classes.
