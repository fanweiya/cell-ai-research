3 Method 3.1 Cross-modal contrastive architecture The framework, as shown in Figure 1(a) , consists of two parallel encoding streams that map histological and genomic data to a shared joint embedding space. Histological stream: Visual features extracted from the frozen CONCH backbone [ 13 ] are refined by a visual adapter (a 2-layer MLP with layer normalization and ReLU) into a task-specific latent space. To preserve spatial structure within the permutation-invariant MIL setting, we employ Fourier Positional Encodings, mapping normalized patch coordinates to a high-dimensional frequency domain. Next, because different tumor histologies present distinct regions of interest, we compute the final slide-level embedding using our Tumor Histology Conditioned Attention (THCA), a lightweight gated-attention MIL module that conditions each patch’s attention score on a learned histology-type embedding, yielding a histology-weighted sum of the adapted features. THCA guides the network to learn histology-specific attention profiles. Finally, an MLP projects these features into a shared space with the RNA embeddings, while a concurrently trained probe (2-layer MLP) enables direct WSI classification at inference. Genomic stream: We encode the 10-gene expression signature into a latent representation utilizing a permutation-invariant embedding scheme followed by a Self-Normalizing Neural Network (SNN) [ 10 ] . We assign a learnable identity vector to each gene. The initial aggregation is computed as the expression-weighted sum of these identities, since it learns a gene-specific semantic space while preserving permutation invariance. The aggregated vector is refined by an SNN composed of linear layers with Scaled Exponential Linear Units (SELU) and Alpha Dropout. Finally, similar to the histological stream, the features are projected to a common space with the RNA features using an MLP. 3.2 Losses To address label ambiguity and data scarcity, we optimize a composite objective to promote the latent space to be structured by molecular phenotype and separable for downstream classification. Task Supervision (Classification Loss): To enforce separability and refine the decision boundaries of the shared projection manifold, we apply a classification probe to both the aggregated WSI features and the genomic RNA features. The total classification loss is computed as the unweighted average of the Binary Cross-Entropy (BCE) losses from both modalities. This equal weighting encourages that both the histological and genomic representations independently maintain high diagnostic utility and are mapped correctly with respect to the target task’s decision boundary. 3.3 Inference strategy At inference, the genomic stream is detached, enabling unimodal operation on standard H&E WSIs to bypass sequencing costs while retaining multimodal benefits. The resulting embedding can be exploited in two complementary ways. Direct Classification: The pre-trained probe projects the histological slide-level embedding onto the decision boundary to output a probability score. Retrieval Strategy: Given a query H&E slide, its embeddings are projected into the shared latent space and used to retrieve the most similar RNA embeddings from the reference dataset. We evaluated several cross-modal strategies: Inverted Softmax [ 21 ] , α \alpha -CSLS [ 11 ] , cosine k k -NN, Diffusion [ 7 ] , and AQE [ 4 ] . We found CSLS combined with Reciprocal reranking to provide the best retrieval performance, mitigating hubness and refining cross-domain alignment. The dataset consists of 142 “Non-Inflamed” and 123 “Inflamed” cases, with ground truth labels derived from a 10-gene RNA signature ( ADORA2A, BLM, CCL20, CD3E, CD5, CD74, GLS, HLA-E, ITGAL, RPL23 ) [ 19 ] . Additionally, cases were stratified by expert pathologists according to the histology Lauren classification into 173 Intestinal, 65 Diffuse, and 16 Mixed subtypes. WSI processing was standardized using the Trident [ 25 ] framework for tissue segmentation and non-overlapping patching at 40 × \times . Finally, patch-level embeddings were extracted using the CONCH [ 15 ] foundation model. 4.2 Training setup Framework and hardware All experiments were implemented using the PyTorch framework, leveraging the pytorch-metric-learning library for loss formulations. Training was accelerated on a single NVIDIA A40 GPU (48GB VRAM). Due to the computational overhead of processing dense WSI feature bags, we utilized a batch size of B = 8 B=8 patients with gradient accumulation of 4 steps, which was the maximum capacity allowed by memory constraints. Optimization The network was trained for 50 epochs per fold using a 5-fold cross-validation scheme with early-stopping. Each fold was composed of a train, validation and test sets of 80%, 10% and 10% of the cohort respectively. We employed the Adam optimizer with a fixed learning rate of 5 × 10 − 5 5\times 10^{-5} and the loss defined in Section 3.2 . Baselines We compare CCMIL against representative state-of-the-art unimodal (H&E-only) and multimodal (H&E + RNA) approaches. We evaluate three widely used unimodal MIL models: ABMIL [ 6 ] , CLAM-SB [ 14 ] , and TransMIL [ 20 ] . For multimodal fusion, we compare against MCAT [ 3 ] , which explicitly co-attends and fuses WSI and genomic features. For fair comparison, all models utilize identical CONCH embeddings and are evaluated across the same 5-fold splits. To isolate the contribution of our class-aware supervised contrastive framework, we evaluate a retrieval baseline that utilizes the same spatial architecture but relies solely on instance-level alignment (CLIP loss [ 17 ] ). This setup mirrors standard multimodal contrastive methods widely used in the field. 4.1 Dataset and preprocessing We utilized a curated subset of the TCGA Gastric Adenocarcinoma cohort comprising N = 265 N=265 diagnostic WSIs, manually reviewed by expert pathologists to ensure optimal tissue quality. As demonstrated in Figure 2(b) , CSLS + Reciprocal reranking mitigates high-dimensional hubness and maintains the highest RNA cosine similarity to the Oracle upper bound for reliable signature imputation. Using this retrieval strategy, the 5-fold cross-validation results (Table 1 ) demonstrate the effectiveness of CCMIL as a cross-modal retrieval engine. We report performance across two retrieval settings: Test → \to Train, mimicking real-world clinical inference by querying a new test slide against an established set of training cases, and Test → \to Test, which retrieves entirely within the test set to accurately compute R@1 and R@5 scores (percentage of queries that retrieve a match in the top k k results). Compared with the CLIP baseline, our class-aware formulation achieves a Test → \to Test R@5 of 0.92 and MRR of 0.80. Aggregating these retrieved WSI via weighted k k -NN voting in the Test → \to Train setting yields a classification Accuracy of 0.72 and an F1-score of 0.76. This learned latent space also enables effective downstream classification. CLAM achieves comparable Accuracy (0.73) but lacks clinical balance, while TransMIL and multimodal MCAT yield poor Precision (0.51, 0.61) due to domain gaps. Conversely, CCMIL achieves the highest F1-score (0.76) and Recall (0.92) indicating improved sensitivity for ICI pre-screening. Our ablation study (Table 2 ) confirms our architectural choices: representation learning alone yields poor decision boundaries (Accuracy 0.54), omitting the contrastive signal degrades retrieval (R@1 0.63), and replacing THCA with standard attention impairs both fine-grained cross-modal retrieval (R@1 0.60) and overall F1-score (0.74). 5.2 Qualitative Evaluation Spatial attention heatmaps (Fig. 3(a) ) highlight the interpretability of our framework. Compared to the baseline methods, which exhibit sparse under-activation (ABMIL), severe attention saturation (MCAT), or diffuse noise (CLAM), CCMIL generates dense, localized hotspots that align closely with ground-truth tumor segmentations. Because the “Inflamed” signature is visually indistinguishable to pathologists, these biologically coherent activations suggest CCMIL captures subtle morphological patterns associated with the “Inflamed” phenotype. Furthermore, we show that the embedding space captures a continuous phenotype (Fig. 3(b) ). By projecting test slides onto an inflammation axis (the vector between class centroids), we selected seven evenly-spaced query slides. Displaying these alongside their retrieved RNA expression profiles (via Slide → \rightarrow RNA CSLS+Reciprocal reranking at ranks 1, 2, 3, and 10) confirms that CCMIL organizes slides along a biologically meaningful gradient, surfacing transcriptomically coherent neighbors even at larger retrieval ranks. 5.1 Quantitative Evaluation We first select the retrieval strategy by evaluating multiple metrics on the validation set. By integrating a composite loss with our THCA module, CCMIL learns a shared histology-transcriptomics latent space during training, improving cross-modal alignment relative to the evaluated baselines. Beyond binary classification, CCMIL acts as an interpretable search-by-case engine, allowing pathologists to retrieve transcriptomically coherent neighbors and impute a patient’s continuous inflammatory phenotype. This learned retrieval-oriented representation also supports effective downstream classification, yielding a highly sensitive classifier for immunotherapy pre-screening. While our cross-validation demonstrates consistent performance within the TCGA cohort, this proof-of-concept has been evaluated only on a retrospective cohort. Future work will focus on validating the framework in an independent external cohort to assess generalizability. Ultimately, by providing biologically meaningful spatial heatmaps and bypassing genomic sequencing at inference, CCMIL offers a scalable framework for precision immuno-oncology.
