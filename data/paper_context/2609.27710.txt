Pathology vision-language foundation models. Contrastive image-text pretraining [ 18 ] has been transferred to histopathology by a growing family of foundation models, whose training corpora range from medical Twitter threads [ 1 ] , narration transcribed from educational videos [ 2 ] to figure-caption pairs from the open-access literature [ 3 ] , in-house clinical collections [ 4 , 5 ] , and released corpora restructured under a disease ontology [ 6 ] . Benchmarks agree that no member of this family dominates: across 31 31 models and 41 41 tasks, the top of the ranking changes with the tissue [ 7 ] . Few-shot adaptation of vision-language models for histopathology images. Few-shot adaptation is particularly important in histopathology because assembling labeled whole-slide image datasets requires substantial clinical resources and expert effort. For frozen VLMs, CoOp [ 19 ] learns prompt context vectors, while CLIP-Adapter [ 20 ] adapts features through a lightweight bottleneck. Tip-Adapter [ 21 ] uses a training-free support-set cache, and Graph Adapter [ 22 ] refines textual representations through a dual knowledge graph. For WSI analysis, ViLa-MIL [ 10 ] uses LLM-generated prompts at two magnification scales with 16 16 labeled slides per class. MSCPT [ 11 ] learns multi-scale, context-aware prompts, while FOCUS [ 12 ] uses language prompts to select diagnostically relevant patches. MGPATH [ 9 ] couples the Prov-GigaPath [ 23 ] vision encoder with the PLIP [ 1 ] text encoder through multi-granular prompting. PathPT [ 8 ] applies prompt tuning to rare cancer subtyping with 1 1 – 10 10 WSIs per subtype. Related to our alignment stage, FSF [ 13 ] aligns independently pretrained visual and textual encoders using a closed-form orthogonal Procrustes transformation and regularizes adaptation with a flow-matching prior. These methods adapt a single backbone or a predefined vision-text pair. FFM-CP jointly refines visual and textual representations across M M frozen VLMs, enabling information exchange during adaptation, and combines predictions from all M 2 M^{2} ordered backbone pairs. Combining multiple foundation models in pathology. MFMF [ 15 ] combines features from multiple patch encoders within a multiple-instance learning network for whole-slide classification. ELF [ 16 ] integrates five tile-level foundation models through slide-encoder pretraining on 53,699 53{,}699 WSIs from 11 11 datasets. It combines contrastive alignment with weakly supervised objectives and uses fixed slide representations for downstream linear probing. FuseCPath [ 17 ] combines patch- and slide-level foundation models across scales. At the prediction level, LogitProd [ 24 ] learns sample-adaptive weights over frozen experts’ logits without aligning their feature spaces. Its training procedure first fits task-specific expert heads, then trains a fusion gate on a separate labeled subset. Meanwhile, F ​ M 2 FM^{2} [ 25 ] distills general and pathology foundation models into a single student by disentangling their consensus and divergence, followed by zero- and few-shot evaluation. Our work focuses on learning task-specific fusion directly from a few-shot support set. FFM-CP retains each model’s frozen visual and textual encoders and learns cross-model representation refinement and prediction fusion from the C ⋅ K C\cdot K labeled support tiles of a C C -class, K K -shot task. This approach requires no separate large-scale fusion pretraining or distillation stage. 3 Method 3.1 Preliminaries Vision-language foundation models for pathology. Contrastive image-text pretraining has been extended to histopathology [ 1 , 2 , 4 , 6 , 26 , 5 , 3 ] . These models typically comprise a visual encoder E v E_{v} and a text encoder E t E_{t} , which map H&E tiles and descriptions to representations z v z_{v} and z t z_{t} , respectively. Pretraining encourages high cosine similarity between paired image and text representations. In our framework, both encoders remain frozen during downstream adaptation. Despite this shared structure, their pretraining data, objectives, and architectures differ. Training corpora include medical social media posts [ 1 ] , transcribed educational videos [ 2 ] , article figure-caption pairs [ 3 ] , and non-public clinical cohorts [ 4 , 5 ] . Most models directly align paired tiles and captions. MUSK [ 5 ] first applies unified masked modeling to unpaired data comprising 50 50 million tiles and 10 9 10^{9} pathology-text tokens, then aligns the modalities. Text encoders range from generic CLIP-style Transformers to biomedical models informed by disease ontology [ 6 ] . Visual architectures and embedding dimensions also vary. These differences may contribute to task-dependent variation in model performance. Complementary strengths of pathology foundation models. In a benchmark of 32 foundation models on 41 computational pathology tasks, Bareja et al. [7] report tissue-dependent variation in model rankings. Different models lead on brain and breast tumor classification, bladder and prostate subtyping, and lung tasks. Differences in model development may contribute to this variability. The morphological features and scales used for diagnosis and grading also differ across tumor types. Nottingham grading of breast carcinoma combines tubule formation, nuclear pleomorphism, and mitotic count [ 27 ] . Gleason/ISUP grading of prostate carcinoma focuses primarily on glandular architecture, including gland fusion, cribriform formation, and loss of discrete glands [ 28 ] . Lung adenocarcinoma subtype assesses the predominant growth pattern from the arrangement of tumor cells within tissue [ 29 ] . These pathological differences provide a plausible basis for performance variation across tissues, although they cannot alone explain model rankings within a given tissue. The complementary strengths of existing models therefore motivate combining multiple pathology FMs. Few-shot multi-model setting. Our graph construction is inspired by GraphAdapter [ 22 ] , which refines textual prompt features using task-specific structural knowledge obtained by modeling inter-class relationships. We extend this idea to a unified graph that jointly refines support-image features and visual and textual class prototypes across the M M pathology VLMs. Histopathological diagnostic categories should not be viewed as unrelated labels. Tumor classifications are hierarchical by construction [ 31 ] . For example, histological grading in breast cancer provides an ordinal scale in which neighboring grades differ in degree rather than in kind [ 27 ] . Accordingly, adjacent or morphologically similar categories may share morphological pattern and can pose diagnostic challenges for pathologists [ 32 ] . The datasets span four organs including lung, skin, colorectum and breast. Moreover, they cover tissue-type recognition (Kather2016, LubLung, HeidelbergSkin) and diagnostic subtyping (LungHist700, BRACS, BACH), with sizes ranging from 400 images to 129,364 tiles and class counts from 4 to 16. Detailed description is included in Appendix A.1 . Every dataset was divided into disjoint training, validation and test proportions. Given a shot count K K , we draw K K examples per class from the training partition, and min ⁡ ( K , 4 ) \min(K,4) examples per class from the validation partition, following standard practice for few-shot adaptation of vision-language models [ 22 ] . For BRACS, we used the officially released test set as the test partition without further modification.
