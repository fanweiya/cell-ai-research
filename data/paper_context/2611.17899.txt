Flow matching for generative modeling. Flow matching (FM) has recently emerged as an effective paradigm for generative modeling, demonstrating strong performance across diverse domains, including vision, biomolecular modeling, and robotics ( Jing et al., 2024 ; Zhang et al., 2025 ; Jin et al., 2025 ; Wang et al., 2025 ; Song et al., 2025 ) . Rather than simulating a reverse stochastic process during training, FM learns a time-dependent vector field that transports samples from a simple prior distribution to the target data distribution along a prescribed probability path. This formulation yields a conceptually simple objective and supports efficient, often deterministic, sampling, making it attractive for large-scale generation tasks. Recent studies have further revisited the parameterization of continuous generative models, suggesting that directly predicting clean signals can simplify the learning objective and improve empirical performance, compared with modeling noise or velocity fields ( Li and He, 2025 ; Lu et al., 2026 ; Yu et al., 2026 ; Song et al., 2025 ) . Motivated by these advances, we formulate gene expression prediction as a generative process that maps a flexible prior to target expression profiles, which is well suited to large gene panel prediction settings. 3. The model is trained using the standard velocity-matching objective: (8) ℒ FM = 𝔼 𝐱 0 , 𝐱 1 , t ​ [ ‖ 𝐯 θ ​ ( 𝐱 t , t , 𝐜 I ) − 𝐯 ‖ 2 2 ] \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{FM}}=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}_{0},\mathbf{x}_{1},t}\left[\left\|\mathbf{v}_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t},t,\mathbf{c}_{I})-\mathbf{v}\right\|_{2}^{2}\right] This formulation can therefore be interpreted as x-prediction with a velocity-space loss : the network output is the clean endpoint 𝐱 ^ 1 \hat{\mathbf{x}}_{1} , whereas supervision is applied after converting the endpoint prediction into the corresponding velocity ( Li and He, 2025 ) . Prior distribution. ST data exhibit two key characteristics: high sparsity due to excessive zero counts (i.e., non-activated genes) and overdispersion where the variance exceeds the mean. Standard distributions such as Gaussian or zero fail to capture these properties. The parameter ϕ \phi allows the negative binomial component to capture overdispersion beyond the Poisson assumption. This provides a more accurate statistical description of gene expression patterns and improves the modeling of realistic count data during training. Sampling. We start from an initial expression sample 𝐱 0 \mathbf{x}_{0} drawn from the ZINB distribution 𝒵 ⁡ ( μ , ϕ , π ) \mathcal{Z}(\mu,\phi,\pi) and iteratively refine it through the learned flow model. This process is repeated across time steps and produces the final gene expression at t = 1 t=1 . Histological tokenizer. Instead of training a pathology encoder from scratch, we directly leverage pretrained foundation models to extract histological representations. These models are trained on large-scale pathology datasets and provide general-purpose features with strong transferability, which is particularly beneficial in ST settings where labeled data are limited and distribution shifts across slides are common. Such representations also help mitigate batch effects by capturing more stable morphological patterns. Formally, we denote f H ​ ( ⋅ ) f_{\text{H}}(\cdot) as a pathology foundation model that maps an input H&E image patch I i I_{i} to a pathology embedding E i E_{i} , i.e., { E 0 , ⋯ , E N } = f H ​ ( { I 0 , ⋯ , I N } ) . \{E_{0},\cdots,E_{N}\}=f_{\text{H}}(\{I_{0},\cdots,I_{N}\}). In practice, we follow Stem ( Zhu et al., 2025 ) setting to obtain patch-level embeddings produced by UNI ( Chen et al., 2024 ) and CONCH ( Huang et al., 2023 ) , and project them to the generative model hidden space for subsequent processing. To further improve robustness and reduce overfitting, we also adopt lightweight data augmentation strategies, including rotations and flipping, without altering the underlying tissue structure. The resulting histological embeddings are then used as conditional inputs for downstream gene expression modeling. Gene tokenizer. We assume that local morphological context in ST is implicitly provided by the pretrained histological representations for each spatial spot; therefore, to maintain a simple and efficient design, we do not explicitly encode the spatial coordinates or inter-spot relations. Given a gene expression vector x ( k ) ∈ ℝ G x^{(k)}\in\mathbb{R}^{G} for each spot k k , we first project it into a latent token space using the gene tokenizer. Formally, the tokenization process can be written as Z ( k ) = ϕ ⁡ ( x ( k ) ​ W + b ) + P , Z^{(k)}=\phi\!\left(x^{(k)}W+b\right)+P, where W ∈ ℝ G × ( h ​ d ) W\in\mathbb{R}^{G\times(hd)} and b ∈ ℝ h ​ d b\in\mathbb{R}^{hd} denote the learnable projection parameters, ϕ ⁡ ( ⋅ ) \phi(\cdot) is a non-linear activation function, and P ∈ ℝ h × d P\in\mathbb{R}^{h\times d} is a learnable gene token-type embedding. The output is then reshaped into Z ( k ) ∈ ℝ h × d Z^{(k)}\in\mathbb{R}^{h\times d} for each spot, where h h denotes the number of gene module tokens and d d is the token dimension. By compressing high-dimensional gene expression into a fixed number of gene module tokens, the model captures shared structure among genes while maintaining a compact and computationally efficient representation. In addition, the modular tokenization provides a more interpretable latent space, as each token can capture a relatively coherent functional gene program rather than an entangled mixture of unrelated signals. Overall fusion architecture Our GeneFlow network is built on a DiT-based transformer ( Peebles and Xie, 2023 ) that parameterizes a flow matching model. Gene expression inputs are encoded using a gene tokenizer, resulting in a sequence of hidden vectors with D D dimensions. Time information is encoded using a standard 256-dimensional sinusoidal embedding followed by a 2-layer MLP. For histology, each patch is represented by a single embedding extracted from pretrained pathology foundation models, either via special tokens or pooling; when multiple models are used, their embeddings are concatenated and projected through a 2-layer MLP to obtain a unified conditioning vector. This conditioning vector, together with the time embedding, is injected into the transformer blocks via adaLN-Zero modulation. The model consists of 12 DiT blocks with 6-head attention, followed by a final adaLN and linear layer to predict the flow field. An overview of the architecture is presented in Fig. 3 . Some useful tricks. 1. Parameter-free RMSNorm. We apply RMS normalization ( Zhang and Sennrich, 2019 ) without learnable parameters throughout the DiT network. Given the high sparsity and large dynamic range of gene expression data, this design provides stable feature scaling across spots and genes without introducing additional parameters. It is inserted after the input embedding, before each attention and MLP block, and before the final prediction head, ensuring stable feature scaling with zero parameter overhead. 2. ReLU 2 activation. We replace GELU with a squared ReLU activation ( Zhang et al., 2024 ) in MLP blocks, i.e., F.relu(x).square() . This activation better matches the non-negative and sparse nature of gene expression signals, promoting sparse responses without sacrificing representational capacity. 3. QK normalization. Although RoPE does not provide noticeable gains in our setting, we retain query–key normalization and apply it directly as q , k = norm ⁡ ( q ) , norm ⁡ ( k ) q,k=\mathrm{norm}(q),\mathrm{norm}(k) . This stabilizes attention computation and removes the need for additional techniques. 3.3. The generalized manifold perspective of our formulation Boundary cases. When t → 0 t\to 0 , the interpolated state approaches the prior sample, i.e., 𝐱 t → 𝐱 0 \mathbf{x}_{t}\to\mathbf{x}_{0} , and the predicted endpoint 𝐱 ^ 1 = net θ ​ ( 𝐱 t , t , 𝐜 I ) \hat{\mathbf{x}}_{1}=\text{{net}}_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t},t,\mathbf{c}_{I}) provides an explicit target on the gene-expression data manifold. The resulting velocity 𝐯 θ = ( 𝐱 ^ 1 − 𝐱 t ) / ( 1 − t ) \mathbf{v}_{\theta}=(\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{1}-\mathbf{x}_{t})/(1-t) therefore points from the prior toward a predicted clean expression profile. When t → 1 t\to 1 , both 𝐱 t \mathbf{x}_{t} and the ideally predicted 𝐱 ^ 1 \hat{\mathbf{x}}_{1} approach the clean data 𝐱 1 \mathbf{x}_{1} , so the displacement 𝐱 ^ 1 − 𝐱 t \hat{\mathbf{x}}_{1}-\mathbf{x}_{t} connects two states close to the data manifold. Meanwhile, the direction of the predicted velocity is still anchored by an explicit terminal expression estimate rather than an unconstrained velocity output. General case. For t ∈ ( 0 , 1 ) t\in(0,1) , the interpolated state 𝐱 t \mathbf{x}_{t} may lie outside the low-dimensional and highly non-uniform manifold of observed gene-expression profiles. GeneFlow first maps this intermediate state and its histology condition to a clean endpoint estimate 𝐱 ^ 1 \hat{\mathbf{x}}_{1} , which is directly supervised by real expression profiles, and then constructs the velocity from the displacement between 𝐱 t \mathbf{x}_{t} and 𝐱 ^ 1 \hat{\mathbf{x}}_{1} . This endpoint-oriented parameterization encourages the predicted flow to point toward biologically plausible regions of the expression space, whereas direct velocity prediction does not explicitly associate its output with a point on the data manifold. Our empirical results suggest that it provides a useful manifold-anchored inductive bias for sparse, heavy-tailed, and technically noisy ST data. 3.2. Conclusion In this work, we propose GeneFlow, a novel framework for spatially resolved gene expression inference from H&E-stained histology images. By combining flow-based generative modeling with structured multi-modal fusion, GeneFlow provides a simple yet effective way to capture both morphological cues and cross-gene dependencies in a unified representation space. As a result, GeneFlow generates highly accurate and biologically meaningful predictions for unseen histology images at test time and achieves state-of-the-art performance across multiple datasets and evaluation metrics. Its strong accuracy, efficiency, and scalability suggest that simple generative designs can serve as practical and competitive solutions for large-scale spatial gene expression prediction. 6. Limitations and Ethical Considerations Our results are bounded by several limitations. First, its prediction accuracy is constrained by the quality and representation capacity of the pathology foundation model. Second, GeneFlow relies primarily on paired H&E and ST data for supervision and does not explicitly incorporate external biological knowledge, which may limit its generalizability in settings where paired training data are scarce. For future work, it would be interesting to extend GeneFlow by incorporating additional modalities, such as language information. We conduct secondary analysis of publicly available, de-identified human and animal datasets without collecting new specimens or accessing personally identifiable information. We position this work for research use and recommend that any deployment follow institutional biosafety review and applicable regulations. Effect of ZINB Distribution Hyperparameters We use a zero-inflated negative binomial distribution 𝒵 ⁡ ( μ , ϕ , π ) \mathcal{Z}(\mu,\phi,\pi) to model the sparsity and overdispersion of ST expression counts. During training, the initial expression 𝐱 0 \mathbf{x}_{0} is sampled directly from 𝒵 ⁡ ( μ , ϕ , π ) \mathcal{Z}(\mu,\phi,\pi) and interpolated with the ground-truth expression 𝐱 1 \mathbf{x}_{1} as 𝐱 t = ( 1 − t ) ​ 𝐱 0 + t ​ 𝐱 1 \mathbf{x}_{t}=(1-t)\mathbf{x}_{0}+t\mathbf{x}_{1} . Loss Space Analysis As shown in Fig. 11 , increasing the hidden dimension monotonically reduces the converged training loss, indicating that larger models can better fit the FM objective in the velocity space. However, the best predictive accuracy is achieved at a moderate dimension (24), while further increasing capacity degrades performance, suggesting that minimizing ℒ FM \mathcal{L}_{\text{FM}} is not well aligned with the downstream metric and cannot reliably reflect generalization. It indicates the need for more effective training-time evaluation protocols that are better correlated with downstream prediction quality, rather than relying solely on the FM loss as a proxy.
