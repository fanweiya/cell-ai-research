LABEL-FREE CORESET SELECTION WITH FOUNDA- TION MODELS FOR EFFICIENT ANNOTATION IN COM- PUTATIONAL PATHOLOGY Tuo Yin1,2, Jennifer Dhont1,2,3 1Department of Medical Physics, Institut Jules Bordet, Hˆopital Universitaire de Bruxelles (HUB), Belgium 2Radiophysics and MRI Physics Laboratory, Universit´e Libre de Bruxelles (ULB), Belgium 3Laboratory of Image Synthesis and Analysis (LISA), Universit´e Libre de Bruxelles (ULB), Belgium {tuo.yin, jennifer.dhont}@hubruxelles.be ABSTRACT Computational pathology has the potential to improve clinical outcomes through a demonstrated increase in diagnostic and prognostic accuracy. However, the de- velopment and validation of deep learning algorithms still require annotated data, a costly procedure involving expert pathologists who already face critical work- force shortages. Existing coreset selection methods to optimize annotation ef- forts currently all rely on hyperparameters tuned on natural-image benchmarks that do not transfer to histopathology and are cumbersome to use in clinical prac- tice. In this study, we present GCcore, a novel label-free coreset selection method that embeds every image of a dataset with any pathology foundation model and greedily selects the samples that collectively maximize the global coverage of the embedding space. The proposed method provides a lower-bound guarantee on the global coverage of the returned coreset for any coreset size, while being completely hyperparameter-free and deterministic. We demonstrate GCcore’s su- perior performance over 14 baselines including state-of-the-art methods across 10 tasks and datasets spanning whole slide image classification, tile classification, and tissue segmentation, where it ranks first on six and within the top three on nine, while also demonstrating how existing methods can shift by up to five rank positions depending on their hyperparameter settings. Histopathology plays a central role in clinical prac- tice, underpinning a wide range of diagnostic, , prog- nostic, and treatment selection decisions, particu- larly in oncology. At the same time, recent ad- vances in artificial intelligence (AI) have demon- strated considerable potential to enhance and ex- tend histopathological analysis. Digitization of whole-slide images (WSIs) and the increasing use of computational pathology has enabled faster and more standardized (semi)automated tissue evalua- tion (Verghese et al., 2023). Deep learning (DL)- based methods, in particular, have achieved per- formance comparable to that of expert pathologists across a number of tasks, while also broadening the scope to tasks that were previously too time- consuming, such as detailed WSI tissue segmenta- tion. More recently, large-scale pathology founda- tion models (FMs) pretrained on millions of image tiles have demonstrated to be robust feature extrac- tors (Campanella et al., 2025; Bareja et al., 2025), 1 arXiv:2610.05987v1 [cs.CV] 5 Oct 2026 enabling a wide range of downstream tasks while requiring smaller amounts of annotated data (Roth et al., 2024; Nicke et al., 2025). Nevertheless, the development of DL models in computational pathology still relies heavily on expert-annotated data, whether for fully supervised training or for fine-tuning in the context of FMs. Obtaining such high-quality annotations is time-consuming and cognitively demanding (Montezuma et al., 2023), motivating a fundamental question: given an unlabeled histopathology dataset of N samples and a budget of k ≪N annotations, which k WSIs or tiles, i.e., which coreset, should be selected for expert annotation to maximize downstream performance? Selecting a coreset without access to prior label information remains challenging, and, to date, no systematic and reproducible strategy has been established and validated for histopathology. In computer vision, coreset selection methods have been proposed, but with the primary goal of re- ducing computational cost during training rather than optimizing annotation efforts (Coleman et al., 2019; Zheng et al., 2025; Griffin et al., 2026). Consequently, most methods were formulated with access to labels and only subsequently adapted to the label-free regime. Existing approaches can be broadly grouped into three categories. Coverage-based methods select samples according to their distribution in the embedding space, aiming to minimize redundancy among selected samples. Importance-based methods assign each sample a scalar importance score, derived from training dynamics or embedding geometry, and select samples based on this score alone. Hybrid methods combine these two principles by jointly accounting for the individual importance of each sample and the redundancy among selected samples. However, we show that existing coreset selection methods cannot be reliably transferred to histopathology datasets for two main reasons. First, existing label-free coreset selection methods usually rely on hyperparameters that were selected based on downstream performance on a limited set of natural-image benchmarks (a full list is given in Appendix C), and neither the values nor the ranges of robustness established in this context are necessarily transferable. For instance, Info- Max (Tan et al., 2025) was reported to be robust to its pairwise weight α over the range [0.3, 4] on CC12M. On PANDA at a 2% retention rate, however, varying α within this range yields quadratic weighted Cohen’s κ (QWK) values between 0.58 and 0.70. Similarly, ELFS (Zheng et al., 2025) sets the number of pseudo-classes ˜C to the number of classes in the dataset. On TCGA-BRCA (binary classification) at a 5% retention rate, varying ˜C from 2 to 20 yields test balanced accuracies between 0.71 and 0.85, with the prescribed value ˜C = 2 performing worst. Moreover, this rule does not transfer to the four-class segmentation task of BEETLE, where individual tiles do not carry a single class label; varying ˜C from 4 to 25 at a 0.02% retention rate yields test weighted Dice values between 0.70 and 0.84. These examples show that (1) a range reported as robust on natural images can produce substantial variation on histopathology data; (2) the recommended value can be among the worst choices; and (3) the default may be undefined, as for segmentation. Moreover, it is also unclear whether hyperparameters optimized for histopathology generalize across different cancer types and tasks, or whether they need to be determined separately for each new application, which may itself require additional labelled data in contradiction with the main goal of coreset selection. This motivates a genuinely hyperparameter-free coreset selection method for computational pathol- ogy. Second, existing methods generally provide no a priori guarantee on the quality of the selected subset with respect to a specified objective function. Such a guarantee is of particular value in histopathology, where every selected sample is converted into a dense annotation at a cost measured in pathologist hours, and where this cost cannot be recovered once a selection has proven poor. To address these two challenges, we propose GCcore, a novel label-free coreset selection method that iteratively adds the sample from the remaining pool that maximizes the global coverage (GC) of the embedding space (Algorithm 1). It accounts for both coverage and importance without a weighting term, unlike existing hybrid methods: a sample is selected only when it is both repre- sentative, i.e., surrounded by many samples, and novel, i.e., far from the samples already selected. We compare GCcore with 14 baselines on 10 pathology datasets spanning WSI classification, tile classification and segmentation (Section 4). GCcore achieves the best mean rank on all three task families (2.17, 1.88 and 2.75, respectively; Fig. 1), ranking first on six datasets and within the top three on nine, while remaining computationally efficient: it selects a coreset of 100 samples from a pool of 100,000 in 89 s. We further prove GCcore attains a lower bound guarantee 1 −1/e on the GC objective on any dataset and at every coreset size (Section 3). 2 Our contributions are: (1) A new selection principle. We formulate label-free coreset selection as the maximization of a scale-invariant GC objective, defined as the area under the coverage–radius curve, and show that its greedy maximization balances representativeness and diversity without any weighting term. The resulting method, GCcore, is hyperparameter-free and deterministic, applies to the embeddings of any FM, and requires no labels. (2) An a priori guarantee at every budget. We prove that GC is monotone and submodular, so that the greedy solution attains at least 1−1/e of the maximum GC achievable at every coreset size. Its practical value is that pathologists can annotate samples in the order chosen and stop whenever the budget is exhausted, with a certified bound on the coverage of the annotated subset, whereas existing label-free methods generally offer no such as- surance. (3) Comprehensive evaluation and superior performance. We benchmark 15 label-free coreset selection methods on 10 histopathology datasets and three tasks, including segmentation, the most annotation-intensive task in histopathology, on which none of the existing methods had previously been evaluated. GCcore achieves the best overall mean rank (2.2), whereas no baseline is consistently competitive; moreover, the rank of a baseline shifts by 0.9 to 2.1 positions on average, and by up to 4.5 positions on a single dataset, depending solely on its own hyperparameters within recommended intervals (Table 1). 2 RELATED WORK We discuss below the existing coreset selection methods that are label-free by design or that can be adapted into a label-free variant (see Appendix C for a full list), including coverage-based, importance-based, and hybrid methods. Coverage-based selection. Coverage-based methods select samples according to their arrange- ment in the embedding space and differ in the covering criterion they optimize. Clustering-based selection partitions the embedding space into groups and selects representatives from each (Mac- Queen, 1967; Ester et al., 1996; Arthur et al., 2007); how samples are allocated across clusters (uni- formly or proportionally to cluster size) and which representatives are chosen within each cluster (the sample nearest the centroid, or samples stratified by distance from it) are hyperparameters with no principled label-free setting. K-center greedy (Sener & Savarese, 2017) minimizes the radius needed to cover the single least-covered point; the selection is therefore dictated by the extremes of the distribution and systematically favors outliers over representative samples. Importance-based selection. Importance-based methods assign a scalar score to each sample and select samples based on this score alone, thereby treating set-level information as a sum of inde- pendent contributions. The earliest methods derive scores from training dynamics (Toneva et al., 2018; Pleiss et al., 2020; Paul et al., 2021). All require labels and training on the full dataset, and are thus inapplicable to annotation planning. Prototype (Sorscher et al., 2022) clusters features with K-means and scores each sample by its distance to its cluster centroid, keeping atypical samples when the budget is abundant and prototypical samples when it is scarce. Moderate coreset (Xia et al., 2022) instead keeps samples whose distance to their class center is closest to the class median. Without labels, both require the number of clusters, and Prototype additionally requires the budget threshold at which the pruning mode switches. CCS (Zheng et al., 2022) prunes a budget-dependent fraction of the hardest samples by AUM and samples uniformly from equal-width bins of the re- maining score range; the pruning rate and the number of bins must be set for each budget. Finally, ELFS (Zheng et al., 2025) recovers training-dynamics scores through pseudo-labels obtained by deep clustering and prunes both the hardest and the easiest samples. Its selection inherits the errors of the clustering step, depends on the proxy model’s training settings, and requires the number of pseudo-classes and the pruning fraction. Hybrid methods. Hybrid methods account for both the importance of individual samples and the redundancy among selected samples, but predominantly through heuristic procedures that re- quire weighting the two. D2-Pruning (Maharana et al., 2024) builds a k-nearest-neighbor graph over difficulty-weighted samples, propagates neighborhood difficulty into each node’s score by for- ward message passing, and then repeatedly selects the highest-scoring node while discounting its neighbors by reverse message passing. The number of neighbors and the forward and reverse message-passing weights, which balance difficulty against redundancy, must be fixed in advance. ZCore (Griffin et al., 2026) draws random probes in random low-dimensional subspaces, rewards the sample nearest each probe while penal- izing its neighbors, and retains the top-k scorers; the procedure is stochastic and trades runtime against performance through the number of sampling iterations, which must be set together with the dimensionality of the subspaces. In summary, every method above exposes at least one hyperparameter whose value was calibrated on natural-image benchmarks and cannot be re-tuned on a new cohort without the labels that coreset selection is meant to spare. We treat the random seed as a hyperparameter as well, since for stochas- tic methods, such as K-center, K-means and ZCore, it determines the selected subset. Moreover, none of these methods offers an a priori guarantee for an objective that reflects coverage of the data distribution as a whole. A third limitation concerns evaluation: none of these methods have been as- sessed on segmentation, the most annotation-intensive task in histopathology, and the one for which the number of clusters required by clustering-based selection, Prototype and ELFS is hardest to set, since it does not correspond to any observable notion of class. 3 METHODS As illustrated in Fig. 2(a), the overall pipeline consists of three steps. First, the entire un- labeled dataset S = {x1, x2, . . . , xN} is encoded by a pre-trained FM f(·) into embeddings E = {e1, e2, . . . , eN}, where ei = f(xi) ∈RD, N is the number of samples, and D is the feature dimension. Second, our proposed method, the global coverage-based coreset selection algo- rithm (GCcore), identifies a representative subset S′ ⊂S of size |S′| = k ≪N that covers the data distribution in the embedding space through iterative global coverage maximization; the selection mechanism is illustrated in Fig. 2(b) and detailed below. Finally, the selected coreset is annotated by pathologists to provide ground-truth labels, which serve as the basis for supervised training. Collectively, these datasets span prostate, colorectal, breast, lung and gastric tissue, as well as a pan-cancer cohort, with several datasets collected across multiple medical centers. 4.1 EXPERIMENTAL SETUP For each dataset, we first extracted embeddings for all images using an FM. Coresets of varying sizes were then selected from the embeddings of the full training set, and used to train a model comprising a frozen FM as the encoder followed by a trainable task-specific head. Performance was evaluated on the held-out test set. Unless otherwise specified, test performance is measured using QWK for ordinal multiclass classification, balanced accuracy for binary classification, and weighted Dice for segmentation, following the community standard (Ding et al., 2025). For each dataset, we defined four coreset sizes in the low-retention regime (0.01%-20% depending on the dataset, as detailed in Appendix E.1), corresponding to the selection of only a few tens to a few hundred samples for annotation, a quantity that could realistically be annotated by a pathologist in clinical practice. Baselines We compared our deterministic GCcore against 14 label-free baselines. For each base- line we sweep a single characteristic hyperparameter over five values, sampled within the range reported as robust on natural-image benchmarks or around the reported default value, holding the remainder at their default settings, and report the mean test performance across the five configu- rations. We sweep rather than fix a value because the defaults and robust ranges of these hyper- parameters, established on natural images, generally do not transfer reliably to histopathology (see Appendix F.1), so any single value could favor or penalize a baseline. Averaging over five values in- stead estimates the performance a practitioner can expect on a new cohort without labels for tuning, and the standard deviation (SD) quantifies the sensitivity of the baseline to its own hyperparameter. WSI classification For WSI classification, we conducted experiments on PANDA (Bulten et al., 2022), IMP-CRS (Oliveira et al., 2021; Neto et al., 2022; 2024), and TCGA-BRCA datasets. We used the PRISM slide encoder (Shaikovski et al., 2024) to extract the slide embeddings of WSIs. The classification head is a single linear layer that maps a slide embedding e ∈RD to C logits, where D is the native dimensionality of the slide encoder and C is the number of classes defined by the dataset label. Since the classes are mutually exclusive, the logits are normalized by a softmax and the head is trained with class-weighted cross-entropy loss. Tile classification The tile classification task encompasses experiments on four datasets, namely WSSS4LUAD (Han et al., 2022), SICAP (Silva-Rodr´ıguez et al., 2020), CRC-100k (Kather et al., 2018), and DeepHP (Goncalves et al., 2022; Gonc¸alves e Gonc¸alves et al., 2023). We used the H-optimus-1 tile encoder (Scalbert et al., 2026) to extract the embeddings of tiles. For SICAP, CRC-100k and DeepHP, the classification head is identical to that of WSI classification, except that D is the dimensionality of the tile encoder. In WSSS4LUAD the tile labels are not mutually exclusive, since one tile may contain several tissue types; we therefore replace the softmax with C = 3 independent sigmoids applied to the logits of the same linear layer and train with binary cross-entropy loss. Performance on WSSS4LUAD is measured using macro AUROC, averaged over the three classes. It is trained with class-weighted cross-entropy, with unannotated pixels, the ignore class in BEETLE and background in CAMELYON16, excluded from both the loss and the evaluation metrics. 4.2 MAIN RESULTS Tables 1 and 2 show that no single baseline is consistently competitive. GCcore achieves the best mean rank over the 10 datasets, 2.2 out of 15, ahead of random selection (4.3), K-means (4.6), and ELFS-Proto and K-center (both 5.7). It ranks first on six of the 10 datasets and within the top three on nine. Every baseline that is competitive on average is weak somewhere: K-means is best on CRC-100k (1.3) but reaches only 7.0 on CAMELYON16, K-center is best on panCK (3.3) but 11.3 on TCGA-BRCA, and ELFS-Proto is best on SICAP (2.0) but 8.8 on WSSS4LUAD. Table 2 shows the same asymmetry in test performance at the lowest retention rate of each dataset. GCcore achieves the best test performance on seven of the 10 datasets and trails the best method by at most 3.9 points on the remaining three, whereas a competitive baseline that outperforms GCcore on one dataset can be behind GCcore by up to 11.9 points on another (Random, +3.9 on TCGA-BRCA against −11.9 on CRC-100k; ELFS-Proto, +2.9 on SICAP against −11.9 on CRC-100k; K-means, +2.2 on TCGA-BRCA against −8.3 on DeepHP). The subscripts in both tables quantify the sensitivity of a method to its own configuration. For each dataset the subscript is the SD, across five hyperparameter values, of the rank (Table 1) or of the test performance (Table 2) the method obtains. Because GCcore selects its coreset as a deterministic function of the embeddings and exposes no such hyperparameter, this SD is zero on every dataset, whereas the rank SD of a baseline averages 0.9 to 2.1 positions across datasets and reaches 4.5 positions on a single dataset. Choosing the value of M that realizes the advantage requires held-out labels, which is what a clinical low-label setting precludes. See Appendix G.1 for further details. Computational efficiency We measured the selection runtime of GCcore and 14 baselines on a single machine (AMD Ryzen Threadripper PRO 3995WX, 64 cores / 128 threads, 256 GB RAM) using CRC-100k, averaged over five runs. Under low retention rates (20–200 tiles retained from a 100,000-tile training set), GCcore averages 86.4 s and is faster than 12 of the 14 baselines, behind only Random (0.0002 s) and K-center (0.49 s). Selecting 100 tiles, for instance, takes GCcore 88.9 s, against 0.0002 s for random and 0.53 s for K-center. In return, GCcore attains a mean balanced accuracy of 0.905 across the four budgets, against 0.868 for random and 0.875 for K-center. 4.3 ABLATION STUDIES We investigate the robustness of GCcore to the experimental design and to the data: the choice of FM for extracting the embeddings, a discrepancy between the FM used for coreset selection and the FM used for downstream model training, class imbalance in the dataset, the distance used to instantiate the objective, and the embedding dimensionality. Figure 3 summarizes the five studies and Appendix G.2–G.6 give the experimental details and the analysis for each. (a) FM choice (Ta- ble 7). GCcore is the best-ranked of the 15 selection methods under five of the six feature extractors and is never worse than rank 3.0, giving a mean rank of 1.5 against 3.9 for the strongest baseline; the advantage is therefore a property of the selection method rather than of any particular encoder. (b) Selection vs. training FM (Table 8). GCcore attains the best mean rank of all methods (2.7); whenever a stronger FM (PRISM or TITAN) provides the selection embedding, it is among the two best selection methods irrespective of which FM is used for training, so a coreset need not be recom- puted for a different downstream encoder. (c) Class imbalance (Table 9). Across three simulated imbalanced training sets (imbalance ratio 55.7–231.3) GCcore has the best mean rank (3.3) and is the only strategy placed among the two best on all three; on IMB2 it recovers 90.7% of the full-data test performance from 19 WSIs, which Random does not match until 751 WSIs. (d) Distance metric (Table 10). GCcore with the ℓ1 and with the ℓ2 distance both rank ahead of every baseline on aver- age (2.7 and 3.4 among 16, against 5.0 for the strongest baseline), so the advantage follows from the global coverage maximization mechanism rather than from the distance used to instantiate it. Note that the guarantee of Eq. 11 holds for any distance metric. (e) Embedding dimensionality (Ta- ble 11). After principal component analysis (PCA) retaining 99% of the explained variance, which removes 73–85% of the dimensions, GCcore remains the best-ranked method (2.5), suggesting that GCcore can be applied directly to compressed slide representations without re-tuning. 9 5 CONCLUSION To optimize the annotation and training process of DL models in computational pathology, we pro- posed a novel hyperparameter-free coreset selection algorithm, GCcore, that exploits embeddings from any histopathology FM to identify representative samples within heterogeneous and unlabeled histological datasets. Overall, at clinically realistic retention rates, our approach outperformed ex- isting methods and showed a more consistent performance across different cancer types and tasks. GCcore therefore provides a reliable and reproducible strategy for optimal data selection, identify- ing informative subsets that support downstream training while substantially reducing the required labeling effort.
