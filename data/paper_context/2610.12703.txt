2 A Concise Taxonomy of XAI Methodologies In this section, we provide a unified taxonomy and classification of explainable artificial intelligence (XAI) techniques, providing a formal mathematical framework that encompasses both ante-hoc and post-hoc paradigms across local and global scopes. We first define the core notation, decision spaces, and predictive models underlying these methodologies. Definition 1 is deliberately agnostic: the taxonomy below organizes the admissible choices of Φ \Phi according to when it is applied, the scope it describes, the model access it requires, what it returns, and by which mechanism it is computed. The most fundamental split in explainable artificial intelligence involves the timing of explanation, with a distinction between ante-hoc and post-hoc methodologies. Ante-hoc approaches [ 18 ] , often referred to as intrinsic interpretability, involve the use of models that are transparent by design, where the internal logic and decision-making pathways are inherently understandable to a human observer. These methods contrast with post-hoc techniques, which are applied to a model after the training phase has been completed to extract explanations from an otherwise opaque “black-box” architecture [ 7 ] . While the former relies on the structural simplicity of the model to ensure clarity, the latter employs external mechanisms, such as perturbation or gradient analysis, to provide meaningful insights into the learned representations of complex systems without altering the underlying model itself. A second distinction is between Global and Local Interpretable Methods [ 9 ] . Global Methods aim to describe the overall behavior of a model, capturing its general decision logic across the dataset. They provide insights into how input features influence predictions on average, for example, through feature importance measures or partial dependence analysis [ 9 ] . In contrast, local methods focus on explaining individual predictions. These approaches aim to clarify why a model produced a specific output for a given input instance, often by identifying the most influential features or by generating counterfactual examples. The distinction is captured by the domain of the explanation map: a local method returns Φ ⁡ ( f θ , x , c ) \Phi(f_{\theta},x,c) for a fixed x x , whereas a global method returns a single summary Φ glob ​ ( f θ ) = 𝔼 x ∼ p ⁡ ( x ) ​ [ Φ ⁡ ( f θ , x , y ^ ​ ( x ) ) ] \Phi^{\mathrm{glob}}(f_{\theta})=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim p(x)}\bigl[\,\Phi(f_{\theta},x,\hat{y}(x))\bigr] , or an analogous aggregate over the data distribution, in which the dependence on the individual instance has been marginalized out. Local explainability methods are widely used and constitute the main focus of this paper. Model agnosticism refers to whether an explanation method can be applied independently of the underlying model. Model-agnostic methods treat the model as a black-box and can be applied to any model, regardless of its internal structure, e.g., Local Interpretable Model-agnostic Explanations (LIME) [ 19 ] . Conversely, model-specific methods are designed for particular model classes and typically exploit internal information such as gradients, activations, or weights, e.g., Layer-wise Relevance Propagation (LRP) for neural networks [ 20 , 21 ] . The output format or modality of an explanation represents a critical distinction in XAI taxonomies, determining how the interpretation is communicated to the end-user. These formats are generally categorized based on the type of information they provide: • Feature attribution : These methods identify and rank the input features that primarily influenced a specific decision. In computer vision, this is typically achieved through spatial visualizations, such as heatmaps or saliency maps, which highlight the regions or pixels contributing most to the prediction (e.g., via Grad-CAM [ 22 ] or LRP [ 21 ] ). • Textual explanations : With the rise of Large Language Models (LLMs), some approaches generate natural language justifications. These methods produce human-readable descriptions of the reasoning behind a prediction, often utilizing chain-of-thought (CoT) style prompting. • Example-based explanations : These approaches explain predictions by selecting representative samples from the training set. They typically identify prototypes (examples structurally similar to the input) or criticisms (examples that contrast with it) to explain a decision through analogy. Furthermore, feature attribution methods can be classified into two main categories based on their underlying computational mechanism: perturbation-based and propagation-based approaches. Perturbation/Occlusion-based methods represent the most intuitive approach for probing a black-box model. These techniques function by systematically masking, removing, or modifying specific input features, executing a forward pass to compute the new output, and measuring the resulting deviation from the original prediction. Most of the XAI frameworks evaluated in the remainder of this paper fall precisely within the taxonomic intersection of post-hoc, local methods that utilize a feature attribution output modality derived through propagation-based mechanisms. 4 Datasets In this study, we selected datasets from completely different domains to evaluate different models. In particular, the datasets also include, for each sample, gaze heatmaps recorded for each image via eye-tracking. This choice is not accidental, since the study of the human gaze is a key element in understanding the extent to which models can replicate human perceptual processes. The importance of having data relating to the human gaze lies in the large amount of information they contain. Given the overwhelming volume of data and objects around us, it is necessary to ensure that the available resources are dedicated to what is most important to understand the context in which we find ourselves, since any model, whether computational, biological or digital, has limited capabilities. For this reason, in recent years the demand for data of this type has grown significantly in the fields of computer vision, natural language processing, decision-making learning, and robotics. In the field of computer vision, in particular, it is essential to extract the most “salient” visual characteristics from images [ 46 ] . However, since eye-tracking devices and software are expensive, datasets providing this type of freely accessible data are scarce in the literature. Therefore, in the following, we describe in more detail the datasets used in this study, which include annotations of human gazes in support of our analysis. 4.1 CUB dataset This dataset consists of 11,788 images ( 448 × 448 448\times 448 ) belonging to 200 species of birds (about 60 images for each species) [ 47 ] . It was chosen because it has been widely used in several computer vision and explainable AI fine-grained classification tasks [ 48 , 49 , 50 ] The peculiarities of this dataset lie in the local differences between each class, consequently making human attention more unified than when observing significantly different classes that have scattered differences. Regarding the human gaze data, those provided by [ 51 ] were used. These were collected and analyzed using the Tobii Spectrum Eye Tracker (1200 Hz) with chin support to ensure greater precision during eye recordings. Twenty-five subjects participated, all with no specific knowledge of ornithology, to collect the gaze data. In particular, each participant is shown two images belonging to different classes but with similar characteristics to each other, and must observe the distinctive differences between them. In a second phase, a single photo belonging to one of the two previous classes is shown, and the participant must classify the image. This second phase lasts only 3 seconds, so that the participant focuses only on the salient areas of the image. The raw gaze data is preprocessed to extract fixation locations using the Velocity-Threshold Identification (I-VT) algorithm [ 52 ] . The resulting fixation points offered in the dataset include coordinates and duration information. Based on this information, saliency maps for human gaze are generated. More information can be found in the original paper [ 51 ] . It is worth noticing that relying on non-expert participants introduces a potential bias related to the specific image pairs shown during the sessions. We hypothesize that, while an ornithologist would possess a priori knowledge of which anatomical details to observe, novices may rely entirely on the immediate visual comparison. Consequently, the precision of their attention is highly dependent on the similarity of the compared birds; comparing highly similar species might guide their gaze toward precise, discriminative features, whereas comparing more visually distinct species could result in a more scattered attention. 4.2 CXR dataset This dataset consists of 1083 chest x-ray images ( 3000 × 3000 3000\times 3000 ) from the MIMIC-CXR dataset [ 53 , 54 ] . The images are classified into three distinct categories: Normal (indicating a healthy condition), Pneumonia, and Congestive Heart Failure (CHF). This dataset was selected because it differs significantly from the one described in Subsection 4.1 , allowing us to evaluate the same models and explainability methods within a medical domain. Many studies [ 55 , 9 ] have used explainability techniques to validate classification results produced by neural network architectures, without having access to ground-truth human gaze data to assess the findings. Instead, for the creation of this dataset, the fixation data were acquired using a Gazepoint GP3 eye-tracking system (60 Hz sampling rate) mounted on a monitor with a resolution of 1920 × 1080 1920\times 1080 . The data collection involved a single American Board of Radiology certified radiologist with over five years of experience. To ensure an objective visual assessment, the expert performed a “cold reading” of 1,083 frontal chest X-rays, blinded to the patients’ specific clinical history aside from the emergency department setting. The recording process was divided into sessions of 30 cases to minimize fatigue, with a 9-point calibration procedure performed at the beginning of each session to ensure tracking accuracy. More information can be found in the original paper [ 53 ] . 4.3 Uniss-FGD dataset This dataset introduces a completely different domain focusing on human faces and expressions. It consists of 120 high-quality facial images selected from the Karolinska Directed Emotional Faces (KDEF) database [ 56 , 13 ] . In the following, we use the term KDEF-FGD to refer to the dataset comprising both the images and their corresponding human gaze annotations. The images depict 40 different individuals (20 female and 20 male), each displaying three distinct expressions: happy, sad, and neutral. The inclusion of this dataset serves to evaluate how models and explainability methods behave when confronted with human faces, a highly salient stimulus that naturally drives human visual attention. The human gaze data were collected using a Tobii Pro TX300 Eye Tracker, which ensures high precision with a sampling frequency of 300 Hz. A total of 20 observers (10 female and 10 male), recruited among university students and academic staff, participated in the recording sessions. During the experiment, participants were asked to freely view the sequence of faces, with each image displayed on the screen for exactly 3 seconds. This free-viewing task, without specific recognition constraints, allows capturing natural human attention patterns as they vary across different facial expressions [ 13 ] . Notably, since recognizing basic human emotions does not require specific prior training or domain expertise, relying on a general audience reliably reflects innate human visual processing. However, it is essential to consider the demographic constraints of the dataset, as the original study highlights that both culture and age significantly influence visual attention patterns on faces. Since the data were predominantly gathered from a cohort of young Western adults (mostly aged 19–24), the resulting gaze patterns might not generalize to non-Western populations or to different age groups, such as children and the elderly [ 13 ] . This choice was dictated by the fact that through skip connections, the gradient flows more easily during backpropagation. This solves the vanishing gradient problem that can affect deeper networks, such as VGG. To correctly use the network with the different datasets, the last layer (adapted to the 1000 ImageNet classes) was removed and replaced with a new linear layer to classify the required classes. The new layer is initialized with Kaiming (He Initialization) to maintain stable variance at the beginning of training [ 61 ] . This method accounts for the non-linearity of ReLU activation functions by scaling the weights according to the number of input units, thereby preventing the signal magnitude from diminishing as it propagates through the final stages of the network. Images are resized to 448 × 448 448\times 448 pixels and augmentation is performed only with random resized crop and random horizontal flip. To analyze and understand network decisions, the XAI techniques used are Grad-CAM [ 22 ] , Score-CAM [ 62 ] and Ablation-CAM [ 33 ] , i.e. the operators defined in Eqs. ( 9 ), ( 11 ) and ( 12 ), all instantiated on the output of the last convolutional stage ( h = w = 14 h=w=14 at the input resolution used here). Regarding Vision Mamba, we used the ViM-Base model pretrained on ImageNet-1K, whose layers implement the selective recurrence of Eq. ( 16 ) applied to the sequence of image patches scanned along both directions. We also experimented with smaller variants (e.g., ViM-Tiny and ViM-Small); however, their accuracy on the evaluated datasets was substantially lower, preventing a meaningful analysis of their explainability. For Vision Mamba fine-tuning, different steps are performed: 1. Input : To ensure compatibility between the pretrained model and the new architecture, the positional embeddings are interpolated to match the spatial resolution required by the current patch configuration. The extra tokens (such as class tokens) are preserved, while only the spatial positional tokens are reshaped and resized using bicubic interpolation. This procedure allows the model to reuse pretrained positional information even when the number of patches differs from that of the original checkpoint (in our experiments, from 224 × 224 224\times 224 to 448 × 448 448\times 448 ). 2. Output : The last layer was removed and replaced by a new linear layer to classify the required classes. Xavier uniform initialization was applied to initialize these new model parameters. This choice accounts for the linear transformations and gating mechanisms of the Mamba architecture. By preserving signal variance across the state-space sequences, Xavier initialization prevents parameter saturation and ensures stable gradient flow, which is critical for balancing the dynamics of the classification head with the pretrained SSM layers. 3. Inner layers : All model parameters were frozen except for the head and the [CLS] token. In additional experiments, we explored partially unfreezing only the SSM layers, as well as applying LoRA (Low-Rank Adaptation) [ 63 ] , a parameter-efficient fine-tuning technique, to the projection layer; however, these strategies did not yield significant performance improvements. We used the Adam optimizer with decoupled weight decay regularization [ 64 ] , rather than the classic stochastic gradient descent with momentum. To analyze and understand network decisions, the XAI technique used is MambaLRP [ 40 ] , with the conservation-preserving rule of Eq. ( 17 ). Regarding Transformer-based models, we adopted the ViT-Base architecture [ 11 ] , pretrained on the ImageNet 2012 dataset. For this model, we followed a fine-tuning strategy similar to the one used for ViM-Base. In particular, we employed bicubic interpolation to adapt the positional embeddings, allowing the use of higher-resolution input images during the fine-tuning phase without losing spatial consistency. Moreover, to reduce the risk of overfitting and to preserve the rich visual representations learned during pretraining, we froze all the backbone layers of the network. To adapt the architecture to the target task, we replaced the original MLP head with a new linear classification layer projecting the representations to the class logits. Only the classification head and the [CLS] token were left trainable, enabling the model to adapt to the target task while keeping the majority of the pretrained parameters fixed (in tables 6 - 7 with name ViT-Base-F , where F stands for frozen). The same architecture is trained without freezing any parameters. The Adam optimizer with cosine annealing learning rate schedule was used. To analyze and understand the network’s decisions, the XAI technique described in [ 37 ] , referred to in this paper as ViTLRP, i.e. the operator of Eq. ( 13 ), is used. The selection of vanilla architectures, namely ResNet-50, ViT-Base, and ViM-Base, rather than more complex, heavily optimized state-of-the-art variants, is a deliberate methodological choice driven by two primary factors. First, it ensures the isolation of the underlying computational paradigms (convolutional, attention-based, and state-space modeling, respectively) without introducing architectural biases or specialized modules that could compromise the comparative analysis. Second, the reliable application of XAI techniques remains an open challenge. Methods such as Grad-CAM and LRP rely on traceable gradient flows and standard structural assumptions to generate faithful attribution maps. Custom architectures risk violating these assumptions, making it difficult to discern whether an ambiguous saliency map stems from the model’s opaque decision-making or a failure of the XAI algorithm itself. By utilizing these canonical baseline models, we guarantee a higher theoretical fidelity for the XAI methods, ensuring that our comparative evaluation reflects the true explainability of the core architectures. 6 Experimental Analysis This section presents the core empirical evaluation of our study, aimed at quantifying the alignment between machine-generated saliency maps and human visual attention. To move beyond purely qualitative assessments, we deploy an experimental framework across three representative architectural families—CNNs, ViTs, and Vision Mamba—and test them across three distinct visual domains: fine-grained natural classification, medical diagnostics, and facial emotion recognition. The primary objective of this initial validation is to demonstrate that the baseline architectures, despite lacking the extreme optimizations of highly specialized state-of-the-art variants, achieve solid and competitive classification performance. Establishing this baseline is crucial, as it legitimizes the subsequent XAI benchmark: interpreting model attention against human gaze is only meaningful if the underlying model has learned robust and accurate representations of the data. 6.3.1 Classification Metrics The AUC (Multi-Class OVR) metric is computed by applying the One-vs-Rest (OVR) strategy to the multi-class setting. For each class c ∈ { 1 , … , K } c\in\{1,\dots,K\} , a binary classifier is formed by considering class c c as the positive class and all other classes as the negative class. A ROC curve is then generated for each class, and its corresponding AUC value, denoted as AUC c \mathrm{AUC}_{c} , is computed. Denoting by ℐ c + = { n : y n = c } \mathcal{I}^{+}_{c}=\{n:y_{n}=c\} and ℐ c − = { n : y n ≠ c } \mathcal{I}^{-}_{c}=\{n:y_{n}\neq c\} the positive and negative index sets and by s n , c = f θ ^ ​ ( x n ) c s_{n,c}=f_{\hat{\theta}}(x_{n})_{c} the score assigned to class c c , the class-wise AUC admits the Mann–Whitney form: AUC c = 1 | ℐ c + | ​ | ℐ c − | ∑ i ∈ ℐ c + ∑ j ∈ ℐ c − ( 𝟏 [ s i , c > s j , c ] + 1 2 1 [ s i , c = s j , c ] ) , \begin{split}\mathrm{AUC}_{c}&{}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{I}^{+}_{c}||\mathcal{I}^{-}_{c}|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}^{+}_{c}}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{I}^{-}_{c}}\Bigl(\mathbf{1}\bigl[s_{i,c}>s_{j,c}\bigr]\\ &\qquad\qquad\qquad\quad{}+\tfrac{1}{2}\,\mathbf{1}\bigl[s_{i,c}=s_{j,c}\bigr]\Bigr),\end{split} (29) i.e. it estimates the probability that a randomly drawn positive sample receives a higher score than a randomly drawn negative one, which is exactly the area under the corresponding ROC curve and makes the metric invariant to any strictly increasing transformation of the scores. When comparing our baseline classification results on the KDEF dataset (Table 5 ) to recent literature, it is important to contextualize state-of-the-art performance claims. For instance, the multi-domain fusion transformer proposed by Shu and Wang [ 67 ] achieves a remarkable accuracy of 99.7 % 99.7\% on KDEF. This impressive performance is obtained through a 70/30 train-validation split tailored for model selection and hyperparameter tuning, which effectively optimizes the architecture for that specific data distribution. Additionally, their framework introduces a sophisticated multi-branch design incorporating spatial, channel, and frequency domain fusions. While highly effective for maximizing nominal accuracy, such architectural complexity can pose inherent challenges for certain XAI techniques when tracing gradient flows, as discussed in Section 5 . Therefore, maintaining standard canonical baselines in our experiments represents a deliberate methodological choice to ensure the theoretical fidelity and interpretability of the generated saliency maps, prioritizing architectural transparency for explanatory purposes. Ensemble Aggregation (Q4). We also investigated ensembles composed of pairs of neural networks, aiming to determine whether architectures with similar inductive biases lead to improved performance when combined, as well as investigating whether models exhibiting different explainability gazes can achieve improved classification performance when combined. We adopted a sum rule strategy for ensemble inference: given the softmax output vectors of each model, these were summed element-wise, and the final prediction was obtained by applying the arg ⁡ max \arg\max operator to the resulting vector. Formally, given M M models with softmax outputs 𝐩 k \mathbf{p}_{k} , the ensemble prediction y ^ \hat{y} is defined as: y ^ ens ​ ( x ) = arg ⁡ max c ∈ 𝒴 ​ 1 M ​ ∑ k = 1 M f θ ^ k ​ ( x ) c , \hat{y}_{\mathrm{ens}}(x)=\arg\max_{c\in\mathcal{Y}}\ \frac{1}{M}\sum_{k=1}^{M}f_{\hat{\theta}_{k}}(x)_{c}\,, (34) where the normalization by M M is immaterial for the arg ⁡ max \arg\max but makes the aggregate a member of Δ K − 1 \Delta^{K-1} , so that the ensemble is itself a classifier in ℱ \mathcal{F} and Eq. ( 20 ) applies to it verbatim. We use M M rather than K K for the number of models to avoid a collision with the number of classes. Averaging posteriors attenuates the component of the estimation error that is zero-mean and uncorrelated across members, while leaving the shared bias untouched; the sum rule is therefore expected to help precisely when the errors of the members are weakly correlated, which is the hypothesis empirically tested below. Experimental results show that, on the CUB dataset, the ensemble composed of a fine-tuned Transformer with frozen and unfrozen backbone models achieved higher performance compared to the corresponding individual models. This indicates that combining models with the same architecture but different training strategies can effectively improve generalization. Additionally, the ensemble formed by ViT and Mamba architectures also yielded competitive results, suggesting that these two models provide complementary representations despite their architectural differences. In contrast, the ensemble combining CNN-based models and ViTs did not lead to performance improvements. This outcome suggests that the representations learned by these architectures may be less complementary in this setting, limiting the effectiveness of simple ensemble aggregation strategies. However, experiments on the KDEF-FGD dataset reveal a significantly different behavior regarding cross-architecture ensembles. Unlike in CUB, combining a CNN-based model with either ViTs or Mamba architectures led to substantial performance gains. Notably, the ResNet50 + ViM-base ensemble achieved the highest accuracy among our configurations at 99.29%, effectively outperforming the individual ResNet50 (96.32%) and ViM-base (97.24%) models. Similarly, the ResNet50 + ViT-base ensemble reached 99.08%. This indicates that for the KDEF-FGD domain, the local feature extraction of CNNs and the global context capture of ViT or ViM provide highly complementary representations, making simple ensemble strategies highly effective.
