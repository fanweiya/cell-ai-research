Email: kesi.xu@warwick.ac.uk 2 Abstract Programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) tumor proportion score (TPS) informs immunotherapy selection in non-small cell lung cancer. Pathologists must distinguish tumor cells from stained immune cells and assess membranous staining in heterogeneous tissue. Automated scoring can provide a repeatable TPS estimate. Near the 1% and 50% cutoffs, the score should also indicate when closer review is warranted. We designed a three-stage framework to follow the sequence of TPS assessment. Stage 1 identifies tumor regions and excludes macrophage-rich patches. Stage 2, a dual-branch cell counting network (D2Count-Net), counts PD-L1-positive and PD-L1-negative tumor cells in these regions to calculate TPS. Stage 3, TPS-Guard, an uncertainty-guided triage mechanism, reports model uncertainty as the disagreement among independently trained models, refers the most uncertain slides, and flags scores near the 1% or 50% cutoff when model estimates disagree across that cutoff. Flags state the reason for referral or alert the pathologist to possible scoring errors on auto-reported slides. In a retrospective, single-center study, 154 whole-slide images were scored by three pathologists, and the model and each pathologist were compared in turn with the mean of the other two. On the 114 slides unseen in training, model mean absolute error (MAE) was 6.96 percentage points, within the range of the three pathologists (4.24 to 8.29), and model uncertainty correlated with absolute TPS error (Spearman ρ = 0.77) and inter- pathologist disagreement (ρ = 0.71). After referral of the 15% most uncertain slides, model and pathologist MAE both decreased. Applied without retraining to an external, publicly available dataset, TPS-Guard still directed review to higher-error cases, and referring more uncertain cases raised sensitivity at the 50% cutoff. TPS-Guard provides reasons for reviewing individual automated scores and identifies slides that also produce greater disagreement among pathologists. Prospective reader studies should assess TPS-Guard’s effect on TPS reporting. Keywords: PD-L1, tumor proportion score, non-small cell lung cancer, deep learning, uncertainty- guided triage, digital pathology 3 Programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) expression in tumor cells informs the selection of pembrolizumab monotherapy in advanced non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC).1 PD-L1 expression is assessed by immunohistochemistry (IHC), with several antibody clones in use, including 22C3, 28-8, SP263 and SP142. 22C3, 28-8 and SP263 show broadly comparable tumor cell staining in NSCLC, whereas SP142 stains fewer tumor cells.2 The development cohort in this study was stained with the VENTANA PD-L1 (SP263) assay. The external test set, from a public dataset, contained SP263-stained regions and regions stained with E1L3N, a clone used in laboratory-developed tests that have been evaluated against the 22C3 and SP263 assays.3 Expression is reported as the tumor proportion score (TPS), the percentage of viable tumor cells with partial or complete membranous staining of any intensity.4 The 1% and 50% thresholds inform treatment selection in the appropriate clinical context.1,5 Scoring differences near these thresholds can therefore affect eligibility for a treatment option, particularly when the available material is a small biopsy. Accurate TPS assessment depends on identifying viable tumor, separating tumor staining from staining in immune cells and macrophages, and recognizing any degree of membranous positivity including weak or incomplete staining.4 Variable cellularity and heterogeneous staining further complicate estimation across a section. Agreement between pathologists varies across clinical cutoffs,2,6–8 and differences between readers can exceed differences between assays.6 Automated scores must therefore be evaluated alongside the variation already present in human assessment. Automated TPS methods have used region-based or cell-based image analysis, including tumor- region segmentation and tumor-cell detection and classification.9–14 Hondelink et al.11 combined tumor segmentation with cell detection across whole-slide images (WSIs), while van Eekelen et al.12 evaluated cell detection and TPS agreement across centers, assays, and scanners. Agreement across a cohort, however, does not identify which individual estimates need closer examination or explain why a score near a treatment threshold may be unreliable. Studies have also examined how artificial intelligence (AI) could support pathologists in TPS assessment. In 584 tissue microarray samples, Ito et al.15 found no statistically significant overall improvement with AI assistance, but reported higher scoring accuracy among cases with discordant pathologist scores. Makhlouf et al.16 proposed referral for manual review when automated TPS fell within specified intervals near clinical cutoffs. Building on these approaches, 4 we examine whether disagreement across models can help select TPS estimates for review, and whether cutoff-specific flags can give the reason for review. Uncertainty estimation and selective classification offer a way to rank individual predictions for additional review.17,18 For TPS, disagreement between models can signal instability in the numerical estimate. Proximity to a cutoff poses a separate problem: a small error may change the reported category even when models agree. A review strategy should therefore consider both variability in model predictions and evidence of possible threshold crossing. We developed a framework that follows the sequence of TPS assessment: identifying the tumor area, estimating positive and negative tumor cell counts, and selecting cases for review. A dual- branch cell counting network combining detection and density estimation (D2Count-Net) accommodates variation in tumor cellularity. TPS-Guard uses disagreement across models to select slides for review and risk flags around the 1% and 50% cutoffs to record the reason for review. We evaluated associations with model error and disagreement among pathologists in 114 WSIs, and examined case selection in external regions of interest. 1. Materials and Methods 1.1 Study Cohort and Data Collection 1.1.1 Algorithm Development Cohort This retrospective study at University Hospitals Coventry and Warwickshire (UHCW) used de- identified archival images under Research Ethics Committee (REC) approval, as detailed in Ethics Approval and Consent to Participate. The cohort comprised 154 PD-L1 IHC WSIs of NSCLC with paired hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) WSIs. The PD-L1 slides were stained with the VENTANA PD-L1 (SP263) Assay (Ventana Medical Systems, Inc., Tucson, AZ, USA) and digitized on a Philips Pathology Scanner SG300 (Philips Medical Systems Nederland B.V., Best, the Netherlands) at 40× magnification (0.25 μm/pixel). Table 1 summarizes the cohort characteristics. Three anonymized pathology readers (P1, P2, P3) independently scored TPS for the 154 WSIs in the analysis cohort. The reader panel comprised pathologists with consultant-level National Health Service (NHS) diagnostic pathology experience in thoracic or surgical pathology and registrar- level surgical pathology experience, with varying levels of PD-L1 reporting experience. 5 Slides were reviewed using WASABI, a web-based slide viewer built on the Digital Slide Archive.19 During scoring, pathologists could not see the other readers’ scores, the algorithm output or their own previous TPS scores for the same case. They had access to the original diagnostic indication, but no other clinical information, treatment response data or outcomes. Following routine diagnostic practice, each pathologist reviewed the paired H&E and PD-L1 slides side by side and assigned a TPS from 0% to 100%. Range reports such as <1% were encoded as described in Supplementary Section S1. The algorithm used only the PD-L1 IHC slide. The arithmetic mean of the three independent TPS scores (the three-reader mean) served as the reference for continuous scores and cutoff classifications. Three of P1’s scores (WSIs 152, 371 and 400) were corrected by P1 after a check of slides with large differences between readers. All other scores are the original independent scores, and no consensus round was held. Results with the original scores are given in Supplementary Table S1. Section 1.11 defines the reader comparisons. 1.1.2 External Validation Cohort The external cohort was used to test the model on a second staining clone and on other centers and scanners. The ROI- only release limited evaluation to cell counting and excluded Stage 1. Unlike the prior WSI-level analysis, we derived a continuous TPS for each ROI from cell counts. From the annotated regions, 1,115 non- overlapping patches (224 × 224 pixels, 20×) were extracted. Of these, 235 contained no tumor cells, reflecting tumor–stroma boundary patches. The 42 WSIs were split into five outer folds by slide, and each patch was evaluated by the fold models that had not seen its source slide. Forty of the cell-annotated WSIs are also in the analysis cohort of 154 WSIs. Each of these 40 WSIs was scored by the three fold models, one per training run (Section 1.4), that excluded it from training and validation. The other 114 WSIs were not used for cell-level training. We report two evaluation frames: the full cohort of 154 WSIs and the 114 WSIs without cell-level annotations, which form the primary analysis. These model assignments determine the final TPS estimate; model uncertainty and patch-derived risk features use outputs from all 15 cross-validation models, as specified in Supplementary Section S2. For each of the 40 cell-annotated WSIs, 12 of these 15 models used its annotated regions for training or validation, so uncertainty-based referral is fully held out only in the 114 WSIs. 1.4 External Validation and Experimental Design The cell analysis model was evaluated on the external test set under three conditions: direct application without retraining (zero-shot transfer), fine-tuning on the external cohort, and external- only training. Three independent training runs on the development cohort each produced five cross-validation models, with WSIs kept separate between training and validation within each fold. Each run supplied models for both direct application and fine-tuning. External-only training used one ensemble of five models. Results for direct application and fine-tuning are reported for the 15 models, with cell counts averaged across models. The external training, validation, and test 9 partitions contained 185 ROIs from 44 patients, 61 ROIs from 10 patients, and 98 ROIs from 21 patients, respectively, with no patient overlap. 1. Application without retraining (zero-shot transfer). The development-trained models were applied directly to the external test ROIs without adaptation. 2. Fine-tuning. Each development-trained model was adapted to the external training/validation split using the same multi-task loss. Cell counts from the 15 adapted models were averaged. The 15 models shared one external training/validation partition and differed in development-cohort initialization. 3. External-only training. Five models were trained independently on the external training/validation split, starting from ImageNet-pretrained encoder weights and using the same architecture as the development-cohort models. Cell counts were averaged across the five models and evaluated on the same held-out ROI test set. For each external ROI, TPS was calculated from counts aggregated across non-overlapping patches of 224 × 224 pixels. Training settings and TPS calculation details are given in Supplementary Sections S3.2 and S2. 1.5 Stage 1: Tumor Region Segmentation Virchow221, a vision transformer pretrained on histopathology images, was frozen as the patch- level feature extractor. On 30 WSIs, annotations marked invasive tumor, non-tumor (stroma, normal lung, and other benign elements), and macrophage-rich intratumoral regions. The third class captures PD-L1-positive macrophages that can mimic positive tumor cells. A linear classifier on the Virchow2 class token features assigned each 224 × 224-pixel patch at 20× to one of these three classes. At inference, patches classified as macrophage-rich were merged into non-tumor and excluded from Stage 2. Because exclusion operates at patch level, tumor cells within mixed patches may also be omitted. 1.6 Stage 2: D2Count-Net Cell Analysis Within the segmented tumor region, D2Count-Net processes non-overlapping 224 × 224-pixel patches at 20× magnification. A shared EfficientNet-B0 encoder22 feeds the detection and density branches, each implemented as a U-Net-style decoder.23 The branches provide two estimates of 10 cell counts for tissue fields with different cellularity. Pathologists annotated 50,615 nuclei on 1,115 patches from 42 WSIs, of which 3,152 were PD-L1-positive tumor cells (6.2%), 18,060 PD-L1- negative tumor cells and 29,403 non-tumor cells (Supplementary Figure S4). The detection branch marks individual cells and classifies each as PD-L1-positive tumor, PD-L1- negative tumor, or non-tumor. This branch is designed to count cells that can be resolved individually. The density branch predicts continuous density maps for positive and negative tumor cells, with counts obtained by spatial integration. This branch provides a complementary estimate for crowded fields where individual cells are difficult to separate. A tissue scene classifier estimates the probability that each patch belongs to one of four tumor cellularity categories: background, low, medium, or high. A fixed mapping converts these probabilities into weights for the detection and density branches. This weighting favors detection in sparsely cellular fields and density estimation in crowded fields. Supplementary Section S3.1 gives the equations and fixed priors, and Supplementary Figure S2 illustrates the architecture and fusion. 1.7 Slide-Level TPS Calculation Slide-level TPS is computed by aggregating cell counts across all tumor patches: 𝑇𝑃𝑆𝑠𝑙𝑖𝑑𝑒 = 𝛴 𝑁𝑝,𝑖 𝛴 (𝑁𝑝,𝑖 + 𝑁𝑛,𝑖) × 100% (1) where the summation is over all patches 𝑖 within the segmented tumor region, and 𝑁𝑝,𝑖 and 𝑁𝑛,𝑖 are the fused positive and negative tumor cell counts, respectively. Each of the 40 cell-annotated WSIs is scored by its three held-out fold models, one from each training run. For the other 114 WSIs, positive and negative tumor cell counts are averaged across the 15 models before TPS is calculated from these averages. Non-tumor cells (immune cells, stroma, and macrophages) are excluded from both numerator and denominator, consistent with the PD-L1 TPS definition. 1.8 Uncertainty Quantification and Referral Model uncertainty was measured as the standard deviation (SD) of TPS estimates from the 15 independently trained cross-validation models. This SD describes disagreement among model estimates for a given slide. We retrospectively assessed the association of model uncertainty with absolute TPS error and inter-reader disagreement. Reader scores are not required to calculate 11 model uncertainty. Supplementary Section S2 details the TPS calculations used for development WSIs and external ROIs. Cases are ranked by model uncertainty, with the highest values referred for pathologist review. The referral rate is the proportion of slides referred for pathologist review. In the proposed workflow, the pathologist remains responsible for the final TPS report for every slide. 1.9 Fusion Weights The four scene priors and their mapping to the detection weight were fixed across the development and external cohorts. We assessed the contribution of scene-based weighting by recalculating TPS from the same branch outputs with equal weights for the detection and density branches (Supplementary Section S3). 1.10 Stage 3: Uncertainty-Guided Triage with TPS-Guard TPS-Guard adds cutoff-specific flags to the uncertainty ranking described in Section 1.8. A small numerical error near the 1% or 50% threshold can change the reported TPS category, even when model uncertainty is low. TPS-Guard gives three reasons to review an automated score. The flag for possible over-scoring at 1% applies to fused TPS from 1% to less than 5% when model estimates straddle 1%, the fused estimate falls within a fixed margin above 1%, or predicted positive tumor cells are few or concentrated in a few patches; such a slide may be negative at the 1% cutoff. The flag for possible under-scoring at 50% applies to fused TPS from 20% to less than 50% when a component or alternative estimate reaches 50%, or high-TPS patches account for a substantial share of predicted tumor cells; such a slide may have a TPS of 50% or more. The flag for limited tumor-cell sampling identifies scores based on few predicted tumor cells or scoring patches, with additional rules for specimen type and proximity to a clinical cutoff. Supplementary Section S2 specifies the full criteria. TPS-Guard selects the 15% of slides with the highest model uncertainty for review. On these referred slides, the flags give the reason for review; on the auto-reported slides, they are reported as advisory labels, and a flag never forces referral. Exact ranking and rounding rules are given in Supplementary Section S2. The three reasons (possible over-scoring at 1%, possible under-scoring 12 at 50%, or limited tumor-cell sampling) can be considered alongside the TPS estimate and the slide. The rules were derived on the development cohort using model outputs and specimen type. Sensitivity analyses on the 114 WSIs without cell-level annotations and confidence intervals are reported in Supplementary Section S6 and Supplementary Table S2. Supplementary Section S7 reports matched-slide reader comparisons and a score-substitution simulation for uncertainty ranking (the main analysis) without flags. 1.11 Statistical Analysis Agreement between the model and the reference was summarized by mean absolute error (MAE) in TPS percentage points, the intraclass correlation coefficient for absolute agreement (two-way random effects, single measures; ICC(2,1)),24 and Bland–Altman analysis.25 At the 1% and 50% cutoffs, sensitivity and specificity were the proportions of reference-positive and reference- negative cases correctly classified, respectively. Three-tier accuracy was the proportion assigned to the correct TPS category (<1%, 1% to <50%, or ≥50%). On the development cohort, estimated TPS was compared with the mean of three readers. Two reader benchmarks were used. The pairwise benchmark averaged agreement across the three pairs of pathologists. In the leave-one-reader-out benchmark, the model and the held-out pathologist were each compared with the mean of the remaining two pathologists, so that both were judged against the same reference. Reader comparisons were recalculated for every set of slides analyzed. The associations of model uncertainty with absolute model error and with inter-pathologist disagreement (the population SD of the three reader scores) were assessed by Spearman correlation, with two-sided P values. One-sided permutation tests (10,000 permutations) assessed whether flagged slides had higher rates of the specified error events than all 114 WSIs without cell-level annotations. Each flag was evaluated at its own cutoff: for possible over-scoring at 1%, a reference TPS below 1% with an estimated TPS of 1% or more; for possible under-scoring at 50%, a reference TPS of 50% or more with an estimated TPS below 50%. P values were reported without adjustment for the two permutation tests. Ninety-five percent confidence intervals (CIs) were obtained by percentile bootstrap resampling (2,000 resamples unless stated otherwise). Resampling used slides in the development cohort and patients in the external cohort, retaining all ROIs from each sampled patient. The external 13 comparison of fine-tuning with application without retraining used a paired patient bootstrap (10,000 resamples). Training and resampling settings are given in Supplementary Section S3.2. 2. Results 2.1 Model–Expert Agreement Slides auto-reported after referral of the 15% most uncertain slides had lower TPS error and less reader disagreement than all 114 slides. Before referral, model MAE against the three-reader mean was 6.70 percentage points (95% CI 4.66 to 8.97) across the 114 WSIs without cell-level annotations; the mean pairwise difference between pathologists was 6.41. Against the mean of the other two readers, the three pathologists had MAEs of 4.24, 5.49 and 8.29 points and the model 6.96, a difference from a single pathologist of 0.95 points (95% CI −0.53 to 2.48), about one- seventh of the mean disagreement between pathologists (6.41 points). At the 50% cutoff the model and a single pathologist placed 5.6% and 6.1% of slides in a different category from the mean of the other two readers; at the 1% cutoff, 19.9% and 17.5%. TPS-Guard selected 17 slides (14.9% of 114 WSIs) with the highest model uncertainty for review. On the 97 auto-reported slides, model MAE against the three-reader mean was 4.96 points and the mean pairwise difference between pathologists 4.93; against the mean of the other two readers, the model (5.18) and a single pathologist (4.61) differed by 0.57 points (95% CI −0.71 to 2.03; Table 2; Supplementary Table S1). Where tumor cells were well separated, the detection branch counted most of the annotated tumor cells. In crowded fields it counted fewer tumor cells than the pathologist, whereas the density count stayed close to the annotated count and the fused count lay between the two. Discussion 3.1 Model Uncertainty and Reader Disagreement Slides referred on the basis of model uncertainty also showed greater disagreement among pathologists. Model uncertainty correlated with reader disagreement, and selecting the most uncertain slides for review reduced disagreement among the auto-reported slides (Figure 5D; Supplementary Table S4). This finding gives the uncertainty ranking a clinically relevant role: directing attention to slides that are difficult to score consistently. TPS-Guard uses this ranking to select slides for review and adds cutoff-specific flags that give the reason for review or an advisory on auto-reported slides. Published automated PD-L1 TPS studies use different reference standards, metrics, and uncertainty-reporting conventions. Kapil et al.9 evaluated SP263-stained NSCLC needle biopsies against the median of three readers. Pan et al.14 used a multistage ensemble strategy on SP263- and 22C3-stained NSCLC samples. Van Eekelen et al.12 assessed an AI system in a multicenter reader study of 100 WSIs, reporting both continuous and categorical TPS agreement. These differences in specimens, references, and agreement measures limit direct numerical comparisons (Supplementary Table S8). Our study relates uncertainty in individual TPS estimates to both automated error and reader disagreement, alongside performance at the clinical cutoffs. Makhlouf et al.16 described a PD-L1 workflow that directs cases near clinical cutoffs to manual review. TPS- Guard builds on this review-oriented use of automated scoring by combining model uncertainty with branch disagreement, fusion-weight sensitivity, and sampling-related flags. Comparisons with existing referral strategies will require a shared evaluation cohort. 23 3.2 Branch Complementarity and Scene-Aware Fusion Fusion weights reflect tumor cellularity rather than PD-L1 positivity. In reference-negative slides, incorporating detection counts can increase TPS error relative to density estimation alone. The gain from fusion over equal weighting was small on the development cohort (Supplementary Section S3), and detection alone had lower error after external fine-tuning and external-only training. In the development cohort, detection was more accurate for many slides with high TPS, while fused predictions still tended to underestimate high TPS (Figure 4; Supplementary Figure S1). Disagreement among the branch and alternative fusion estimates can raise the flag for possible under-scoring at 50% when the final TPS remains below 50%. Macrophage staining can be confused with positive tumor cells. The framework excludes macrophage-rich patches and treats macrophages within retained tumor regions as non-tumor cells. The effect of macrophage burden on fused TPS error was not quantified across the cohort because region-level annotations were available for only the 30 WSIs used to develop tumor segmentation. 3.3 Selecting Cases for Pathologist Review Cutoff-specific flags relate review to the TPS categories used in practice. Near 1% and 50%, a small score difference can change the reported category. The model returns a continuous estimate. A pathologist reports a score near a cutoff as a judgment about which category the slide belongs to, so small differences on the continuous scale are not resolved in the reported score. Disagreement between the model and the pathologists near a cutoff therefore reflects both model error and the way scores are reported, and the two cannot be separated with reader scores alone. TPS-Guard flags these slides for the pathologist with the reason stated rather than deciding the category. The flag for possible over-scoring at 1% identifies a low-positive estimate that may fall below 1%, whereas the flag for possible under-scoring at 50% identifies an estimate below 50% with other model evidence suggesting a score at or above 50%. The flag for limited tumor-cell sampling draws attention to few scoring patches or low predicted tumor cell counts, particularly near a cutoff. These signals give the pathologist specific reasons to recheck the automated estimate alongside the slide. 24 Uncertainty ranking selects slides for review by identifying disagreement among the 15 models across the TPS range; the flags state the concern at a clinical cutoff, as the reason for referral or as an advisory. 3.4 Cross-Center Generalizability External TPS error was lower for SP263 than for E1L3N, a clone that was absent from development training. Clone, center, and imaging conditions varied together, so their individual contributions remain unresolved. After adaptation to ROI scale, TPS-Guard identified groups with higher rates of the specified scoring errors in the external cohort. The external rules retained the TPS windows and disagreement criteria while adapting count thresholds and patch conditions (Supplementary Section S2). Substantial continuous TPS error remained. 3.5 Limitations and Future Work The development study was retrospective and conducted at a single center. Reader comparisons had wide bootstrap confidence intervals, and the EBUS analysis involved a small subgroup. External validation comprised 98 ROIs from 21 patients and omitted tumor segmentation. Sex- stratified performance, reader confidence, and clinical outcomes after review were not assessed. Positive tumor cells made up 6% of the annotated nuclei, which left the training data imbalanced between PD-L1-positive and PD-L1-negative tumor cells, the two classes on which TPS depends. More annotated positive tumor cells will be needed to address this imbalance in the cell annotations; in the development cohort, retraining with the positive class reweighted did not improve agreement with the pathologists (Supplementary Section S15). Flag criteria were developed on the cohort used for evaluation, and external count quantiles were calculated from the external test cohort’s model outputs. Prospective studies should use fixed, prespecified rules on new whole slides and compare pathologist scoring with and without TPS- Guard cues, including accuracy and review time. 4. Conclusion In this retrospective study, model uncertainty identified slides with larger TPS errors and greater disagreement among pathologists. TPS-Guard added cutoff-specific flags that enriched defined 25 error events and recorded a reason for review. These findings support uncertainty-guided triage of automated TPS estimates. External ROI analyses supported this selection behavior after adaptation of count-dependent rules, despite substantial residual scoring error. The next step is prospective evaluation of how these cues affect pathologist scoring and review time. Acknowledgments The authors thank all contributors to data collection and annotation.
