3.2 Models The framework requires only a model that produces a per-unit count; any such model can be substituted. We consider two groups of models a practitioner might choose for a new task: detectors fine-tuned on the target data, and larger frozen models used off the shelf. Fine-tuned detectors We consider two Faster R-CNN detectors ( Ren et al. 2015 ) , one with a larger ResNet-50 ( He et al. 2016 ) backbone and one with a smaller MobileNetV3-Large ( Howard et al. 2019 ) backbone. Both are initialized from COCO-pretrained weights and adapted to a single foreground class by replacing the box-prediction head with a binary object-versus-background head. They are initially fine-tuned on the warmup set of w = 50 w=50 units, and continually retrained every 25 25 steps during the sequential labeling loop. Training details are provided in Appendix F . Frozen OVDs and MLLMs Given an input image and a target object name, OVDs return localized detections. We use the number of detections above a 0.5 confidence threshold as the per-unit count. We evaluate three OVDs: Florence-2 ( Xiao et al. 2024 ) , Grounding DINO ( Liu et al. 2024 ) , and OWLv2 ( Minderer, Gritsenko, and Houlsby 2023 ) . MLLMs take an image and a natural-language prompt and respond in free-form text. For all MLLMs, we use the prompt “How many { object_name } are in this image?” and extract the count using a constrained LLM-based answer extractor ( Lawrence et al. 2026 ) . Predictions without a clear numeric answer are assigned a count of zero. To compare locally runnable open-source models with proprietary models, we evaluate five open-source models (Qwen3-VL-8B ( Bai et al. 2025 ) , InternVL3.5-8B ( Wang et al. 2025 ) , Molmo-7B-D-0924 ( Deitke et al. 2025 ) , Molmo2-8B ( Clark et al. 2026 ) , Aya Vision-8B ( Dash et al. 2025 ) ) and three commercial models (GPT-5.2 ( OpenAI 2025 ) , Gemini 3.1 Pro ( Google DeepMind 2026 ) , Qwen3.7-Plus ( Qwen 2026 ) ). See Appendix B for more details. 4.1 Datasets and Task Construction We build the benchmark from four publicly available, exhaustively annotated detection datasets spanning distinct visual domains. Malaria Cell Images (BBBC041v1) ( Ljosa, Sokolnicki, and Carpenter 2012 ) contains microscopy images annotated with seven cell categories: six states of malaria infection and a difficult category for cells that cannot be confidently typed. Birds ( Hamilton et al. 2026b ) consists of image tiles from two high-resolution ground-level photographs of Tree Swallow flocks. Kenyan Wildlife Aerial Survey ( Eikelboom et al. 2019 ) comprises aerial survey photographs annotated for elephants, giraffes, and zebras. DOTA v1.5 ( Xia et al. 2018 ) contains aerial and satellite imagery with annotations for sixteen categories of urban objects, such as planes and storage tanks. Each dataset-class pair defines a counting task. For each task, we collect images containing the target object from the dataset and convert them into a set of estimation units. Fig. 1 shows examples of each task. To standardize apparent object scale, we estimate a task-specific unit size from 30 annotated objects sampled from roughly ten whole images, choosing the resolution so that the median object appears at about 80 pixels after resizing the tile to the Faster R-CNN’s standard resize target, 800 pixels. Images whose longer side is at most 1.5 × \times the tile size are kept whole to avoid thin edge tiles; larger images are split into non-overlapping square tiles, cropped at the right and bottom edges. These 30 samples are used only for tiling configuration and do not count toward the labeling budget or warmup. Objects are assigned to the tile containing the center of their bounding box, preventing double counting and ensuring that counts sum to F true F_{\text{true}} (details in Appendix D ). We characterize each task by its empty fraction , defined as the proportion of units containing no target objects, to study how object sparsity in the collection relates to different counting strategies. Across the four datasets, 27 classes are grouped into four occupancy tiers based on this fraction: full ( < 5 % <5\% ), partial ( 5 5 – 60 % 60\% ), sparse ( 60 60 – 90 % 90\% ), and rare ( ≥ 90 % \geq 90\% ). From each tier, we select the three classes with the largest F true F_{\text{true}} while preserving diversity in object type, yielding twelve tasks (Table 2 ). 4.2 Evaluation Protocol and Metrics We evaluate every task and counting strategy combination by the absolute fractional error AFE t = | F ^ 1 : t − F true | / F true \mathrm{AFE}_{t}\;=\;{\bigl|\hat{F}_{1:t}-F_{\text{true}}\bigr|}/{F_{\text{true}}} averaged over 1,000 1{,}000 trials and traced from t = 1 t=1 to t = N / 2 t=N/2 . Reporting the whole curve exposes how fast each strategy converges and where strategies cross over. Frozen-model trials are cheap: each unit’s prediction is computed once and never changes, so a single cached set of predictions g 0 g_{0} per model–task pair can be reused across all trials. Fine-tuned models are different, since the model is retrained as labels arrive and its predictions must be recomputed along every trajectory. Running 1,000 1{,}000 such trials for all fine-tuned configurations would have required roughly 82 GPU-years on our compute. We therefore run 5 genuine adaptive trajectories per task–strategy pair and cache the model’s predictions at each retraining step (every 25 steps), covering all units in Ω \Omega , not only those sampled. Each of the 5 cached sequences is then replayed under 200 200 different sampling seeds, holding the model’s adaptation path fixed while sampling randomness varies, yielding 1,000 1{,}000 trials in total. Hamilton et al. (2026b) used a similar approach with one genuine trajectory. Our results show that model-assisted estimation generally outperforms both model-only predictions and model-free Monte Carlo baselines, although the best strategy depends on the labeling budget and task structure. Importance sampling is a strong default when model predictions reliably rank high-count units. MLLMs offer an effective training-free starting point at low budgets, while fine-tuned detectors become increasingly competitive as more labels are acquired. Lightweight adaptation can further adapt models to deployment data without incurring the full cost of detector fine-tuning. Our findings suggest that models for collection-level counting should be selected not solely on the basis of raw counting accuracy, but also on how effectively their predictions support sampling and statistical estimation. Our pronghorn case study demonstrates that these gains translate to practice: AIS achieved a ± 5 % \pm 5\% confidence interval around the true total after reviewing only ∼ \sim 3.2% of the imagery. Our work also has several limitations. Although the benchmark spans diverse visual domains and occupancy tiers, it is limited to 12 single-class counting tasks. The findings may not fully generalize to multi-class estimation, alternative error metrics, noisy annotations, or settings in which target objects are difficult to verify consistently. Moreover, our comparisons do not capture all deployment costs, including image acquisition, model hosting, annotation interfaces, and the engineering required for repeated adaptation. Finally, our results reflect a snapshot of current frontier models and may need to be revisited as these models continue to improve. Proxy evaluation Table A5 evaluates each proxy with three metrics, all of which compare proxy values with the models’ realized errors. A model’s realized error is the lowest mean absolute fractional error it reaches at that budget in our benchmark runs, over the seven estimators available to a frozen model. The first metric, ρ \rho , measures how well a proxy orders all candidate models. It is the within-task Spearman rank correlation between the models’ proxy values, averaged over the 300 300 pilots, and their realized errors. Because averaging removes pilot noise, ρ \rho can be interpreted as an optimistic estimate of how well a single pilot orders the candidates. The other two metrics measure how good a single 50 50 -unit pilot is at selecting the best model. beats is the fraction of the other candidates whose error is higher than that of the pick (mean over pilots), and ratio is the pick’s error divided by that of the actual best model among candidates (median over pilots). For all three metrics, the ideal value is 1.0.
