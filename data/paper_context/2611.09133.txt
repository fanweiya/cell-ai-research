3 Method Given an RGB microscopy image 𝐗 ∈ ℝ 3 × H × W \mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times H\times W} , the objective is to predict an instance map 𝐌 ^ ∈ { 0 , 1 , … , N ^ } H × W \hat{\mathbf{M}}\in\{0,1,\ldots,\hat{N}\}^{H\times W} , where zero denotes background and each positive label identifies one nucleus. The final nucleus count N ^ \hat{N} is obtained from the number of unique non-background labels in 𝐌 ^ \hat{\mathbf{M}} . As shown in Fig. 1 , DINO-Nucleus does not predict the instance map directly. Instead, the network produces a foreground probability map 𝐘 ^ fg ∈ [ 0 , 1 ] H × W \hat{\mathbf{Y}}_{\mathrm{fg}}\in[0,1]^{H\times W} , a center heatmap 𝐘 ^ c ∈ [ 0 , 1 ] H × W \hat{\mathbf{Y}}_{\mathrm{c}}\in[0,1]^{H\times W} , and a two-channel center-directed flow field 𝐘 ^ f ∈ ℝ 2 × H × W \hat{\mathbf{Y}}_{\mathrm{f}}\in\mathbb{R}^{2\times H\times W} . The foreground map identifies nucleus pixels, the center heatmap provides explicit instance locations, and the flow field directs each foreground pixel toward the center of its corresponding nucleus. The input image is encoded by a pretrained DINOv2 backbone adapted with LoRA, while features from multiple transformer depths are retained. A lightweight CNN branch also extracts higher-resolution local information from the input. The projected transformer and CNN features are fused through a shared decoder and passed to separate foreground, center, and flow heads. Training combines foreground segmentation, center localization, and flow regression. During inference, the three outputs are jointly used to integrate pixel trajectories, detect nucleus centers, assign foreground pixels to instances, and obtain the final instance map and count. 3.1 Encoding and Feature Fusion Given an input image 𝐗 \mathbf{X} , we use a pretrained DINOv2 ViT-S/14 encoder and retain features from different stages of the transformer to capture information from its early, intermediate, and final layers. Among the layer combinations considered during model development, layers 2 2 , 5 5 , 8 8 , and 11 11 provided the best validation performance and were therefore selected. For each layer l l , the feature map is written as 𝐅 l ∈ ℝ C × H p × W p \mathbf{F}_{l}\in\mathbb{R}^{C\times H_{p}\times W_{p}} , where H p H_{p} and W p W_{p} denote the patch-grid dimensions. Although these features share the same spatial resolution, they represent different levels of abstraction: earlier layers retain more local structure, while deeper layers provide stronger semantic context. Each feature is projected into a common channel space using a layer-specific 1 × 1 1\times 1 convolution, 𝐏 l = ϕ l ​ ( 𝐅 l ) \mathbf{P}_{l}=\phi_{l}(\mathbf{F}_{l}) , before the projected features are combined into a multi-level representation. To adapt the pretrained encoder efficiently, all early transformer blocks remain frozen, while the final four blocks are adapted using LoRA modules. The original weights of these blocks remain fixed, and only the low-rank updates are optimized. The final normalization layer is fully trainable to further align the adapted representations with the microscopy task. Since the DINOv2 features remain on the patch grid, a lightweight CNN branch also extracts higher-resolution local features 𝐋 1 / 4 \mathbf{L}_{1/4} and 𝐋 1 / 2 \mathbf{L}_{1/2} from the input image. The fused transformer representation is then progressively upsampled and combined with these local features through the decoder, producing a shared full-resolution representation 𝐙 \mathbf{Z} for the foreground, center, and flow prediction heads. 3.2 Center-Flow Learning and Instance Reconstruction A foreground map identifies nucleus regions but cannot reliably separate touching nuclei once they form a connected component. We therefore complement it with an explicit center heatmap and a pixel-wise center-directed flow field. Together, 𝐘 ^ fg \hat{\mathbf{Y}}_{\mathrm{fg}} , 𝐘 ^ c \hat{\mathbf{Y}}_{\mathrm{c}} , and 𝐘 ^ f \hat{\mathbf{Y}}_{\mathrm{f}} describe nucleus extent, instance locations, and pixel-to-instance associations. The supervision targets are generated from the ground-truth instance masks. The foreground target 𝐘 fg \mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{fg}} is obtained by merging all nucleus instances into a binary mask. For each nucleus, its center c k c_{k} is selected as the point with the largest Euclidean distance from the boundary, ensuring that it lies inside the instance even for irregular shapes. Training begins with a decoder-only warm-up, after which LoRA parameters and final normalization layer are optimized. During inference, the foreground map is thresholded to identify candidate nucleus pixels, whose trajectories are then integrated through the predicted flow field. Their endpoints are accumulated into an endpoint-density map, where high-density regions indicate likely nucleus centers. This density is combined with the predicted center heatmap, and local peaks in the fused response are selected as instance centers. Each foreground pixel is assigned to a detected center according to its trajectory endpoint. Disconnected regions sharing the same provisional label are separated, and small isolated fragments are removed. The remaining connected regions form the final instance map 𝐌 ^ \hat{\mathbf{M}} , from which nucleus count N ^ \hat{N} is obtained. 4 Experiments We describe the dataset, evaluation protocol, metrics, and implementation settings, followed by qualitative analysis of the learned representations, center heatmaps, and flow fields. We then compare DINO-Nucleus with TC-UNet++, Cellpose3, StarDist, and CellViT [ 20 , 18 , 16 , 5 ] in terms of segmentation, counting, and parameter efficiency. Finally, we compare it with a basic DINOv2 watershed baseline [ 13 ] to assess the contributions of center-flow consistency. Dataset and preprocessing. We use BBBC038, containing 670 microscopy images with instance-level nucleus annotations [ 2 ] . Using a fixed seed, images are stratified by nucleus count and mean intensity into 468 training, 67 validation, and 135 test samples. The validation set is used for model selection and post-processing calibration, while the test set is reserved for final evaluation. Training images are aspect-ratio resized and zero-padded to 448 × 448 448\times 448 , with random geometric and photometric augmentation. Test images are processed using 448 × 448 448\times 448 sliding windows with 112-pixel overlap. Evaluation metrics. We evaluate foreground segmentation using Dice and IoU, and instance segmentation using object-level F1, panoptic quality (PQ), and aggregated Jaccard index (AJI) [ 9 , 10 ] . Instances are matched at IoU > 0.45 \mathrm{IoU}>0.45 . Counting is evaluated using mean absolute error (MAE) and Pearson correlation. Implementation details. DINO-Nuclei uses a pretrained DINOv2 ViT-S/14 encoder [ 13 ] and fuses features from blocks 2 2 , 5 5 , 8 8 , and 11 11 through a 128-channel decoder. Rank-8 LoRA adapters with scaling 16 and dropout 0.05 are applied to the final four blocks [ 8 ] . Foreground, center, flow, and endpoint-consistency losses are weighted by 1.0 1.0 , 0.5 0.5 , 0.5 0.5 , and 0.05 0.05 , respectively, with consistency enabled after five warm-up epochs. During inference, foreground pixels follow the predicted flow and are grouped by their converged endpoints [ 19 ] . Reconstruction and post-processing parameters are calibrated on the validation set and fixed for testing. Flow reconstruction uses 48 integration steps with unit step size; the remaining reconstruction thresholds are selected on validation set and fixed for testing. All baselines use the same splits, augmentations, epoch budget, and evaluation protocol while retaining architecture-specific training settings. Experiments run on an NVIDIA A100 GPU. The PCA maps show that nucleus regions form clear patterns that differ from the surrounding background. This is also visible in the prototype-score maps, where nucleus locations appear nucleus-like across all four layers. The t-SNE plots provide another view of the feature space: background patches often form separate groups, while nucleus and boundary patches remain more closely related. This is expected as boundary patches contain both nucleus and background pixels. Taken together, PCA maps show that DINOv2 preserves the spatial structure of nucleus regions, while the t-SNE plots show that nucleus-dominant patches occupy feature-space regions that are largely separate from background patches, with boundary patches naturally appearing between them. Comparison with existing methods. Table 1 compares our method with existing nucleus segmentation approaches and the DINO baseline on BBBC038. Our method achieves competitive foreground segmentation, despite adapting only a small part of the pretrained encoder through LoRA. Together with the feature analysis in Fig. 2 , this indicates that pretrained DINOv2 features already provide strong information for locating nucleus regions. The main advantage of our method appears in instance segmentation and counting, where it consistently outperforms all competing methods. Compared with the DINOv2 baseline using watershed reconstruction, the foreground results remain similar, as the center-flow formulation is not mainly designed to improve foreground detection. In contrast, the large improvements in instance segmentation and counting show that the predicted centers, center-directed flows, and their consistency are effective for separating neighboring nuclei and reconstructing individual instances. A limitation of the current formulation is its reliance on a single convergence center per nucleus, which may be less suitable for highly elongated or irregular shapes. In addition, evaluation is limited to BBBC038, and the validation-calibrated reconstruction parameters may require adjustment for datasets with substantially different imaging characteristics. 5 Conclusion In this paper, we introduced DINO-Nucleus, a parameter-efficient framework for nucleus instance segmentation and counting. By leveraging multi-level DINOv2 features adapted via lightweight LoRA modules, the model effectively combines fine spatial details with robust semantic context. Our approach jointly predicts foreground regions, explicit nucleus centers, and pixel-wise flows, utilizing a consistency objective that directs pixel trajectories toward valid centers. This center-guided formulation accurately separates touching nuclei without relying on sensitive watershed post-processing. Experiments on the BBBC038 dataset demonstrate that DINO-Nucleus significantly outperforms existing methods in both instance segmentation and counting accuracy. Ultimately, this highlights the strong potential of combining efficiently adapted self-supervised representations with geometric learning for reliable quantitative microscopy.
