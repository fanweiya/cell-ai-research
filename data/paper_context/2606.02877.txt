2.1 Histology-only WSI modelling Weakly supervised whole-slide image (WSI) modelling is commonly formulated as a multiple-instance learning problem, in which a slide is represented as a bag of patch-level features. Attention-based MIL [ Ilse et al., 2018 ] and transformer-style MIL [ Shao et al., 2021a ] models have enabled slide-level prediction by learning to aggregate heterogeneous patch evidence into a patient-level representation. More recent WSI models further improve aggregation through prototype learning [ Guo et al., 2025 , Koyun et al., 2025 ] , modern mamba architecture [ Cheng et al., 2026 ] , or mixture-of-experts transformations [ Shao et al., 2026 ] . These models satisfy the requirement for H&E-only deployment. However, training from histology alone may leave valuable transcriptomic information unused. MoPE retains the deployment advantage of WSI-only inference while using paired RNA profiles during training to provide pathway-structured molecular supervision for the histology branch. 3 Method 3.1 Problem setting and overview This study addresses a privileged-information setting in which paired whole-slide image (WSI) and bulk RNA sequence data are available during training, whereas only WSIs are available during deployment. The RNA branch is therefore used to provide pathway-structured supervision during model development and is removed at inference. The model performs all downstream predictions from H&E WSI features alone. The overall framework is shown in Fig. 1 . Figure 1: Overview of MoPE for pathway-structured privileged distillation. a , Clinical motivation. RNA expression, protein abundance and tissue phenotype are connected through a complex and non-linear biological cascade, producing a many-to-many relation between molecular measurements and H&E morphology. This presents the partial observability: H&E morphology captures only the tissue-level consequences of some RNA-associated programmes and cannot be assumed to recover the full molecular state of a tumour. RNA profiles can refine cancer phenotyping and risk assessment, but molecular measurements are not consistently available in routine care. b , Model training workflow. H&E patches are processed by pathway-indexed experts, whereas RNA expression profiles are organized into Hallmark pathway tokens that provide privileged supervision during training. c , Knowledge-distillation branch. Matched WSI expert tokens and RNA pathway tokens are encouraged to use a shared memory basis in similar ways, providing a soft distillation objective rather than direct feature imitation. d , Model deployment workflow. After training, the RNA branch is removed and the pathway-distilled histology branch performs downstream prediction from WSIs alone. 3.2 WSI branch: pathway-indexed morphology tokens Let 𝐗 ∈ ℝ B × N × D w ​ s ​ i \mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{B\times N\times D_{wsi}} denote WSI patch features extracted by a frozen pathology foundation model [ Chen et al., 2024 ] , where B B is the batch size, N N is the number of patches, and D w ​ s ​ i D_{wsi} is the patch feature dimension. We used the Hallmark 50 pathways as the RNA-branch vocabulary, so the WSI branch contains K = 50 K=50 pathway-indexed experts, denoted by E 1 , … , E K E_{1},\ldots,E_{K} . Each expert corresponds to one Hallmark pathway and contains L L learnable morphology slots. The slots allow one pathway-indexed expert to collect multiple candidate visual patterns before producing a pathway-level WSI token. For pathway expert k k , we define a set of learnable slots 𝐒 k = [ 𝐬 k , 1 , … , 𝐬 k , L ] ⊤ ∈ ℝ L × D wsi \mathbf{S}_{k}=[\mathbf{s}_{k,1},\ldots,\mathbf{s}_{k,L}]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{L\times D_{\mathrm{wsi}}} . (1) where 𝐖 key ∈ ℝ D wsi × D wsi \mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{key}}\in\mathbb{R}^{D_{\mathrm{wsi}}\times D_{\mathrm{wsi}}} is a learnable key projection and the softmax is applied over the patch dimension. Each row of 𝐀 k \mathbf{A}_{k} is the attention distribution of one morphology slot over all patches. The corresponding slot embeddings are 𝐇 k = 𝐀 k ​ 𝐗 , 𝐇 k = [ 𝐡 k , 1 , … , 𝐡 k , L ] ⊤ ∈ ℝ L × D wsi . \mathbf{H}_{k}=\mathbf{A}_{k}\mathbf{X},\qquad\mathbf{H}_{k}=[\mathbf{h}_{k,1},\ldots,\mathbf{h}_{k,L}]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{L\times D_{\mathrm{wsi}}}. (2) Thus, 𝐡 k , ℓ \mathbf{h}_{k,\ell} summarizes the WSI evidence selected by slot ℓ \ell in pathway expert k k . 3.3 Low-rank expert transformation and slide aggregation After slot-level morphology extraction, each pathway-indexed expert contains L L slot embeddings, where each slot summarizes one candidate morphology pattern from the WSI. Assigning an independent full-rank projection to every expert would overfit limited paired WSI-RNA cohorts. Inspired by low-rank adaptation [ Hu et al., 2022 ] , we use a shared-to-specialized low-rank expert transform. For slot ℓ \ell in expert k k , we compute 𝐳 k , ℓ = ρ ⁡ ( 𝐡 k , ℓ ​ 𝐖 D ​ 𝐔 k ) \mathbf{z}_{k,\ell}=\rho\left(\mathbf{h}_{k,\ell}\mathbf{W}_{D}\mathbf{U}_{k}\right) , where 𝐡 k , ℓ \mathbf{h}_{k,\ell} is the slot embedding, 𝐖 D ∈ ℝ D w ​ s ​ i × r \mathbf{W}_{D}\in\mathbb{R}^{D_{wsi}\times r} is a shared down-projection, 𝐔 k ∈ ℝ r × D s ​ h ​ a ​ r ​ e ​ d \mathbf{U}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times D_{shared}} is the expert-specific projection, r ≪ D w ​ s ​ i r\ll D_{wsi} , and ρ ⁡ ( ⋅ ) \rho(\cdot) denotes layer normalization. This parameterization is intended to encourage reuse of low-rank morphology features across pathways, such as tumour cellularity, stromal organization, necrosis, immune infiltration, or glandular architecture. The expert-specific matrix 𝐔 k \mathbf{U}_{k} then adapts the shared projection to the semantic context of pathway k k . The transformed slots are then combined within each expert by a learnable slot gate. For each transformed slot 𝐳 k , ℓ \mathbf{z}_{k,\ell} , we compute a scalar gate logit using a two-layer MLP: u k , ℓ = 𝐰 s ⊤ ​ ( ReLU ⁡ ( 𝐖 s ​ 𝐳 k , ℓ ) ) u_{k,\ell}=\mathbf{w}_{s}^{\top}\left(\operatorname{ReLU}\left(\mathbf{W}_{s}\mathbf{z}_{k,\ell}\right)\right) , where 𝐖 s ∈ ℝ D s ​ h ​ a ​ r ​ e ​ d × D s ​ h ​ a ​ r ​ e ​ d \mathbf{W}_{s}\in\mathbb{R}^{D_{shared}\times D_{shared}} and 𝐰 s ∈ ℝ D s ​ h ​ a ​ r ​ e ​ d \mathbf{w}_{s}\in\mathbb{R}^{D_{shared}} are shared across experts and slots. The slot weights are normalized within each pathway expert: β k , ℓ = exp ⁡ ( u k , ℓ ) ∑ ℓ ′ = 1 L exp ⁡ ( u k , ℓ ′ ) , ∑ ℓ = 1 L β k , ℓ = 1 . \beta_{k,\ell}=\frac{\exp(u_{k,\ell})}{\sum_{\ell^{\prime}=1}^{L}\exp(u_{k,\ell^{\prime}})},\qquad\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\beta_{k,\ell}=1. (3) The pathway-level WSI token is then obtained as 𝐞 k w ​ s ​ i = ∑ ℓ = 1 L β k , ℓ ​ 𝐳 k , ℓ \mathbf{e}_{k}^{wsi}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\beta_{k,\ell}\mathbf{z}_{k,\ell} . The resulting token 𝐞 k w ​ s ​ i ∈ ℝ D s ​ h ​ a ​ r ​ e ​ d \mathbf{e}_{k}^{wsi}\in\mathbb{R}^{D_{shared}} summarizes WSI morphology evidence for pathway k k . Here, β k , ℓ \beta_{k,\ell} measures how much the ℓ \ell -th morphology slot contributes to pathway expert k k for the current patient. After obtaining the expert token for each pathway, we aggregate these pathway-level tokens into a slide representation: 𝐳 s ​ l ​ i ​ d ​ e = ∑ k = 1 K α k ​ 𝐞 k w ​ s ​ i , ∑ k = 1 K α k = 1 . \mathbf{z}_{slide}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\alpha_{k}\mathbf{e}_{k}^{wsi},\qquad\sum_{k=1}^{K}\alpha_{k}=1. (4) The expert weights α k \alpha_{k} are learned from a two-layer MLP and reflect the relative contribution of different pathway-aligned morphology summaries to the final prediction. The slide representation is then passed to a linear prediction head for the downstream task. Overall, this module performs a two-stage compression of WSI morphology. The slot gate first selects relevant morphology patterns within each pathway expert, and the expert gate then selects relevant pathway-level summaries at the slide level. This delayed aggregation avoids prematurely collapsing heterogeneous patch evidence and gives the model an inspectable intermediate representation at the pathway level. 3.4 RNA branch: encoding molecular information The RNA branch is used only during training to provide pathway-level privileged targets. We first organize each RNA profile into Hallmark pathway groups, 𝐑 ∈ ℝ B × K × D r ​ n ​ a \mathbf{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{B\times K\times D_{rna}} , where 𝐑 b , k \mathbf{R}_{b,k} denotes the expression vector associated with pathway k k for patient b b . Each pathway vector is projected into the shared latent space: 𝐞 b , k r ​ n ​ a = f r ​ n ​ a ​ ( 𝐑 b , k ) , 𝐞 b , k r ​ n ​ a ∈ ℝ D s ​ h ​ a ​ r ​ e ​ d . \mathbf{e}^{rna}_{b,k}=f_{rna}(\mathbf{R}_{b,k}),\qquad\mathbf{e}^{rna}_{b,k}\in\mathbb{R}^{D_{shared}}. (5) In our implementation, f r ​ n ​ a f_{rna} is a linear projection followed by layer normalization. These unmasked RNA pathway tokens are used as the teacher targets for memory-usage distillation. We also include a lightweight masked reconstruction auxiliary loss on the RNA branch. During training, a subset of pathway vectors is replaced by zeros before projection: 𝐑 ~ b , k = ( 1 − Ω b , k ) ​ 𝐑 b , k \tilde{\mathbf{R}}_{b,k}=(1-\Omega_{b,k})\mathbf{R}_{b,k} , where Ω b , k = 1 \Omega_{b,k}=1 indicates a masked pathway. The masked RNA tokens are decoded back to the pathway-gene space, 𝐑 ^ b , k = g d ​ e ​ c ​ ( f r ​ n ​ a ​ ( 𝐑 ~ b , k ) ) \hat{\mathbf{R}}_{b,k}=g_{dec}(f_{rna}(\tilde{\mathbf{R}}_{b,k})) . The reconstruction loss is computed only on masked pathways: ℒ r ​ e ​ c = 1 | Ω | ​ D r ​ n ​ a ​ ∑ b = 1 B ∑ k = 1 K Ω b , k ​ ‖ 𝐑 ^ b , k − 𝐑 b , k ‖ 2 2 . \mathcal{L}_{rec}=\frac{1}{|\Omega|D_{rna}}\sum_{b=1}^{B}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\Omega_{b,k}\left\|\hat{\mathbf{R}}_{b,k}-\mathbf{R}_{b,k}\right\|_{2}^{2}. (6) This auxiliary term regularizes the RNA projection and decoder, whereas the distillation targets are computed from the original unmasked RNA pathway vectors. 3.5 Distillation in the latent space Directly matching WSI and RNA representations imposes a strong assumption and it may hinder model training because the student is penalized for failing to recover modality-private RNA signals. We therefore use a memory bank as a shared latent reference system for a soft distillation: 𝐂 ∈ ℝ M × D s ​ h ​ a ​ r ​ e ​ d \mathbf{C}\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times D_{shared}} , where each row 𝐜 m \mathbf{c}_{m} is a latent basis vector. For each matched pair of WSI expert token 𝐞 k w ​ s ​ i \mathbf{e}_{k}^{wsi} and RNA pathway token 𝐞 k r ​ n ​ a \mathbf{e}_{k}^{rna} , we compute their usage/similarity distributions over the shared memory bases: 𝐩𝐫𝐨𝐛 k w ​ s ​ i = softmax ⁡ ( 𝐞 k w ​ s ​ i ​ 𝐂 ⊤ ) \mathbf{prob}_{k}^{wsi}=\operatorname{softmax}\left(\mathbf{e}_{k}^{wsi}\mathbf{C}^{\top}\right) , 𝐩𝐫𝐨𝐛 k r ​ n ​ a = softmax ⁡ ( sg ⁡ ( 𝐞 k r ​ n ​ a ) ​ 𝐂 ⊤ ) \mathbf{prob}_{k}^{rna}=\operatorname{softmax}\left(\mathrm{sg}(\mathbf{e}_{k}^{rna})\mathbf{C}^{\top}\right) , where sg ⁡ ( ⋅ ) \mathrm{sg}(\cdot) denotes the stop-gradient operation. The stop-gradient prevents the RNA teacher target from being updated by the student-side task loss or by the distillation objective. As a result, the RNA branch provides a stable pathway-level target. The distillation loss is defined as ℒ K ​ D = 1 K ∑ k = 1 K D K ​ L ( 𝐩𝐫𝐨𝐛 k r ​ n ​ a ∥ 𝐩𝐫𝐨𝐛 k w ​ s ​ i ) . \mathcal{L}_{KD}=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}D_{KL}\left(\mathbf{prob}_{k}^{rna}\;\|\;\mathbf{prob}_{k}^{wsi}\right). This objective asks the WSI expert token and the RNA pathway token to use the shared latent bases similarly, rather than requiring their embeddings to coincide. This memory-usage distillation is intended to transfer RNA-side structure through shared latent-basis usage while allowing each modality to retain private information. To stabilize the shared memory basis, we use a vector-quantization style memory regularization [ Gray, 1984 ] . For each WSI expert token, we assign it to the nearest memory basis. Specifically, after flattening all WSI expert tokens in a mini-batch into { 𝐞 i w ​ s ​ i } i = 1 I \{\mathbf{e}_{i}^{wsi}\}_{i=1}^{I} , where I = B ​ K I=BK , the nearest memory index is q i = arg ⁡ min m ∈ { 1 , … , M } ⁡ ‖ 𝐞 i w ​ s ​ i − 𝐜 m ‖ 2 2 q_{i}=\arg\min_{m\in\{1,\ldots,M\}}\left\|\mathbf{e}_{i}^{wsi}-\mathbf{c}_{m}\right\|_{2}^{2} . The memory regularization contains a commitment term and an orthogonality term: ℒ mem = 1 I ​ ∑ i = 1 I ‖ 𝐞 i w ​ s ​ i − 𝐜 q i ‖ 2 2 + ∑ m = 1 M ∑ m ′ ≠ m M ( 𝐜 m ⊤ ​ 𝐜 m ′ ‖ 𝐜 m ‖ 2 ​ ‖ 𝐜 m ′ ‖ 2 ) 2 . \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{mem}}=\frac{1}{I}\sum_{i=1}^{I}\left\|\mathbf{e}_{i}^{wsi}-\mathbf{c}_{q_{i}}\right\|_{2}^{2}+\sum_{m=1}^{M}\sum_{m^{\prime}\neq m}^{M}\left(\frac{\mathbf{c}_{m}^{\top}\mathbf{c}_{m^{\prime}}}{\|\mathbf{c}_{m}\|_{2}\|\mathbf{c}_{m^{\prime}}\|_{2}}\right)^{2}. (7) The first term encourages WSI expert tokens to remain close to their assigned memory bases. The second term discourages different memory bases from becoming redundant by penalizing pairwise cosine similarity among codebook entries. Before optimization, we initialize the memory by applying k-means on pooled WSI embeddings from the training set, and use the resulting M M cluster centers to initialize the memory base. 3.6 Training objective and inference The final objective combines task supervision, memory-usage distillation, masked pathway reconstruction, memory regularization, and slot diversity: ℒ = λ t ​ a ​ s ​ k ​ ℒ t ​ a ​ s ​ k + λ K ​ D ​ ℒ K ​ D + λ r ​ e ​ c ​ ℒ r ​ e ​ c + λ m ​ e ​ m ​ ℒ m ​ e ​ m + λ d ​ i ​ v ​ ℒ d ​ i ​ v . \mathcal{L}=\lambda_{task}\mathcal{L}_{task}+\lambda_{KD}\mathcal{L}_{KD}+\lambda_{rec}\mathcal{L}_{rec}+\lambda_{mem}\mathcal{L}_{mem}+\lambda_{div}\mathcal{L}_{div}. (8) The task loss ℒ t ​ a ​ s ​ k \mathcal{L}_{task} is cross-entropy for classification. For survival prediction, we used a negative log-likelihood (NLL) loss. Further, we regularize the slot attention: ℒ d ​ i ​ v = 1 K ​ ∑ k = 1 K 1 L ⁡ ( L − 1 ) ​ ∑ ℓ ≠ ℓ ′ cos ⁡ ( 𝐀 k , ℓ , 𝐀 k , ℓ ′ ) \mathcal{L}_{div}=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{1}{L(L-1)}\sum_{\ell\neq\ell^{\prime}}\operatorname{cos}\left(\mathbf{A}_{k,\ell},\mathbf{A}_{k,\ell^{\prime}}\right) , where 𝐀 k , ℓ \mathbf{A}_{k,\ell} is the slot-to-patch attention vector of slot ℓ \ell in expert k k . In our default setting, the loss weights are set as: λ t ​ a ​ s ​ k = 1.0 \lambda_{task}=1.0 , λ m ​ e ​ m = 0.05 \lambda_{mem}=0.05 , λ r ​ e ​ c = 0.01 \lambda_{rec}=0.01 , and λ d ​ i ​ v = 0.1 \lambda_{div}=0.1 . Specifically, for classification tasks, λ K ​ D \lambda_{KD} is 1.0 1.0 , while for survival prediction, λ K ​ D \lambda_{KD} is 0.5 0.5 , with all other parameters remaining consistent. 4 Experiments 4.1 Datasets We evaluated MoPE using The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov , encompassing breast (TCGA-BRCA, n = 1,023 n=1{,}023 ), lung (TCGA-LUAD, n = 510 n=510 ), glioblastoma/lower-grade glioma (TCGA-GBMLGG, n = 878 n=878 ), stomach (TCGA-STAD, n = 363 n=363 ), and kidney (TCGA-KIRC, n = 498 n=498 ) cancers. For clinical classification, the endpoint including Oncotype DX (ODX) risk and Progesterone Receptor (PR) status in breast cancer, Hypoxia-Inducible Factor and Epidermal Growth Factor Receptor (EGFR) status in lung cancer, and mesenchymal state in glioma. Survival prediction was evaluated across five cancer types to test cross-tissue performance. Matched RNA profiles were retrieved from the UCSC Xena platform [ Goldman et al., 2020 ] , and the 50 Hallmark gene sets obtained from the Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB) [ Subramanian et al., 2005 ] . Detailed statistics for all TCGA tasks are summarized in Supplementary Table S1, with additional clinical context provided in Supplementary Note 1. To test the generalizability of the model, we used two independent external cohorts for ODX risk prediction ( n = 1,645 n=1{,}645 ). These comprised 1,123 whole-slide images (WSIs) of breast cancer from The Ohio State University Wexner Medical Center, and 522 WSIs from the Dartmouth Breast Cancer Recurrence Risk Dataset [ Goyal et al., 2024 ] . These geographically and institutionally distinct cohorts provide comprehensive evaluation of the proposed method under the real clinical scenario. 4.2 Comparison methods We compared our method with four groups of baselines. First, for the omics-only setting, we used the Self-Normalizing Network (SNN) [ Klambauer et al., 2017 ] and GPNet [ Lu et al., 2025 ] as the RNA-based methods. These models use omics features only and do not use WSI information. Second, for the histopathology-only setting, we included representative multiple instance learning methods, including attention based MIL (AttMIL) [ Ilse et al., 2018 ] , DSMIL [ Li et al., 2021 ] , DTFD-MIL [ Zhang et al., 2022 ] , TransMIL [ Shao et al., 2021b ] , and WIKG [ Li et al., 2024 ] . Further, we considered MAMMOTH [ Shao et al., 2026 ] , a mixture-of-experts based method in computational pathology. Following the official implementation, we integrated MAMMOTH with AttMIL and TransMIL, denoted as AttMIL-MoE and TransMIL-MoE in the following sections. Third, for knowledge distillation methods, we compared TDC [ Xing et al., 2024 ] , G-HANet [ Wang et al., 2025 ] , MKD [ Zhang et al., 2025c ] , and DMML [ Zhang et al., 2026 ] . These methods share the same scope as our work: both WSI and RNA are available during training, while remaining RNA-free inference. Finally, we included multimodal methods that use paired WSI and omics data during both training and inference as a performance upper bound, including DMML t [ Zhang et al., 2026 ] , MMP [ Song et al., 2024 ] , LD-CVAE [ Zhou et al., 2025 ] and PAMT [ Yan et al., 2026 ] . Here, DMML t denotes the multimodal teacher model from the DMML paper. 4.3 Implementation details Following established protocols [ Zhang et al., 2025a ] , we segmented the tissue foreground of each WSI and extracted 896 × 896 896\times 896 pixel patches from tissue foreground regions. For feature extraction, we used the frozen foundation model UNI v2 [ Chen et al., 2024 ] , yielding patch-level representations of dimension D w ​ s ​ i = 1536 D_{wsi}=1536 . For the pathway-level RNA representation, we used the 50 Hallmark gene sets and retained genes that overlapped with the original RNA expression profile ( D = 20,531 D=20{,}531 ), resulting in 4,188 unique Hallmark-associated genes. For each patient, raw expression values were first transformed by log 2 ⁡ ( x + 1 ) \log_{2}(x+1) and then standardized gene-wise across primary tumor samples to obtain z-scores. We then constructed a 50 × 4188 50\times 4188 pathway-gene matrix. Entries for genes belonging to the corresponding pathway were filled with their expression values, whereas non-member genes were zero-masked. All models were trained with AdamW (learning rate 2 × 10 − 4 2\times 10^{-4} , weight decay 1 × 10 − 4 1\times 10^{-4} , batch size 1). For classification, we used patient-level stratified 5-fold cross-validation with a 6 : 2 : 2 6{:}2{:}2 train/validation/test split and trained for 30 epochs. For external evaluation, TCGA-BRCA was split 9 : 1 9{:}1 into training and validation sets in each fold, and the trained weight was directly evaluated on two independent cohorts without fine-tuning. Survival models followed the same 5-fold protocol and were trained for 20 epochs. Results are reported as mean ± \mathbin{\text{\textpm}} standard deviation across five folds. All experiments used a single NVIDIA H100 GPU. 4.4 Human-audited visual inspection For the visual inspection analysis, we selected representative correct and failed BRCA-ODX predictions and traced them through expert-level attention maps, slot-level attention maps and high-attention H&E patch tiles. A large language model ( ChatGPT 5.5, utilized in May 2026 ) was used only to convert these model-derived visual materials into a structured morphology summary using the prompt template in Supplementary Note 3. A clinical expert then reviewed both the summary and the underlying image evidence using predefined criteria for morphological accuracy, image support, pathway compatibility and ambiguity. Across-task analysis showed mean AUC gains of 2.0 2.0 percentage points over AttMIL-MoE (95% CI, 0.4 0.4 – 3.6 3.6 ; P = 0.009 P=0.009 ) and 2.7 2.7 points over G-HANet (95% CI, 1.2 1.2 – 4.2 4.2 ; P = 0.0002 P=0.0002 ) (Fig. 2 b(ii)). The results are consistent with three observations. First, molecularly supervised histology prediction can be framed as a partial-observability problem rather than only as a conventional missing-modality problem. Second, pathway-structured privileged distillation can improve WSI-only prediction without requiring RNA at inference. Third, pathway-indexed readouts can make model behaviour inspectable, provided they are interpreted as predictive evidence rather than mechanistic proof. MoPE is motivated by the partial correspondence between transcriptomic activity and tissue morphology: some molecular programs have visible histological correlates, whereas others are only weakly reflected in H&E. We therefore use pathway structure as biologically organized supervision without assuming that histology can recover the full transcriptomic state. Accordingly, MoPE distills cross-modal information through memory usage rather than direct feature alignment, allowing RNA tokens and pathway-indexed experts to share structured supervision under incomplete cross-modal observability. Under this formulation, pathway experts can be interpreted as biological-indexed representations of morphology. Consistent with this interpretation, transcriptomic pathway relevance and WSI-side gate separation showed related but non-identical patterns (Fig. 4 b,c). Such partial correspondence is biologically plausible because molecular risk programs can manifest through histopathological features such as mitotic activity, nuclear grade, and tubule formation [ Klein et al., 2013 , Geradts et al., 2010 ] . Thus, pathway expert usage reflects morphological phenotypes associated with molecular programs, rather than direct measurement of their activity. These pathway readouts provide an auditable intermediate layer between slide-level prediction and local image evidence. Expert usage, slot-level attention and high-attention patches allow predictions to be traced through pathway-indexed representations to specific tissue regions (Fig. 5 ). However, attention and saliency maps can appear visually plausible without faithfully identifying causal features [ Adebayo et al., 2018 , Jain and Wallace, 2019 ] . Human-audited inspection therefore has a bounded role: it can assess whether highlighted regions support a morphology-linked hypothesis and expose failure modes, but it does not establish molecular activity. Direct validation of the corresponding molecular state would require orthogonal spatial measurements, such as spatial transcriptomics, immunohistochemistry [ Zhao et al., 2026 , Andani et al., 2025 ] , or multiplex protein imaging [ Han et al., 2026 ] . The external evaluation gives this design a more realistic retrospective test. Models trained and selected using public benchmark data were evaluated on two independent institutional cohorts without external RNA input or site-specific refitting for the Oncotype DX test prediction. This setting reflects a common practical constraint: molecular assays may inform model development, whereas image-only inference is often the scalable interface. The external results support further study of pathway-structured privileged distillation for endpoint-specific WSI-only biomarker prediction, particularly for settings in which molecular testing is unavailable. Several limitations remain. First, MoPE requires paired WSI–RNA data during training, limiting its development to cohorts with sufficient multimodal annotation. Second, external validation was retrospective and focused primarily on a molecularly informed breast cancer endpoint; broader prospective and multi-institutional evaluation is therefore needed to establish generalizability. Third, model reliability remains sensitive to tissue quality and preprocessing, as illustrated by failure cases involving weak or background-rich regions. Future work should extend validation across institutions and acquisition conditions, incorporate uncertainty-aware quality control, and evaluate pathway-indexed readouts against orthogonal spatial molecular measurements. 7 Conclusion In this work, we presented MoPE, a pathway-structured privileged-distillation framework that formulates molecularly supervised computational pathology under partial cross-modal observability. By organizing RNA supervision through Hallmark pathways and transferring it to pathway-indexed histology experts via memory-usage distillation, MoPE improved WSI-only biomarker classification and survival prediction across multiple public cohorts and maintained strong performance in two independent external breast cancer cohorts, while requiring only histology at inference. More broadly, MoPE provides a structured way to exploit molecular information as privileged supervision without assuming full transcriptomic recoverability from morphology, offering a practical framework for deployable and biologically organized computational pathology.
