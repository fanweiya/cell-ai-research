2.3 Medical Foundation Models RadImageNet was introduced as a radiologic alternative to ImageNet pretraining [ 6 ] . It contains 1.35 million annotated CT, MRI, and ultrasound images from 131,872 patients, covering multiple anatomic regions and pathologic labels. RadImageNet-pretrained networks improved downstream medical classification performance and localization consistency compared with ImageNet-pretrained counterparts, particularly for smaller radiology datasets. These properties make RadImageNet a strong 2D medical baseline for perceptual feature extraction. SegVol targets universal and interactive volumetric medical image segmentation [ 3 ] . It uses a 3D vision transformer image encoder pretrained with masked image modeling on large-scale CT volumes and fine-tuned on labeled volumetric segmentation data. SegVol supports point, bounding-box, and text prompts for more than 200 anatomical categories. Although its training domain is CT segmentation, its 3D medical representation may capture anatomical shape and volumetric consistency that 2D ImageNet networks lack. BrainIAC is a general-purpose foundation model for human brain MRI analysis [ 9 ] . It is trained with self-supervised contrastive learning on large-scale brain MRI and evaluated across sequence classification, brain age prediction, dementia prediction, tumor mutation prediction, survival prediction, stroke timing, and tumor segmentation. Because our downstream application is brain MRI contrast dose simulation, BrainIAC is the most domain-specific candidate and may provide the closest feature alignment to neuroanatomy and MRI acquisition variability. 3 Method 3.1 Candidate Feature Extractors We compare five pretrained backbones. VGG16 and ResNet50 are ImageNet-pretrained natural-image networks and represent conventional perceptual feature extractors. RadImageNet is a radiology-pretrained 2D model. SegVol is a volumetric medical segmentation foundation model. BrainIAC is a self-supervised brain MRI foundation model. For 2D backbones, single-channel medical images are normalized and replicated when necessary to match the expected input channel format. For volumetric backbones, 3D image patches or volumes are resampled to the checkpoint-specific input resolution. All candidate models are frozen during Stage I feature extraction. Feature layers are selected from intermediate and high-level blocks to balance local texture, anatomical boundaries, and global structure. Let ϕ \phi denote a candidate extractor and ϕ l ​ ( x ) \phi_{l}(x) the activation at layer l ∈ ℒ l\in\mathcal{L} for image or volume x x . 3.2 Stage I: Medical Representation Benchmark The purpose of Stage I is to select a perceptual feature extractor before training the dose simulation model. We evaluate frozen features on four public downstream applications highlighted by the RadImageNet repository and paper [ 6 ] : thyroid nodule malignancy prediction on ultrasound [ 7 ] , breast lesion classification on ultrasound [ 1 ] , ACL tear detection on knee MRI [ 2 ] , and meniscus tear detection on knee MRI [ 2 ] . These tasks cover both ultrasound and MRI, small-data transfer settings, and clinically meaningful binary classification labels. Linear probing. For each extractor, we freeze ϕ \phi and train a shallow binary classifier on top of pooled features for the four Stage I labels. Linear probing tests whether the pretrained feature space already separates clinically relevant disease categories without end-to-end adaptation. Retrieval consistency. For each query image x i x_{i} , we retrieve nearest neighbors in feature space using cosine distance or normalized Euclidean distance. Retrieval quality is measured by whether images with the same subject, sequence, anatomical label, or pathology label are ranked close to the query. This tests whether the feature space preserves semantic and structural MRI similarity. Robustness to intensity perturbation. Ultrasound and MRI intensities vary across scanners, protocols, and preprocessing pipelines. We therefore evaluate feature stability under intensity scaling, mild noise, and contrast changes that should not alter the underlying pathology label. A desirable perceptual feature extractor should be sensitive to anatomical and disease-relevant differences while remaining stable to nuisance intensity variation. Selection rule. Each extractor receives a rank for every Stage I dataset and selection metric. The final Stage I selection score excludes intensity-perturbation cosine similarity, because the ViT pipelines apply instance or min–max normalization that largely cancels global intensity shifts. We evaluate ACL tear detection and meniscus tear detection as two separate binary classification benchmarks. Stage II dose simulation data. Higher SSIM and lower MAE indicate closer agreement with the target enhancement at that step. 5 Discussion This study tests whether the feature space used by a perceptual loss matters for MRI dose simulation. The original VGG16 perceptual loss is convenient but domain-mismatched: it encodes natural-image structure, whereas dose simulation requires sensitivity to MRI contrast enhancement and brain anatomy. Medical foundation models offer a more aligned alternative because their representations are learned from radiology images, volumetric anatomy, or brain MRI specifically. Perceptual-loss performance is difficult to summarize with a single scalar endpoint. PSNR, SSIM, RMSE, and residual-uptake CNR quantify useful aspects of agreement with the acquired low-dose image, but they do not directly measure whether a feature space emphasizes clinically relevant enhancement patterns. This makes the visual results central to the Stage II interpretation. In Fig. 3 , RadImageNet better suppresses residual uptake in enhancing structures while preserving the expected low-dose appearance of surrounding tissue. The two-stage design is important. If a foundation model improves the final simulator, the improvement could otherwise be attributed to changes in architecture, tuning, or loss weighting. By selecting the backbone through an independent representation benchmark and then replacing only the perceptual feature extractor in the baseline objective, we make the comparison more controlled. Under this fixed-weight setting, the observed Stage II differences are most plausibly associated with the perceptual feature space. The traditional metrics in Table 2 move only slightly, but the enhancement trajectory in Fig. 2 and the image-space comparison in Fig. 3 show a consistent reduction in residual enhancement. The Stage I results also show that medical foundation models are not interchangeable. RadImageNet is radiology-pretrained and directly compatible with a slice-wise perceptual loss, which makes it practical for the controlled Stage II replacement. SegVol and BrainIAC encode different medical imaging priors, but they were not inserted into the dose simulator after Stage I selection. The Stage II conclusion is therefore specific to replacing VGG16 with RadImageNet under the original Gformer objective. Several limitations remain. First, Stage II contains only one controlled replacement experiment, from VGG16 to the Stage I-selected RadImageNet feature extractor. The resulting inference is therefore specific to this fixed Gformer objective and this selected feature space. Second, medical foundation models may encode biases from their pretraining data, so improvements on this cohort may not transfer unchanged to other scanners, contrast agents, or disease distributions. Finally, standard image metrics do not fully capture perceptual-loss behavior or diagnostic equivalence, so downstream clinical tasks and reader studies remain important future validation steps. 6 Conclusion This study evaluates medical foundation model features as perceptual losses for brain MRI contrast dose simulation. In Stage I, RadImageNet provides the strongest frozen representation among the eligible medical models across the public benchmark suite. In Stage II, replacing only the VGG16 perceptual feature extractor with RadImageNet produces modest endpoint-metric changes but clearer visual improvements in residual-enhancement suppression and low-dose appearance. These findings support radiology-pretrained features as a practical perceptual feature space for MRI synthesis, while the clinical relevance of the gains requires larger cohorts and reader-centered validation.
