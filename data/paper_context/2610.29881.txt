Mean gains are 0.0433 0.0433 in zero-shot balanced accuracy, 0.0201 0.0201 in linear-probe balanced accuracy, and 0.1330 0.1330 percentage points in retrieval. The experiment uses one seed and a short schedule, so these means do not establish statistical significance or the effect under full-data training. 6.3 Qualitative evidence The appendix collects two kinds of visual evidence. Before-and-after retrieval examples in Appendix H show individual ranking changes through fundus and OCT integration. Similarity-gradient visualizations in Appendices I and J compare spatial responses to finding descriptions and paired reports. For the linear-probe examples, the saliency target is image–prompt similarity rather than the image-only classifier’s class score. These selected visualizations complement the quantitative evaluations and do not measure localization performance. 7 Discussion and limitations The results support a distinction between acquiring new representations and preserving an existing embedding function. New experts provide trainable capacity for an added modality, while frozen original paths preserve earlier embeddings under the conditions in Proposition 1 . Whether the new expert reaches the strongest task performance remains an empirical question. In the matched-data experiment, the proposed technique learns both fundus and OCT while retaining earlier tasks, but its OCT retrieval score is below those of the adapted baselines. Retention and new-task performance therefore need to be assessed together. The broader baseline comparisons do not isolate a single source of the performance differences. Models differ in architecture, pretraining data, parameter count, and optimization. The proposed technique uses a large bank of stored experts, and the matched-data experiment does not match trainable parameter counts or computational cost. The ViT-B routing and tokenizer experiments provide more controlled evidence, but they use limited subsets and short training schedules. Their results do not establish the size of the same effects after converged ViT-L training. Our expansion rule assumes that the modality is supplied at inference and that existing preprocessing, persistent state, routing, and inference operations remain fixed. Unchanged embeddings imply unchanged task outputs only when scoring rules, candidate sets, and downstream classifiers are also fixed. The feature-identity checks cover evaluated inputs. A change to the assembled model’s learned logit-scale parameter is separate from those checks, so preserved features do not establish identity of every scaled logit. Evaluation also has limits. Some linear-probe splits operate at the image or data-row level rather than the patient or slide level, including HEST’s image-level split. The retention benchmarks sample capabilities across the base modalities, rather than testing every possible downstream use. The qualitative examples visualize similarity responses without a quantitative localization evaluation. These experiments assess representation learning; clinical use would require prospective validation in the intended setting. 9 Conclusion The proposed technique combines masked pretraining on unpaired medical images with contrastive image–text alignment and expandable modality experts. The resulting model supports nine imaging modalities and achieves the best or tied-best result in 24 24 of 25 25 classification, linear-probe, and retrieval settings. In a separate matched-data sequential experiment, new experts improve fundus and OCT retrieval while the evaluated preceding fundus and pathology tasks remain unchanged. The preservation argument follows from retaining the original computation paths under explicit inference conditions. Together, these results support modular expert expansion as a way to extend medical vision–language representations while retaining existing embeddings. At each routed image layer, we add the shared image FFN output to the output of the expert selected by the given modality ID: FFN ⁡ ( x , m ) = FFN shared ⁡ ( x ) + Expert m ⁡ ( x ) . \operatorname{FFN}(x,m)=\operatorname{FFN}_{\mathrm{shared}}(x)+\operatorname{Expert}_{m}(x). Here, m m denotes the image modality. Only the selected expert contributes to the layer output. However, this formulation does not claim that the implementation avoids every computation associated with unselected experts. Paired image–text training supervises the image expert; it does not add or train a modality-specific text expert. Learning a new modality with a frozen text encoder. Each training pair is processed by both encoders. The image passes through the frozen shared visual pathway and the trainable expert for the new modality. The corresponding caption passes through the frozen text encoder. A contrastive loss aligns the resulting image and text embeddings. We compute text embeddings for every batch, but we do not update the text encoder. Only the image-expert FFNs for the new modality are updated. In this way, the new expert learns to map images into the existing text embedding space without requiring a new text expert. Preserving existing pathways. During new-expert training, we freeze the shared visual parameters, all existing experts, the text encoder, and the projection heads. Existing modalities continue to use their original routes. When persistent state, routing, preprocessing, inference operations, scoring rules, candidate sets, and downstream classifiers also remain unchanged, the existing image and text embedding functions are preserved. The original 24-layer ViT-L core routes layers { 4 , 8 , 12 , 16 , 20 , 24 } \{4,8,12,16,20,24\} , whereas the full-data expansion model uses routed FFNs in all 24 layers. When we convert the remaining layers, FFN remapping and zero-output branches preserve the original computation for existing modalities. A new modality expert therefore contains FFN parameters at every routed layer, rather than a single FFN block. On the evaluated tasks, we verify that the saved features and predictions are bitwise identical.
