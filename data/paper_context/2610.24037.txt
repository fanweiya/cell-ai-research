2 Artifact Segmentation Methods Several methods have been developed for artifact segmentation. Some approaches utilize image metrics such as brightness, contrast, and sharpness, while others employ machine learning techniques, including deep learning. Algorithms can be categorized into those that distinguish artifact-free tissue from background and artifacts, and those that further identify specific artifact types at the pixel or patch level. Additionally, certain tools provide reports or recommendations for subsequent actions, such as re-scanning the slide. However, many of these tools are not publicly available, and often do not provide access to trained model weights or the training datasets, so cannot be used in practice. A comparison of recently published algorithms is provided in Table 1 . The supplementary materials provide detailed information about the errors that occurred during the launch of the tools. It not only enables the identification of artifact-free regions but also the discovery of outliers in the cohort, e.g., slides with incorrect exposure. It works by combining image metrics such as brightness and contrast, color histograms, and features from edge detectors and supervised classifiers to identify artifacts. HistoQC generates visualizations after each processing step and allows users to customize workflows as needed. Additionally, a comprehensive report for the whole dataset is generated at the end of the analysis. The tool was evaluated on 450 HE-stained WSIs from the TCGA BRCA dataset by two pathologists. The results obtained were in concordance with pathologists’ observations in over 95% of cases. Moreover, HistoQC was used for quality control of WSI kidney biopsies stained with other stains, such as Periodic Acid Schiff (PAS), Silver (SIL), and Trichrome (TRI) staining [ 9 ] . The source code of HistoQC, along with its documentation, is publicly available at https://github.com/choosehappy/HistoQC . In this study, HistoQC v2.1 was used in a Docker container with default parameters. It is important to note that the tool requires tissue images to be stored in a pyramidal format, with zoom-level information available in the file metadata. Therefore, it could not be applied to Dataset B, for which the magnification was known but could not be provided as an input parameter. It was assumed that the final mask file (with the mask_use suffix) represents the tissue without artifacts, and that all other pixels belong to the background or artifacts. Haghighat et al. [18] proposed a tool developed for automated quality assessment of WSIs called Pathprofiler. This algorithm performs patch-level classification using the ResNet18 [ 19 ] architecture, with a final fully connected layer producing six class scores through linear output units. The scores correspond to the following classes: usability, no artifact, staining artifacts, focus artifacts, tissue folds, and other artifacts. It also uses patch-level statistics to produce overall usability, out-of-focus, and staining scores for each WSI. Pathprofiler was trained on 198 FFPE HE-stained WSIs, comprising 198 prostate biopsy and transurethral resection specimens from two private cohorts. The tool was further evaluated on unannotated WSIs from 499 TCGA cases containing prostate tissue, including frozen tissue sections, as well as on the FOCUS dataset, which includes 788 WSIs from colorectal cancer specimens. For the FOCUS dataset, quality assessment was restricted to the tumor area. Principal component analysis (PCA) showed that FOCUS WSIs labeled as unusable overlapped with selected examples from TCGA and the annotated test dataset in the principal component space, suggesting that PathProfiler may generalize across different datasets. However, no formal analysis using patch classification or segmentation metrics was performed to substantiate this observation. The tool’s code is publicly available at https://github.com/MaryamHaghighat/PathProfiler (last commit: Jan 4, 2024). PathProfiler also does not support specifying the zoom level as an input parameter for tissue regions saved in the non-pyramidal format. In addition, the program did not execute successfully, likely due to issues with the multithreaded slide-processing implementation. As a result, this tool was excluded from the analysis. HistoROI [ 34 ] is a deep learning-based method for WSI quality control and patch-level artifact classification. It uses the ResNet18 architecture, trained with a human-in-the-loop active learning method, to predict one of six classes: epithelium, stroma, lymphocyte, adipose tissue, artifact, and miscellaneous area. The artifact class includes patches with out-of-focus regions, coverslips, tissue folds, air bubbles, staining problems, and marker traces. The model was trained on 50 HE-stained WSIs containing breast cancer tissue. The innovation of this method lies in training the model under human supervision to identify WSI images containing regions that are difficult to classify, and in manually correcting the model’s predictions during training to improve the accuracy of the final prediction. HistoROI was applied for quality control on a multi-organ dataset comprising 93 WSIs from the TCGA database, with manually annotated tissue segmentation. The average Dice score was 0.04 higher than the score obtained with HistoQC and was higher in 65 WSIs. However, during patch-level classification on the CRC-100k dataset, which contains colorectal cancer tissue, it misclassified over 23% of patches, raising questions about its ability to generalize to other tissue types. The tool’s code is publicly available at https://github.com/abhijeetptl5/historoi (last commit: May 23, 2025). Since the tool can identify both artifacts and specific tissue regions, such as lymphocyte-dense areas and adipose tissue, its multiclass predictions were converted into a binary representation for evaluation. The class explicitly labeled as an artifact, together with the background class, was considered non-tissue, whereas all remaining classes were classified as artifact-free tissue. The tool was not evaluated on dataset B because it does not allow specifying a zoom level as an input parameter for images saved as PNG files. Building on HistoROI, Patil et al. [35] developed a semantic segmentation algorithm that distinguishes artifact-free tissue, out-of-focus regions, tissue folds, and marker artifacts. The segmentation process utilizes four modules, each employing either a UNet [ 37 ] or a UNet++ [ 59 ] architecture with a ResNet18 or ResNet34 backbone. The authors evaluated the concordance between segmentation masks generated by HistoQC and those produced by the proposed tool across 11,666 images from the TCGA database, representing 28 organs. The Dice index was used to quantify agreement between the masks. In cases of low agreement between masks, pathologists more frequently judged the proposed solution as superior to HistoQC, although some segmentations from both methods were assessed as equally inadequate. The implementation of this method is publicly available at https://github.com/abhijeetptl5/wsisegqc (last commit: May 23, 2025). The blurring is quantified on a scale from 0 to 7, where 0 indicates no blurring and 7 indicates very high blurring. For evaluation, pixels were classified as out-of-focus if they received a non-zero value for that class. Additionally, the algorithm segments adipose tissue, which is considered artifact-free tissue during method evaluation. SliDL [ 4 ] is a Python library for pre- and post-processing of WSIs. It supports operations such as tissue and artifact segmentation, image tiling, and annotation management. SliDL uses a DenseNet architecture [ 21 ] for tissue and artifact segmentation. The output mask indicates background, tissue, and artifact regions. The model was trained on a dataset of 9,071 tiles derived from 393 annotations across 61 WSIs, scanned using various machines over different time periods and from multiple tissue types collected from two species. This diversity was intended to capture a wide range of WSI artifact variations, including pen marks, folded or torn tissue, slide bubbles, cracks, blurred or out-of-focus regions, uneven illumination, aberrant staining, and other marks. The artifact segmentation module was evaluated on a dataset comprising 36 HE-stained images from both public and private sources. In this study, the latest available version from the remote repository at https://github.com/markowetzlab/slidl (last commit: January 16, 2023) was used. However, the tool was excluded from the analysis because it failed to run, even though it was installed correctly according to the documentation. The error was caused by the model’s trained weights being inaccessible. While the article included a section on common errors and solutions, it did not address this specific issue. SlideInspect [ 40 ] is a recently developed framework designed for artifact segmentation and quality control. In addition to the pixel-level segmentation mask, it also takes into account information about the quality of tissue staining and provides feedback on the quality of the entire specimen in the form of a traffic light quality indicator (green, yellow, and red for slides of good, average, and poor quality, respectively). It also recommends actions to improve slide quality (re-scan, re-mount, re-cut, no action). For artifact segmentation, a K-Net architecture [ 57 ] with a Swin Transformer backbone [ 28 ] is used. Additionally, a machine-learning classifier is trained to produce the final recommended action for each slide based on stain quality and identified distortions. The tool also provides quantitative output, including the distribution of artifacts. The tool was evaluated on a large multicenter dataset comprising over 3,000 WSIs scanned across multiple scanners and four staining types. According to the authors, the framework demonstrates robustness across various tissue types, staining methods, and scanning platforms. However, this algorithm was excluded from the comparison due to the lack of a publicly available implementation. Smit et al. [43] developed a tool comparable to SlideInspect. It allows for multiclass artifact segmentation, including out-of-focus regions, tissue folds, ink, dust, markers, and air bubbles. The proposed method starts with tissue versus background segmentation using a resolution-agnostic CNN [ 1 ] . Artifact segmentation is then performed with DeepLabV3+ [ 7 ] employing an EfficientNet-B2 [ 44 ] encoder. Subsequently, the quality control module uses a decision tree to recommend one of four actions to improve slide quality: clean up, re-scan, re-cut, or no action. The training dataset consisted of 142 WSIs representing 9 tissue types and 8 staining types, scanned with 7 different scanners. Additionally, the algorithm was evaluated using a separate dataset of 30 unannotated images from an external data center. This algorithm achieved a Dice score of 0.91 on the internal test subset. Moreover, it demonstrated superior performance compared to HistoQC on the external dataset. However, information on improvements in segmentation quality metrics is not reported. The demo is publicly available on the Grand Challenge platform [ 17 ] . However, due to missing model weights in the GitHub repository, technical limitations, and potential legal restrictions on uploading medical data, this tool is not suitable for use in medical institutions. Consequently, it was excluded from the comparison. Schreiber et al. [39] developed a fast, simple thresholding-based method which is designed to eliminate artifacts such as pen markings and scanning artifacts. The algorithm begins by normalizing the RGB image to the range ⟨ 0 , 1 ⟩ \langle 0,1\rangle . Next, it performs element-wise multiplication on the outputs of two ReLU functions applied to the differences between the red and green channels and between the blue and green channels, respectively. Finally, Otsu thresholding is applied to segment the artifact-free tissue. The method requires no parameter tuning. The algorithm was evaluated on a dataset of 60 HE-stained duodenal biopsies scanned with different scanners, including 30 slides with artifacts such as marker traces and scanning artifacts. The method successfully segmented tissue in all cases and failed only once because it could not remove pink marker traces. This algorithm is relatively easy to implement, and the authors made the code publicly available. https://gitlab.developers.cam.ac.uk/bas43/h_and_e_otsu_thresholding (last commit: May 14, 2024). The tool assigns pixels to one of two categories: tissue without artifacts or background, and regions containing artifacts. In this study, tissue segmentation was performed at 10x magnification. GrandQC [ 51 ] is a state-of-the-art tool developed for tissue and multi-class artifact segmentation. It consists of two modules: the background versus tissue segmentation module and the artifact segmentation module. Both parts use EfficientNetB0 as the encoder and UNet++ as the decoder. The algorithm produces a pixel-wise segmentation mask that assigns each pixel one of the following classes: out-of-focus, pen marks, dark spots and foreign objects, air bubbles and edges (coverslip, broken glass), background, and artifact-free tissue. Tissue detection is performed at magnification 1x, while Artifact segmentation can be performed at three magnifications: 5x, 7.5x, and 10x. The lower the magnification, the shorter the computation time, with slightly lower detection quality. The multi-center dataset used to train the model consisted of 208 HE-stained WSIs for the tissue detection task and 420 WSIs for the artifact detection task. These two datasets partially overlapped. The tool was evaluated on a manually annotated dataset comprising 318 WSI images from a single pathology department, a subset of which was used during model evaluation in this study (see Section 3 for details). In addition, two pathologists evaluated the segmentation results on 600 WSIs from five medical centers. Their evaluation confirmed the algorithm’s usefulness for identifying regions unsuitable for analysis. Furthermore, it has been shown that the method can be used for quality control within a given laboratory and for evaluating scanning systems. GrandQC was evaluated using patch-level quality segmentation metrics and compared with existing methods, demonstrating superior performance over HistoQC, HistoROI, and PathProfiler. However, only average Dice index results were reported, which prevents the analysis of cases with good and poor segmentation, and no statistical analysis of the obtained results was presented. Additionally, the segmentation masks provided by the authors for images from the TCGA database contain an error: the inference script does not account for padding at the bottom and right sides of the image, which means that if tissue is present there, that entire region is excluded from the segmentation mask and treated as background, leading to a loss of information. In this study, the publicly available version in a GitHub repository was used https://github.com/cpath-ukk/grandqc (last commit Dec 27, 2025). For artifact segmentation, the 10x magnification model was selected to provide the most accurate segmentation results. In the next work, the GrandQC tool was used to perform quality control on 544 randomly selected WSIs to assess its usefulness in real laboratory settings [ 5 ] . The authors enhanced the original method by developing additional scripts to quantify the number of pixels associated with each artifact type and to generate an Excel file for statistical analysis, which they refer to as GrandQC+. The results demonstrated that GrandQC+ enables quick data quality assessment and facilitates the identification of low-quality slides. However, the developed scripts are not publicly available. HistoKit [ 32 ] is a publicly available tool for WSI processing. It supports tissue and artifact segmentation, patch extraction, data augmentation, and stain normalization. For tissue detection, HistoKit employs a thresholding-based segmentation method [ 31 ] that utilizes the GaMRed algorithm [ 30 ] . During tissue detection, the algorithm also removes low-chroma components and marker traces. For artifact detection, HistoKit combines the GrandQC artifact segmentation module with an improved inference algorithm. This enhancement introduces patch overlaps to minimize discordances at patch borders, aiming to improve segmentation quality, particularly in smaller or less convex tissue regions. The predictions in overlapping patch regions can be aggregated using the Constant or Gaussian modes. The first approach calculates the arithmetic mean of the confidence scores for each artifact class and assigns each pixel in the final mask to the class with the highest mean confidence score. Alternatively, a weighted average can be applied, with the weights determined using a Gaussian kernel with a specified standard deviation. HistoKit also provides confidence maps for the entire image for each artifact class. This feature allows users to exclude specific artifact classes from subsequent analyses. It also generates a detailed report of the artifacts found in each image. Although the tool has not yet been evaluated for its impact on segmentation quality using standard segmentation metrics, the reported visual results demonstrate a reduction in artifacts resulting from patch processing. In this study, version 1.0.1 of HistoKit, available at ( https://github.com/ZAEDPolSl/HistoKit ), was used. Tissue segmentation can be performed at any magnification level, but higher magnification increases the segmentation quality. Both tissue detection and artifact segmentation were conducted at 10x magnification. For algorithm comparison, the constant aggregation mode with 75% patch overlap was used to combine predictions in overlapping regions. GuardQC [ 50 ] is a tool that incorporates the GrandQC artifact segmentation module as its core component. The module generates confidence score maps and connected components, which serve as proposal inputs and are subsequently processed by a dual-stream ResNet18 model. This approach integrates local texture information with tissue context following dynamic multiresolution alignment. Then, the classifier head outputs the probability of artifact presence, enabling the exclusion of regions containing artifact-free tissue prior to any pixel-level refinement. To enhance specificity, iterative hard negative mining [ 42 ] is applied to prevent misclassification of dark cell nuclei as artifacts. Patches containing tissue likely to confuse the algorithm, such as those with cell nuclei and an artifact probability exceeding the 55% threshold, are combined with easily classifiable, artifact-free patches. This set is used for fine-tuning the model over 54 iterations. Finally, candidates with an artifact probability exceeding the threshold undergo local refinement via adaptive thresholding. For network evaluation, a subset of the GrandQC test set was used, consisting of 23 whole slide images (WSIs) from the BEETLE multicentric breast cancer segmentation dataset [ 27 ] . GuardQC reduced the number of artifact-free tissue pixels classified as artifacts in comparison with GrandQC. However, the tool’s source code is not publicly available, so it was excluded from the comparison. AIRAQc [ 16 ] is another deep-learning-based tool for quality control. The algorithm uses a multiple-magnification transformer architecture for tissue and artifact segmentation. It assumes that some types of artifacts, such as marker traces, can be detected at lower magnification than artifacts in out-of-focus regions. It processes images at three different magnifications (2.5x, 5x, and 10x) using a transformer architecture with shared weights across magnification levels, followed by cross-magnification feature fusion. Finally, a lightweight multilayer perceptron (MLP) decoder is employed to produce the final segmentation mask. This algorithm was trained on a large private dataset comprising 40,000 WSIs from various medical data centers. The training dataset included two staining types: HE and IHC. The tool was evaluated on 50 WSIs from the TCGA database, including 25 artifact-free slides. A comparison of the averaged Dice index for various artifact classes and artifact-free tissue segmentation demonstrated that AIRAQc outperformed HistoQC, HistoROI, and GrandQC. However, the results were not statistically analyzed, and confidence intervals were not provided. Moreover, the tool’s code is not publicly available. DiffusionQC [ 48 ] uses a distinct approach, treating quality control as an outlier detection problem. The tool has two architectural variants based on the PixCell-1024 latent diffusion model [ 55 ] . The baseline variant learns the distribution of artifact-free images to detect artifacts as outliers. The enhanced variant additionally uses a contrastive learning component to explicitly push artifact images away from clean images in the latent space. During training, precise artifact segmentations are not needed. Instead, images were divided into patches and classified as either containing artifacts or being artifact-free. A smooth segmentation mask is obtained by applying a straightforward post-processing pipeline that includes blurring the spatial error heatmap, followed by thresholding and morphological operations. However, the parameters used in this step should be selected by the user according to the specific dataset and staining method. To train the model, 50 HE-stained slides from the TCGA database were used, which were made available by the authors of the ARIAQc tool. The enhanced version of DiffusionQC outperformed GrandQC on the test subset in sensitivity, precision, and F1 scores. However, the training and test sets were obtained by splitting the same dataset. Furthermore, no confidence intervals or standard deviations were provided, making it impossible to determine whether the method achieves high-quality segmentation for all images. The tool’s source code is not available, so it has been excluded from this comparison. 3 Datasets Tissue quality control is typically performed before patch extraction. This process requires comprehensive information about the entire tissue area, as artifacts such as air bubbles or blurring may extend across multiple patches. Manual labeling of artifacts is highly time-consuming, thereby limiting the number of datasets available for benchmarking. Consequently, many datasets provide annotations only for separated patches of a fixed size and magnification, lacking information about the broader tissue context. In addition, datasets often contain only a single label indicating the artifact class assigned to the entire patch, without a precise segmentation mask [ 49 , 26 ] . Different algorithms often employ varying patch sizes, and significantly increasing patch size can introduce interpolation artifacts, potentially biasing results due to data availability rather than the algorithm’s performance [ 33 ] . Furthermore, evaluating algorithms solely at the patch level does not reveal their performance on tissue specimens, where distortions often occur at patch intersections. Without procedures to eliminate these disturbances, segmentation outcomes in these regions may be unreliable. To determine whether algorithms can address these challenges, this study employed three image datasets that include larger tissue regions: two publicly available datasets and one newly introduced dataset, thereby more closely simulating real-world laboratory conditions. The datasets vary in both the size of the tissue regions and the types of artifacts present. Figure 2 shows sample images along with segmentation masks from each dataset. Further details about the dataset are available in the supplementary materials. 4 Evaluation In the first task, the methods were compared based on their ability to distinguish artifact-free tissue from both the background and regions containing artifacts. In the second task, GrandQC, HistoKit, and [ 35 ] were evaluated for their ability to identify specific artifact classes. Artifact-free tissue segmentation can be formulated as a binary classification problem at the pixel level, in which tissue is the positive class and artifacts and background are the negative class. In this context, undetected artifacts were considered false positives (FP), undetected tissue regions were false negatives (FN), correctly classified tissue pixels were true positives (TP), and correctly classified background or artifact pixels were true negatives (TN). To compare artifact segmentation models, three performance metrics were employed: Dice coefficient, pixel sensitivity, and pixel False Discovery Rate (FDR). These metrics are defined as follows: D ​ i ​ c ​ e = 2 ⋅ T ​ P ( T ​ P + F ​ P + F ​ N ) Dice=\frac{2\cdot TP}{(TP+FP+FN)} (1) S ​ e ​ n ​ s ​ i ​ t ​ i ​ v ​ i ​ t ​ y = T ​ P T ​ P + F ​ N Sensitivity=\frac{TP}{TP+FN} (2) F ​ D ​ R = F ​ P ( F ​ P + T ​ P ) FDR=\frac{FP}{(FP+TP)} (3) All metrics range from 0 to 1. Higher Dice index and recall values correspond to more accurate artifact-free tissue segmentation. In contrast, lower FDR values indicate that more regions containing artifacts were segmented as artifact-free tissue. To compare algorithms for multi-class artifact segmentation, the Dice score was calculated separately for each class. Pixels associated with a specific artifact were treated as the positive class, while all other pixels were considered the negative class. Consequently, pixels classified as both tissue and an incorrect artifact class were identified as false positives. The Dice index was calculated only for images containing a given type of artifact, so the number of images may vary for each artifact type. In addition, an analysis of misclassified pixels in a given dataset was conducted. Supplementary materials include examples of unlabeled WSIs from the Compass NMD project, processed with algorithms that achieved the best results on annotated datasets. The Friedman test [ 15 ] was used to reveal significant differences among the methods for each dataset and evaluation metric. Pairwise comparisons were subsequently performed using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test [ 11 ] , with the Benjamini-Hochberg correction [ 8 ] applied to account for multiple comparisons. The proposed method was motivated by several observations about artifact morphology and co-occurrence. Air bubbles were frequently accompanied by out-of-focus regions, likely due to local blurring of the surrounding tissue. Moreover, these two artifact types usually form continuous regions without small holes. In addition, because the GrandQC model (on which HistoKit is based) also predicts the background class during artifact segmentation, small holes may appear within tissue regions. Moreover, small tissue areas may remain adjacent to artifacts when the model misclassifies artifacts. The proposed post-processing procedure was therefore designed to improve segmentation of these two classes, while leaving all other predicted artifacts unchanged. The area threshold for filling small holes and removing small objects was set to 0.025% of the total pixels in the WSI mask at 10x magnification. Defining the threshold relative to the image area ensured that the post-processing criteria remained proportional to the spatial resolution and dimensions of the analyzed slide. First, the mask assigned to the Edge class was processed using a morphological opening operation with a disk-shaped structuring element of radius 5 pixels. This operation removed small and irregularly shaped regions. Subsequently, all holes enclosed within the resulting mask regions were filled using a binary hole-filling operation, thereby producing compact Edge regions. Next, the Out-of-Focus mask was processed similarly. A morphological opening operation with a disk-shaped structuring element of radius 5 pixels was applied, followed by the removal of small holes using the predefined area threshold. The processed Edge and Out-of-Focus masks were then combined, and small holes within their union were filled using the same area threshold. This operation removed small gaps occurring between adjacent Edge and Out-of-Focus regions. Pixels introduced by this operation that did not belong to the Out-of-Focus mask were assigned to the Edge class. The Tissue mask was processed independently by filling small holes and removing isolated tissue regions according to the predefined area threshold. This reduced small discontinuities and spurious tissue predictions while preserving larger tissue areas. Finally, the processed binary masks were recomposed. Pixels belonging to classes not modified by the post-processing procedure were preserved from the original segmentation, and those originally classified as Edge or Out-of-Focus that were removed during morphological processing were reassigned to the Tissue class. The processed segmentation mask was reconstructed by combining the Tissue, Out-of-Focus, and Edge masks in this order. In particular, Edge regions representing air bubbles were placed as the final layer, reflecting the fact that such artifacts typically occur on top of the tissue section and may be surrounded by Out-of-Focus areas. The project repository provides the complete implementation of the proposed post-processing procedure. Additionally, the impact of overlap and results aggregation mode was tested. For the Gaussian mode, four different Gaussian kernel standard deviations were tested: 0.75, 1.25, 1.75, and 2.25. Supplementary Figures S3-S5 present heatmaps showing the median score for each dataset across all parameter combinations. In general, larger overlap led to better results. There are only slight differences between aggregation modes. Because processing time is significantly longer and the differences between 75% and 90% overlap are small, we recommend using a slightly smaller patch overlap. Statistical analysis using the paired Wilcoxon test and matched-pairs biserial rank correlation coefficient effect size showed that the proposed post-processing significantly increased Dice and sensitivity for datasets A and B (Supplementary Figure S6). Differences in FDR were not significant. For Dataset C, no statistically significant differences were noted after post-processing. Figure 8 shows the selected images before and after post-processing. For dataset A, most improvement came from closing holes in tissue. The largest decrease in Dice for dataset B was due to foreign objects in the form of dental floss classified as air bubble edges. Closing these areas increased misclassified pixels. For dataset C, the proposed approach closed holes in the air bubble, and differences for the worst Dice result are hardly visible. They yielded superior results for both artifact-free tissue segmentation and segmentation of individual artifact classes. The patch aggregation strategy employed by HistoKit also effectively mitigated distortions at patch boundaries, substantially improving segmentation performance compared with GrandQC. Furthermore, the proposed post-processing approach, which uses the characteristic properties of individual artifact types, improved segmentation quality in most cases. However, HistoKit requires a longer processing time than GrandQC. Therefore, users should consider the trade-off between computational efficiency and segmentation accuracy when selecting the most appropriate method for their application. It is important to note that the GrandQC artifact segmentation module is included in widely used libraries such as TIA Toolbox v2.1.3 [ 36 ] and TRIDENT [ 56 ] , which allow the use of the produced masks to extract patches for downstream tasks, including cell detection, tumor region segmentation, or feature extraction using widely used foundation models. Many of them use magnifications above 10x and typically employ smaller patch sizes, such as 256 × 256 256\times 256 pixels or 224 × 224 224\times 224 pixels. This means a single patch incorrectly predicted by GrandQC, measuring 512 × 512 512\times 512 at 10x magnification, translates into 16 patches of size 256 × 256 256\times 256 at 20x magnification and 64 patches at 40x magnification. More precise segmentation produced by HistoKit can significantly affect results from algorithms that directly use the artifact mask to exclude problematic regions. Our study showed that reporting only the average Dice Index across the entire dataset is insufficient to demonstrate one method’s superiority over another, largely because the metric distributions are highly skewed. To identify potential issues, we need detailed statistical comparisons and analysis of both poor and strong cases on several datasets. This underscores the limitations of tools such as SlideGuard [ 24 ] , which provide users only average Dice, precision, and recall metrics calculated across the entire dataset. Moreover, the current version of the benchmark does not provide information regarding method performance for specific artifact types. Since annotation masks are not publicly available, it is not possible to identify cases where the tools perform poorly or to verify the quality of the available annotations. Furthermore, the literature review indicates that many artifact detection methods are often not publicly available. Even when project code is accessible, it is often not maintained, and authors often do not provide the model weights. These challenges significantly hinder the ability to conduct benchmarks and to identify the strengths and weaknesses of a given method, thereby reducing confidence in its reliability and real-world usefulness. The model used by the HistoKit and GrandQC tools can also effectively segment common artifact types in fresh-frozen tissue samples not included in the training dataset, suggesting a potential for generalization across different tissue types and storage methods. However, the five main artifact classes identified in this study do not encompass all possible artifact types that may occur in fresh-frozen tissue sections, such as freezing and thawing artifacts, as none of the evaluated tools was specifically designed to segment them. Thus, preparation of a new annotated dataset or annotation of freezing and thawing artifacts in existing data is still needed. This study has several limitations. This study did not evaluate how improvements in segmentation quality metrics affect algorithm performance in subsequent tasks, such as cell segmentation or tumor region identification. Future research should investigate how the accuracy of artifact segmentation influences these downstream algorithms. Additionally, the analysis was limited to datasets containing only HE-stained tissues. Assessing algorithm performance on tissue specimens stained with different stains beyond those present in the training datasets would provide insight into the generalization capabilities of the evaluated methods. However, such comparisons remain challenging due to the lack of publicly available datasets with ground-truth annotations. 7 Conclusions This study compared publicly available methods for segmenting artifacts in HE-stained histopathological images across three diverse datasets, evaluating their ability to distinguish artifact-free tissue regions from artifacts and background and to segment different artifact classes. Moreover, a new dataset with precise artifact annotations was released to facilitate benchmarking of future methods. The findings demonstrate that evaluating segmentation algorithms on larger tissue regions, rather than single patches, is crucial for assessing their effectiveness in reducing disturbances at tissue patch edges.
