One visual word may cover patches that show different things, and a caption credits every term it names with the whole image, including the other things in it. Joint words and trust weights address the first, and caption-conditioned reweighting or joint estimation the second. None of them uses a text encoder, a gradient or an annotation, each reduces to plain counting at one setting, and each is used only where development data favor it. Joint words give each patch a finer label. The k k -means algorithm started from different random seeds draws the boundaries between words in different places, so each vocabulary wrongly merges different pairs of patches. A dataset uses at most one of the two corrections, the reweighting on COCO and the joint estimate on VOC. 4 Experimental setup On natural images, the CAPTOR table is built from the captions of a 15,000 15{,}000 -image subset of the COCO training split [ 10 ] , which contains no evaluation image. CAPTOR is evaluated on the 5,000 5{,}000 images of the COCO validation split and on the Pascal Visual Object Classes (VOC) validation set [ 11 ] . Two standard benchmarks are added. COCO-Object adds a background class to the eighty COCO object classes, as in [ 15 ] , and Cityscapes [ 12 ] contributes its 500 500 validation images. In pathology the table is built from the 32,463 32{,}463 PathCap [ 27 ] figures whose caption mentions one of 28 28 tissue terms. Three of these terms are merged into others, leaving 25 25 . Evaluation uses the pixel annotation of the Breast Cancer Semantic Segmentation (BCSS) dataset [ 28 ] and of LUAD-HistoSeg [ 29 ] . Section S1 of the supplement lists every dataset, split and setting (Tables S1 and S2 ) and shows that no evaluation image nearly copies a PathCap figure (Table S3 ). Natural images are scored under two protocols. The matched protocol scores COCO and VOC on a 14 × 14 14\times 14 grid over the center crop of each image, the grid both backbones produce without up-sampling, so every method sees the same pixels. A cell is scored only when one object class covers more than half of it. This keeps 162,215 162{,}215 of the 490,000 490{,}000 cells of the 2,500 2{,}500 COCO reporting images and 82,058 82{,}058 of the 284,004 284{,}004 cells of all 1,449 1{,}449 VOC validation images. Leaving out background isolates how each method scores terms, since each method decides background by its own rule. The standard protocol scores every pixel of the whole image, background included, with each baseline at its released settings and checked against its published number (Section 5.2 ). Natural images use DINOv2 ViT-B/14 with registers [ 17 , 30 ] at an input size of 196 196 pixels, and CLIP ViT-B/16 [ 9 ] . In pathology, CONCH [ 1 ] is trained on image-caption pairs, while UNI [ 2 ] and Phikon [ 21 ] are trained on slide patches alone and have no text encoder. All encoders stay frozen. The natural-image baselines run the authors’ released code of SCLIP [ 15 ] , ProxyCLIP [ 16 ] and Talk2DINO [ 5 ] , together with dense CLIP scoring, which compares each patch token with the text embedding of a term. Each uses its published prompts and scores a class at every patch by the best of its fixed names, which reads no annotation. The reported scores of CAPTOR include the propagation of Equation ( 13 ), so every baseline except on VOC is also scored with the same propagation, computed from the same DINOv2 tokens, and reported margins are against these propagated baselines. On VOC the baselines are compared as released. In pathology, the baselines score each patch by comparing the CONCH text embedding of a term with the patch features, following MaskCLIP [ 14 ] , SCLIP, and the smoothing of CLIP-DINOiser [ 31 ] along patch similarities. Each is given the same propagation, and each method, CAPTOR included, may use a per-term standardization of its scores that reads no annotation, chosen on development patients. CAPTOR takes no gradient and uses no text encoder, and captions are its only supervision for the table. The CLIP-based baselines read a text encoder trained on about 400 400 million image-caption pairs. Talk2DINO trains its alignment on the 82,783 82{,}783 captioned images of COCO 2014, more than five times the images CAPTOR counts. Every evaluation set is split into a development half and a reporting half, with each image, or in pathology each patient, in exactly one of them. For BCSS, 41 41 of the 103 103 patients form the development half and the other 62 62 are scored once. The development half and its segmentation labels choose the seven settings of Table S2 and the background threshold of the standard protocols. They also choose which components of Section 3.5 each dataset uses, and the eight terms that need their full name because the bare word is too general, such as “stop sign”. Every headline number is measured once on the reporting half, and curves and ablations that use all images are marked as such. As a check, K K is also chosen from captions alone, by how well signatures from one half of the corpus rank the images of the other half. This rule selects K = 2560 K=2560 on natural images and K = 320 K=320 in pathology, against 640 640 and 80 80 chosen with the development labels. With these values, CAPTOR reaches 0.569 0.569 mIoU on the COCO reporting half, against 0.565 0.565 , and 0.441 0.441 on the held-out BCSS patients, against 0.497 0.497 . A caption names a term when it contains one of the names of the term in WordNet [ 32 ] , a lexical database of English, such as “plane” or “jet” for “airplane”. Longer terms are matched first, so “teddy bear” is not also read as “bear”. Every difference between two methods is computed from their paired predictions. Its 95 % 95\% interval comes from a percentile bootstrap [ 33 ] that resamples the largest independent unit of each dataset, images on COCO, VOC and Cityscapes and patients on BCSS and LUAD-HistoSeg. A difference whose interval excludes zero is called resolved, and two methods whose difference is not resolved are called level, which means only that the data do not resolve a difference, not that the methods are equivalent. Objects and tissues are named in captions and bind well, while surfaces and materials are rarely named, so scene-parsing benchmarks built on them lie outside what the method addresses. The binding also inherits the level of detail of the captions, as lung stroma shows (Section 5.4 ). An object photographed almost always in one setting absorbs the words of that setting, which is where CAPTOR loses on COCO-Object. Attributes, relations and negation were not tested. A second limit lies in the partition. Patches that share a visual word are identical to every term under the hard assignment, so CAPTOR cannot separate what the encoder partition does not separate. The soft assignment (Table S20 in the supplement), the propagation and joint words relax this, and averaging vocabularies fitted with different seeds removes most of the dependence on any one partition. Captions supervise the table and development labels choose a few settings (Section 4 ), so CAPTOR is not free of supervision, and neither are the text encoders its baselines read. What it removes is gradient training on the pairs and the text encoder. 7 Conclusion We introduced CAPTOR, which estimates the matrix that scores every term against every patch from captions over the visual words of a frozen encoder, where a vision-language model needs a text encoder to form it. On natural images, CAPTOR leads the training-free baselines on COCO, Pascal VOC and Cityscapes, and it is level there with a text alignment trained on the same backbone. In pathology, CAPTOR gives a text interface to encoders that have none, from captions alone and without gradients. Through it, an encoder trained on slides alone names breast tissue better than dense scoring with a trained pathology text encoder. Grounding text in images therefore does not require a text encoder, only captioned images and counting. This opens grounding to many specialized domains in which aligned text encoders and dense annotations are unavailable or costly to obtain. The bars count joint words by the number of captioned images that contain them, and the red curve is the trust weight γ ℓ \gamma_{\ell} at λ = 3 \lambda=3 .
