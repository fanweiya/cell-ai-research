4 Experiments Datasets and Experimental Settings. Although histological images are commonly acquired alongside ST measurements [ 17 ] , large-scale, publicly available 3D ST datasets with paired histological images remain limited. We therefore select two datasets with relatively large numbers of sections, 3DST-RA [ 15 ] and HemiBrain [ 16 ] . To evaluate our method, we artificially withhold tissue sections to simulate missing observations. 3DST-RA contains paired ST measurements and histological images from serial synovial tissue sections of patients with rheumatoid arthritis. We select the patient with the largest number of sections, consisting of seven consecutive sections numbered from 1 to 7. ST measurements are retained for Sections 1, 4, and 7. Among the remaining four sections, three are used as image-only observations, while one is completely withheld for evaluation. We repeat this evaluation four times, each time selecting a different section to be completely withheld. The HemiBrain dataset contains serial brain sections from three mice. We use two samples, HemiBrainA and HemiBrainB, containing 42 and 9 sections, respectively. We retain both ST measurements and histological images at every fourth section. Among the three intermediate sections, the first and third are used as image-only observations, while the second is withheld for evaluation. Prior to training and evaluation, adjacent tissue sections are aligned using XFeat-based feature matching [ 18 ] and RANSAC-based similarity transformations [ 19 ] . These transformations are sequentially composed to register all sections to a common coordinate system, with the middle section as the reference. Evaluation-section histological images are also used for registration to ensure accurate spatial correspondence between predicted and ground-truth gene-expression profiles, but are excluded from model training, inference, and performance evaluation. Implementation Details and Evaluation Metrics. All experiments used PyTorch [ 20 ] with Adam [ 21 ] . Histology image features were extracted using the frozen UNI2-h [ 12 ] . The best model was selected based on validation performance, using 5% of ST spots randomly held out from each observed section. To evaluate reconstruction performance while minimizing slice-specific batch effects, we compute the Pearson correlation coefficient (PCC) [ 9 ] between predicted and ground-truth expression across spatial locations for each gene within each section, then average across genes and sections. Following standard ST prediction protocols [ 22 ] , we evaluate the top 50 and 250 highly variable genes (HVGs) to mitigate measurement noise. We report mean performance over three random seeds. Comparisons. We compare our method with six baselines. 1) “NN” and 2) “KNN” interpolate gene expression from the nearest and k = 5 k=5 nearest observed spots, respectively. 3) “STAGE” [ 6 ] aligns autoencoder latent representations with spatial coordinates, enabling prediction at unobserved locations through its decoder. 4) “3D-STINR” [ 13 ] and 5) “3D-SUICA” [ 14 ] are INR-based methods originally developed for 2D ST and extended to 3D using spatially aligned coordinates across sections. 3D-STINR directly regresses gene expression from 3D coordinates, whereas 3D-SUICA first learns gene-expression embeddings using slice-wise graph autoencoders and then trains an INR to predict these embeddings from 3D coordinates, which are subsequently decoded into gene expression. 6) “SINTER3D” [ 7 ] predicts gene expression using an INR with additional intermediate-feature reconstruction losses to encourage spatial continuity. Table 1 presents the quantitative comparison. Simple interpolation methods, NN and KNN, show poor performance. STAGE performs well on HemiBrainA but shows limited performance on other datasets, indicating a lack of robustness across datasets. This may be because explicitly aligning gene-expression latent representations with spatial coordinates restricts their ability to capture complex expression patterns. Similarly, 3D-STINR and SINTER3D show limited performance, potentially due to the difficulty of directly regressing high-dimensional expression vectors using INR. In contrast, 3D-SUICA achieves relatively consistent performance, suggesting that low-dimensional latent modeling facilitates INR training. Our method achieves the best performance on all metrics for 3DST-RA and HemiBrainB and on HVGs250 for HemiBrainA, demonstrating the effectiveness of histological supervision for continuous 3D reconstruction from sparse ST observations across datasets. To evaluate the effect of morphological supervision, we compare our method with “Ours w/o HIM”, which excludes image-based supervision. Both reconstruct gene expression in the same low-dimensional latent space, differing only in whether the shared INR backbone receives additional supervision from histological images. As shown in Table 2 , our method achieves relatively consistent and competitive performance across datasets, suggesting that dense histological supervision helps the INR learn spatial continuity more reliably than gene expression alone. To further assess histological supervision, we vary the number of missing ST sections (3, 5, and 7) on HemiBrainA, the largest dataset. The middle missing section is held out for evaluation, while the others provide only histological images. As shown in Fig. 2 , both methods perform comparably with three missing sections, whereas our method increasingly outperforms Ours w/o HIM as the number of missing sections increases. This suggests that histological supervision is particularly beneficial when sparse ST observations make learning spatial continuity from gene expression alone challenging. Validity of Histology as Multi-task Supervision. To validate the use of densely sampled histological images as auxiliary supervision in multi-task learning, we consider an alternative that first interpolates histology image features at unobserved sections and then uses them as direct inputs to predict gene expression. To evaluate this alternative without errors from histological feature interpolation, we train a linear probe on frozen UNI2-h features from ST-observed sections (UNI2-h+LP) and evaluate it using ground-truth histological images at the evaluation sections. Importantly, these images are unavailable in the actual reconstruction setting and are never provided to our method. As shown in Table 3 , our method outperforms the UNI2-h+LP baseline even when ground-truth histological images are provided at the evaluation sections. This suggests that histological supervision can be beneficial for learning continuous spatial representations beyond this simple image-based prediction strategy. 5 Conclusion We proposed HIM-INR, a histology-informed multi-task INR for continuous 3D gene-expression reconstruction from sparse ST observations. By jointly learning gene-expression and histological representations through a shared INR backbone, our method leverages densely sampled histological images as auxiliary supervision to capture spatial continuity between observed ST sections. Experiments on two 3D ST datasets demonstrated improved reconstruction over existing methods, with increasing benefits as ST observations become sparser. Importantly, our method enables gene-expression prediction at arbitrary 3D coordinates without requiring histological images at inference time.
