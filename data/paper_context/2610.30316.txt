3. Proposed Methodology 3.1. Research Motivation From the literature review conducted it is observed that most of the existing histopathology models for gastric cancer provide only binary or categorical outputs. They do not have the ability to compare and learn the different stages and progression trends in disease development across populations. Therefore, applying a model that can compare and learn enables systems to adapt over time to understand different stages and track them, which can be easily verified by clinicians at a much faster pace than raw data. Additionally, Deep learning models might face difficulties that are caused by variability that arises in capturing disease samples, which can be caused by the staining methods used on the equipment, which can lead to biases or deviations. Therefore, using methods like CL can help capture the underlying disease information instead of deviation from image variance due to similarity and dissimilarity learning. Simultaneously, Deep Learning models in histopathology often act as black boxes, which limits the trust and reliability of these models. Without clear explainability, the model predictions or results cannot be understood, as the decision process and the features that contribute to this decision are not known, which makes applying XAI a useful tool for transparency and understanding. Hence, this research focuses on incorporating these aspects into Gastric Cancer Disease Diagnosis. 3.2. Data Source: Gastric Cancer Histopathology Tissue Image Dataset The dataset used in this study is the Gastric Cancer Histopathology Tissue Image Dataset from Kaggle ( 26 ) , which was published by researchers from the Harbin Medical University Cancer Hospital. It provides a comprehensive set of high-resolution images of gastric cancer tissues. For the study, only two categories of NORM and TUM were selected as they provide a clear binary separation for the diagnostic contrastive learning framework. Each image is a non-overlapping path size of 224x224 pixels that is extracted from hematoxylin and eosin-stained whole slides. The images are processed and imported using PyTorch - compatible structures and undergo some augmentations to build the CL encoder and help with generalization. The two categories themselves comprise of 4000 image samples. Overall, the GCHTID dataset supports the development of efficient diagnostic tools that make it a valuable asset for advancing AI-assisted gastric cancer analysis. 3.3. Implementation Pipeline Figure 2. Implementation Architecture 3.3.1. Data Processing and Augmentations The dataset was loaded using PyTorch-compatible structures, with explicit class-to-index mappings defined as NOR → 0 and TUM → 1 to ensure consistency across training and evaluation phases. During supervised fine-tuning, a WeightedRandomSampler was applied to address class imbalance by oversampling the minority class to produce 6,000 balanced training samples per epoch. For the self-supervised contrastive learning phase, a custom ContrastiveTransform class was implemented to generate two stochastically augmented views of each input image. The augmentation pipeline comprised of: (i) RandomResizedCrop to spatial size 224×224, encouraging scale and translation invariance; (ii) RandomHorizontalFlip and RandomVerticalFlip to encode orientation invariance; (iii) RandomRotation up to 15°; (iv) ColorJitter with brightness, contrast, saturation, and hue perturbations (factor 0.5/0.5/0.5/0.1) to desensitise the encoder to staining variability; and (v) RandomGrayscale with probability 0.2. These transformations collectively encourage the encoder to extract representations invariant to non-pathological image variation while preserving diagnostically relevant tissue structure. For supervised fine-tuning, a lighter augmentation strategy was applied (RandomHorizontalFlip, RandomVerticalFlip, RandomRotation(15°), ColorJitter(0.3/0.3/0.3/0.05)), with test images subjected only to resizing and tensor conversion to ensure unbiased evaluation. Figure 3. Contrastive learning Technical Framework 3.3.2. Contrastive Learning Encoder The contrastive encoder was constructed using ResNet-50 as the backbone architecture. ImageNet pre-training was omitted (pretrained=False) to avoid domain bias because histopathological images exhibit different properties from natural images, and pre-trained features may encode irrelevant textures or colour statistics. The final fully connected layer of ResNet-50 was replaced with an identity mapping to expose the 2048-dimensional feature vector. A two-layer projection head was appended to map encoder features into a normalised 128-dimensional embedding space: Linear(2048→512) → ReLU → Linear(512→128), followed by L2 normalisation. This projection head is standard in modern CL frameworks such as SimCLR and has been shown to improve the quality of learned representations by separating the task-specific and task-agnostic components of the feature space. The model was trained using the Normalised Temperature-scaled Cross-Entropy (NT-Xent) loss with temperature T = 0.5. For a batch of N image pairs, NT-Xent treats the two augmented views of the same image as a positive pair and all other 2(N-1) images in the batch as negative samples. The loss encourages alignment of positive pairs while maximising angular separation from negatives in the embedding space. Training was conducted for 20 epochs using the Adam optimiser (lr = 1 × 10 − 3 1\times 10^{-3} , batch size = 64). 3.3.3. Supervised Downstream Classifier Following the CL pre-training, the learned encoder was transferred to a supervised binary classification task. The encoder weights were initially frozen, and a lightweight classifier head was appended: Linear(128→128) → ReLU → Dropout(0.3) → Linear(128→2). This architecture allows the classification head to adapt to the labelled training distribution while preserving the general representations learned during contrastive pre-training. After 3 warm-up epochs with the encoder frozen, the encoder was unfrozen and the entire model fine-tuned using differential learning rates: lr = 1 × 10 − 4 1\times 10^{-4} for encoder parameters and lr = 1 × 10 − 3 1\times 10^{-3} for the classification head, implemented via AdamW with weight decay 1 × 10 − 4 1\times 10^{-4} . A ReduceLROnPlateau scheduler (factor = 0.5, patience = 2) was applied based on validation accuracy. Early stopping with patience 5 was used to prevent overfitting. Training was conducted for up to 40 epochs with model check based on best test accuracy. 3.3.4. Model Evaluation The fine-tuned classifier was evaluated on the held-out test set using a comprehensive suite of metrics: overall accuracy, per-class precision, recall, F1-score, ROC-AUC, the Precision-Recall curve, and the confusion matrix. The Dice coefficient was additionally computed per class as: Dice = 2·TP / (2·TP + FP + FN), providing a symmetric measure of predictive overlap. All evaluations were conducted exclusively on the test set, which was not observed during any phase of training. 3.3.5. Tumor Progression Analysis with Contrastive Clustering To investigate potential intra-class heterogeneity within tumour samples, which may correspond to distinct progression substages, the trained encoder was used to extract embeddings for all TUM images from both training and test splits. The resulting embeddings were standardised using StandardScaler before clustering to ensure unit variance across all embedding dimensions. K-Means clustering was then applied to the scaled embeddings. The optimal number of clusters (k = 4) was determined using the Elbow Method as shown in Figure 4 of the plot of within-cluster sum of squares (inertia) against k from 1 to 10. t-SNE dimensionality reduction was applied to the standardised embeddings to produce a 2D visualisation of the cluster structure. A Random Forest classifier was subsequently trained on the cluster assignments using an 80/20 stratified train-test split to quantify cluster separability. Dice coefficients were computed per cluster to assess intra-cluster consistency. Essentially, direct clustering will only cluster based on color, orientation and superficial metrics, but a CL encoded embedding can help the clustering algorithm cluster semantically. Figure 4. Elbow Method 3.3.6. Explainability by Grad-CAM For interpretability, Gradient-weighted Class Activation Mapping (Grad-CAM) was integrated into the Streamlit interface. Grad-CAM computes the gradient of the class score with respect to the final convolutional feature maps of the ResNet-50 encoder, producing a spatial heatmap that localises the tissue regions most influential in the classifier’s decision. This enables clinicians to visually verify that the model attends to biologically plausible morphological structures rather than staining artifacts or tissue boundaries, directly addressing the transparency gap identified in the literature review. 3.3.7. Clinical Interface All trained components-the CL encoder, fine-tuned classifier, and clustering pipeline were employed within a Streamlit application to support interactive clinical inference. The interface accepts a histopathological image as input, produces a binary NOR/TUM classification with class probabilities, generates a Grad-CAM heatmap overlaid on the input tissue image, and optionally assigns the image to a tumour progression cluster when classified as TUM. This integrated interface is designed to function as a decision-support tool for pathologists, providing both a diagnostic prediction and an interpretable justification. 3.4. The clear geometric separation between clusters - with minimal inter-cluster overlap, confirms that the CL encoder has learned semantically meaningful features that extend beyond superficial pixel-level similarity, encoding morphologically distinct tumour substates in the embedding space. The proposed self-supervised framework achieves competitive performance while operating with substantially reduced annotation requirements. Gradient-weighted Class Activation Mapping (Grad-CAM) was applied to the fine-tuned classifier to generate spatial saliency maps highlighting the tissue regions most influential in each prediction as shown in Figure 9 . Warmer colours (red/yellow) indicate regions of highest gradient magnitude with respect to the predicted class, corresponding to areas most influential in the model’s decision. Across all three example patches, the activation concentrates on irregular glandular structures and regions of elevated nuclear density, morphological features consistent with the histopathological criteria applied by pathologists in gastric cancer grading. Notably, activation is absent from tissue boundaries, background regions, and areas of uniform staining, indicating that the CL Pre-training has successfully suppressed the encoding of acquisition-related confounds. This spatially coherent explainability output provides pathologists with a mechanism to verify AI predictions against their domain expertise, directly addressing the transparency gap identified. The models built were saved and incorporated into a Streamlit application, wherein users can input images and the model predicts if the image contains a tumor or is normal, and given that it is a tumor, it provides which cluster category it belongs to. This is also accompanied by a Grad-CAM image that depicts which region of the image causes the model to predict a given decision. This integrated output is designed to function as a decision-support tool rather than an autonomous diagnostic system, enabling pathologists to leverage AI-generated insights while retaining clinical authority over final diagnostic decisions as depicted by Figure 10 and the interface is depicted by Figure 11 . The interface is structured into three parts: (i) an image upload where the clinician submits a histopathological patch; (ii) a prediction displaying the binary NOR/TUM classification; and (iii) an explainability image presenting the Grad-CAM heatmap overlaid on the input image, highlighting decision-relevant tissue regions. When the model predicts TUM, a fourth progression grouping activates, displaying the assigned pseudo-stage cluster derived from the K-Means pipeline. The interface is designed to be non-disruptive to clinical workflow, a pathologist can upload a patch extracted from a scanned WSI and receive a structured diagnostic output within seconds, with the Grad-CAM overlay providing an immediate visual justification that can be cross-referenced against the physical slide. 5. Conclusion & Future Scope This study presents an integrated framework that enables contrastive learning for gastric cancer diagnosis and addresses the issues of traditional machine learning models. By utilizing ResNet50 based contrastive learning encoder, the model efficiently learns discriminative and generalizable feature representations directly from images and reduces dependency on large-scale datasets. The learned embeddings are then subject to a neural network for binary tumor detection and further clustered to identify pseudo-stages within tumor samples, thus capturing intra-class variations that reflect potential disease progression. A Random Forest Classifier is trained on the cluster labels and confirms high separability and robustness of learned features. The proposed system achieved an accuracy of 83% for tumor/normal classification and 97% in intra-tumor stage categorization that demonstrates strong representational quality and stage level differentiation. Overall, this approach not only helps diagnostic workflows but also bridges the gap between efficiency and clinical utility. The research can further be improved by validating the intra-tumour clusters identified by K-Means against expert pathological annotations or established clinical staging criteria (e.g., TNM staging). While the high computational separability of the clusters is encouraging, their biological meaning must be confirmed by qualified pathologists before clinical conclusions can be drawn. All experiments were conducted on a single dataset from a single institution, limiting assessment of cross-institutional generalisability which can be expanded. Future research will focus on pathological validation of the identified tumour clusters through collaboration with clinical pathologists. The framework can also be extended to multi-class tissue classification and trained on larger multi-institutional datasets to improve generalisation and robustness. Additionally, integrating whole-slide image analysis with multiple-instance learning and conducting prospective clinical evaluation of the deployment interface will help assess real-world diagnostic utility.
