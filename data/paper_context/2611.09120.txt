(1) We derived an improved theoretical bound for segmentation-like data using concentration inequalities. Specifically, we model the segmentation task as a Bernoulli random field, explicitly incorporating the variance into the bound, which leads to a tighter guarantee. (2) We revisited the binning approach for binary segmentation calibration, constructing a calibrator that satisfies both marginal and conditional calibration properties, in accordance with the aforementioned theoretical bound. Notably, we formally justified treating each pixel as an individual data point even in the absence of i . i . d . i.i.d. assumptions, showing that this choice not only admits rigorous guarantees but also proves effective in practice. In fact, the binning approach consistently achieves superior performance compared to the baselines, particularly in the conditional setting, where most alternative baselines fail to reach comparable levels of calibration across the majority of datasets. The primary limitations of the present work are as follows. First, our analysis is restricted to binary segmentation; an important direction for future research is the extension of the theoretical framework to multi-class segmentation. Second, while our theoretical justification for a binning approach in the segmentation setting remains valid and supported by the experiments, the bound requires empirical estimation, meaning that the guarantee is evaluated approximately rather than exactly. Moreover, deriving the final bound requires taking the supremum over configurations for both D σ D^{\sigma} and the variance p i ​ ( 1 − p i ) p_{i}(1-p_{i}) , which is potentially loose. Although our experiments show that, in general, the resulting bounds remain useful and tight, developing more refined and efficient strategies to sharpen them is a promising direction for future work. Finally, we are providing pixel-wise guarantees; a natural question is whether it is possible to bound the calibration error conditioned on specific regions of the image. Moreover, we observe that a larger number of bins is generally preferable: too few bins produce overly coarse intervals, increasing the risk of incorrectly reassigning the confidence values of individual pixels. Temperature scaling and Selective scaling do not change the accuracy of the model, hence are not discussed. C.2 Pre-trained Models and Data Splits U-Net We train a lightweight U-Net Ronneberger et al. (2015) from scratch for binary foreground segmentation. The encoder follows a standard contracting path with four downsampling stages: starting from a base width b = 32 b=32 , each stage doubles the number of channels ( b → 2 ​ b → 4 ​ b → 8 ​ b → 16 ​ b b\to 2b\to 4b\to 8b\to 16b , i.e. up to 512 feature maps at the bottleneck) and halves the spatial resolution via 2 × 2 2{\times}2 max-pooling. Every resolution level applies two successive 3 × 3 3{\times}3 convolutions, each followed by batch normalisation and ReLU activation; a spatial dropout layer ( p = 0.2 p=0.2 ) is inserted between the two convolutions to regularise training. The decoder mirrors the encoder with four transposed-convolution upsampling steps ( 2 × 2 2{\times}2 , stride 2), each concatenating the corresponding encoder skip connection before applying the same double-convolution block. A final 1 × 1 1{\times}1 convolution projects the b b -channel feature map to a single output logit per pixel; the sigmoid activation is applied externally. The model takes 3 3 -channel RGB images of arbitrary spatial resolution as input and produces raw logits of the same spatial size. This architecture has been used for the following datasets: ISIC 2016, BUSI, Kvasir-SEG and TN3K. Each training has been performed with 200 200 epochs, batch size of 8 8 and learning rate of 10 − 3 10^{-3} . Images and masks are resized to 256 × 256 256{\times}256 pixels; images are normalized with ImageNet mean and standard deviation. For ISIC, the dataset is already divided in train/val/test. We use the train and val splits to train the U-Net and the test split, featuring 379 images, for the calibration experiments. For BUSI, we use only the images with foreground (cancer). We train the U-Net with 439 images and use only 150 for the calibration experiments.
