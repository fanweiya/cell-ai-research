3.1 Dataset Construction Figure 3: Dataset composition of PCFBench. The 220K PCF samples span 18 canonical families. Each label gives the family’s sample count and proportion. PCFBench is constructed from a large-scale FDTD simulation corpus spanning 18 structurally diverse fiber families, including conventional telecom designs (e.g., solid-core hexagonal lattice, high-birefringence polarization-maintaining, multi-core), hollow-core anti-resonant fibers (e.g., single-ring ARF, nested ARF, kagome), and specialty geometries (e.g., plasmonic, vortex ring-core, fractal quasicrystal). The per-family sample distribution is summarized in Fig. 3 , with paired structure and mode-field exemplars for every family shown in Fig. 4 . Further details on structural motifs, guidance mechanisms, and applications are given in Appendix A.2 . Each of the 220K samples contains four components: (i) a geometric parameter vector 𝜽 = { Λ , d , t , … } \boldsymbol{\theta}=\{\Lambda,d,t,\ldots\} describing pitch, hole diameter, strut thickness, core size, cladding ring count, and family-specific attributes, (ii) a structural image at 336 × 336 336\times 336 resolution representing the refractive-index distribution, (iii) mode-field images simulated by FDTD at two telecom wavelengths, and (iv) multi-level expert annotations supervising geometry description (L1), mechanism and physics QA (L2), parameter–performance reasoning with actionable design suggestions (L3), and executable Meep code (L4). All simulations share a unified Meep FDTD configuration (boundary conditions, resolution, source profile, and run time, listed in Tab. 10 of Appendix A.6 ), ensuring comparability across families. Figure 4: Representative structures (left) and corresponding mode fields at λ = 1550 \lambda=1550 nm (right) for all families. The paired views reveal distinct guidance mechanisms: hollow-core families exhibit dark cores with bright anti-resonant rings, solid-core families show compact guided modes, and specialty geometries (plasmonic, vortex, multi-core) produce characteristic field patterns. See Appendix A.2 for additional exemplars per family. 3.3 Multi-dimensional Evaluation Suite PCFBench evaluates VLMs along two complementary axes. First, a single-VLM track assesses each model independently on every task using category-aligned metrics, with the full evaluation protocol in Appendix B.1 . Second, a multi-agent track decomposes the end-to-end design workflow into a four-stage Sequential Pipeline and benchmarks 12 additional multi-agent coordination strategies under identical inputs (Appendix D.1 ). To ground the inverse-design decision tasks in physics rather than surface-level heuristics, direction and closed-loop ground truth are derived from family-specific expert rules that account for non-monotonic regimes such as anti-resonant strut bands (Appendix D.5 ). A composite Human Score evaluated by two photonics experts across six dimensions (Appendix B.4 ) complements automated metrics by assessing both correctness and physical plausibility. All samples are newly simulated and previously unpublished, so none can appear in model pretraining data. A side-by-side comparison with prior benchmarks is in Appendix A.5 . 4 Experiments 4.1 Can Individual VLMs Bridge Perception to Design? Implementation Details. Per-metric breakdowns appear in Appendix C (Tabs. 16 – 18 ). Several main findings emerge. Perception is the easy part, but design is not. Gemini-3-Flash performs strongly on geometry perception, yet no model reaches 50% on topology recognition, indicating a gap in fine-grained lattice understanding. More importantly, perception correlates weakly with inverse-design success: Qwen3.5-122B-A10B leads in topology accuracy but achieves only 35.5% closed-loop success, while the smaller Qwen2.5-VL-7B reaches 63.8%. This perception–design gap holds across models, revealing a fundamental bottleneck in translating visual understanding into physics-aware action. Bigger is not always better. Scaling provides inconsistent returns. Within the Qwen-VL family, scaling from 7B to 235B improves topology recognition by 25 pp but degrades closed-loop success by 47 pp. GPT-4o-mini outperforms both GPT-4o and GPT-5 on closed-loop design, suggesting that instruction-tuning distribution matters more than raw scale for physically grounded tasks. Text generation and code syntax saturate. BERTScore-F1 for geometry description clusters in a narrow 0.27–0.36 band across models. For simulation code, syntax and parameter-modification completeness are near-saturated. End-to-end executability is in Appendix C.3 . VLMs write syntactically valid Meep scripts even when they cannot reason about the encoded physics. Paired prompts expose majority-class shortcuts. Several models score perfect macro-F1 on Field-only mechanism diagnosis by defaulting to the majority class, while scoring near zero on the Paired variant with all seven options. PCFBench’s dual-prompt design (Paired vs. Field-only) turns this shortcut into a diagnostic signal, since any model whose Field-only score vastly exceeds its Paired score is exploiting label imbalance, not reasoning about physics. 4.2 Does Multi-Agent Coordination Close the Gap? To move beyond aggregate metrics, we close the simulation loop on two representative fibers (Fig. 8 ): an ARF (moderate loss) and a dual-core coupler (near-floor loss). For each case, 11 systems propose a parameter change, which is applied to the original geometry and re-simulated with the same Meep FDTD solver. The resulting loss change, Δ = 10 ​ log 10 ⁡ ( L new / L base ) \Delta=10\log_{10}(L_{\text{new}}/L_{\text{base}}) dB, quantifies improvement (negative) or degradation (positive). On the ARF, 9/11 systems improve, six by 84 to 97 dB and three to a numerically zero loss, mostly by retuning the tube wall thickness. The dual-core coupler shows a complementary pattern: 10/11 systems improve despite a near-floor baseline, most of them by adding cladding rings. In both cases, success occurs when the task reduces to a single-axis, monotonic parameter change aligned with common training patterns. Results on 20 cases (Appendix D.6 ) show that this tractability is family-dependent, as nested and quasi-crystalline geometries that need coordinated multi-parameter changes stay unsolved. Tab. 7 shows that joint two-parameter edits change outcomes by at most one case, whereas three feedback rounds add two to five improved cases, so models gain more from feedback than from a larger action space. More cases in Appendix D.6 . Looking Forward. Closing the perception–design gap hinges on three directions. First, supervision should match the task: simulation-grounded structure–field–loss data provide signals absent from internet-scale pretraining, and domain-specific adaptation can outweigh scale. Second, closed-loop success depends on magnitude, constraints, and multi-parameter coordination, not only on direction, so training signals should reward feasibility, consistency, and bounded updates. Third, solver feedback should be integrated into the loop, via tool use at inference or as a reward for closed-loop tuning, offering greater gains than further scaling of models. 4.3 Analysis and Discussion Does Scaling Help? To move beyond aggregate metrics, we close the simulation loop on two representative fibers (Fig. 8 ): an ARF (moderate loss) and a dual-core coupler (near-floor loss). For each case, 11 systems propose a parameter change, which is applied to the original geometry and re-simulated with the same Meep FDTD solver. The resulting loss change, Δ = 10 ​ log 10 ⁡ ( L new / L base ) \Delta=10\log_{10}(L_{\text{new}}/L_{\text{base}}) dB, quantifies improvement (negative) or degradation (positive). On the ARF, 9/11 systems improve, six by 84 to 97 dB and three to a numerically zero loss, mostly by retuning the tube wall thickness. The dual-core coupler shows a complementary pattern: 10/11 systems improve despite a near-floor baseline, most of them by adding cladding rings. In both cases, success occurs when the task reduces to a single-axis, monotonic parameter change aligned with common training patterns. Results on 20 cases (Appendix D.6 ) show that this tractability is family-dependent, as nested and quasi-crystalline geometries that need coordinated multi-parameter changes stay unsolved. Tab. 7 shows that joint two-parameter edits change outcomes by at most one case, whereas three feedback rounds add two to five improved cases, so models gain more from feedback than from a larger action space. More cases in Appendix D.6 . Looking Forward. Closing the perception–design gap hinges on three directions. First, supervision should match the task: simulation-grounded structure–field–loss data provide signals absent from internet-scale pretraining, and domain-specific adaptation can outweigh scale. Second, closed-loop success depends on magnitude, constraints, and multi-parameter coordination, not only on direction, so training signals should reward feasibility, consistency, and bounded updates. Third, solver feedback should be integrated into the loop, via tool use at inference or as a reward for closed-loop tuning, offering greater gains than further scaling of models. 5 Conclusion This paper proposes PCFBench, the first comprehensive benchmark for PCF inverse design with vision-language models, built on 220K FDTD-simulated samples across 18 fiber families. It defines 26 tasks from perception to design and evaluates 41 VLMs alongside 13 MAS. Our results reveal a persistent perception–design gap : accurate geometry recognition fails to translate into physically grounded design decisions, even under scaling or multi-agent coordination. Closed-loop re-simulation shows that success largely reduces to single-axis edits, while multi-parameter geometries remain challenging. PCFBench serves as a testbed for simulation-in-the-loop training, VLM-based agentic design, and solver-coupled decision making. It serves polarization-insensitive transport and OAM conversion. • Hybrid Plasmonic : a PCF incorporating metallic (typically gold) inclusions that excite surface plasmons resonant with the fundamental core mode. It is the standard geometry for ultra-sensitive surface-plasmon-resonance biosensing. • Fractal Quasicrystal : an aperiodic cladding built from a fractal (Penrose- or Vogel-like) lattice of holes, studied for engineered dispersion, slow light, and on-chip photonic computing.
