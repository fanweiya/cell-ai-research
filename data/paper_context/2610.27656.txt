V Experimental Setup V-A Evaluation Protocol We evaluate whether policy-guided domain allocation improves grounding with domain-specific adapters and whether the sampling benefit also holds with a shared adapter. The domain-specific comparison uses five seeds (1, 18, 42, 123, and 456); the shared-adapter comparison uses eight seeds. Each comparison holds the adapter architecture fixed. We then examine alternative sampling strategies, domain-wise performance, and replay and final-balancing ablations over seeds 42, 1, and 18. A separate 200-update study tests the effect of a larger training budget. Cross-architecture differences provide system-level context because the seed sets, adapter placement, and trainable capacity differ. All adapted models start from the released MedReasoner-Soft-7B checkpoint. The main setting uses 25 optimizer updates; Section VI-G examines 200 updates. We match optimizer updates and gradient-bearing rollouts, not total generation or wall-clock cost. A 25-update run contains 200 gradient-bearing responses. PACE additionally probes at initialization and after updates 5, 10, 15, and 20. Each event evaluates 56 held-out queries with two responses per query, totaling 560 no-gradient responses. A representative PACE run takes about 65–66 minutes on four GPUs; this timing alone does not establish a cost advantage over Vanilla. V-B Dataset and Splits We use the released category-shift protocol derived from U-MRG [ 1 ] . We denote its 6,590-example gradient-bearing update split as U-MRG-6K; the probe and test sets are separate held-out partitions and are not included in the “6K” name. The update split contains 2,717 CT, 252 dermoscopy, 134 digital mammography, 417 endoscopy, 2,023 MRI, 254 ultrasound, and 793 X-ray cases. The fixed probe buffer contains 56 examples, eight per modality. Probe examples guide domain sampling but do not contribute gradient-bearing responses. The held-out test split contains 1,297 unique samples and is used for evaluation, not domain scoring. V-C Implementation Details All runs start from the downloaded released MedReasoner-Soft-7B checkpoint; the reference policy and MedSAM2 remain frozen. Each 25-update run uses four queries per update and two responses per query. Maximum prompt/response lengths are 2,048/256 tokens, learning rate is 10 − 5 10^{-5} , and rollouts use temperature 1.0 and top- p p 1.0 with top- k k disabled. Shared-adapter Vanilla/PACE runs use the original rank-64 attention LoRA on query, key, value, and output projections. Domain-MoE runs use seven rank-16 domain-specific FFN LoRA adapters on gate, up, and down projections. Training uses four GPUs on one node. All optimizer and GRPO settings not listed below are kept identical to the released MedReasoner configuration across compared methods. PACE probes at five-update intervals. It starts with two domains, admits at most two domains at an expansion event, uses replay ratio ρ = 0.20 \rho=0.20 , and reserves the final 20% of updates for all-domain sampling. The deficit mixture weight and temperature in Eq. ( 14 ) are 0.50 and 0.25. The weights on mean reward, reward dispersion, and validity in Eq. ( 10 ) are 0.45, 0.30, and 0.25, and the EMA decay in Eq. ( 9 ) is 0.60. (Eq. 13 ), we set τ c = 0.60 \tau_{c}=0.60 and τ p = 0.01 \tau_{p}=0.01 . A plateau is declared when | C s − C s − 1 | < τ p |C_{s}-C_{s-1}|<\tau_{p} for P = 2 P=2 consecutive probe events. These values were fixed before the multi-seed comparisons and kept unchanged across methods and seeds. A systematic sensitivity analysis is left for future work. VII Discussion VII-A What Does Policy Feedback Contribute? The full-system comparison evaluates the combined design, whereas the within-architecture comparisons assess adaptive sampling without changing adapter placement or capacity. The latter show a positive mean sampling gain in both adapter architectures. The fixed-order control retains admission, replay, and final balancing, but freezes the initial ranking. Its lower seed-42 result suggests that updating the order matters beyond merely dividing training into stages. The ablations also show that prioritization alone is insufficient in the tested setting. Replay keeps earlier domains in the sampling distribution, while final balancing increases the probability of lower-reward domains. Removing either component brings the three-seed mean close to Vanilla-25. The practical role of the complete schedule is therefore to combine selective training with continued domain coverage. This observation supports the design without identifying catastrophic forgetting as the cause of an ablation’s lower score. VII-B Domain-Specific Adaptation and Training Cost Sampling controls how often each domain is trained; the known label selects the parameters that receive its updates. Neither decision requires changing the GRPO advantage or reward. The design shares one frozen backbone and activates one adapter per sample, although all seven adapters must be stored. Probe generation also adds computation. Consequently, the experiments support better grounding at a fixed optimizer-update budget, not a reduction in total generation or wall-clock cost. VII-C Limitations The evidence covers one benchmark and one backbone. The shared-adapter and domain-specific comparisons use eight and five seeds, respectively, but do not provide a capacity- and placement-matched estimate of the adapter effect. The domain-wise aggregate analysis is available for the shared-adapter pair, not for the complete PACE-MoE system. The probe size and scheduler hyperparameters also lack systematic sensitivity analysis. Hard routing assumes correct imaging-domain labels; missing, mixed, and noisy labels are not evaluated. The experiments do not include compute-matched controls or direct implementations of recent bandit and prioritized-replay samplers. Finally, box and point grounding do not measure final mask quality or clinical utility. Broader validation should examine these factors and training budgets beyond the repeated-seed 25-update setting. VII-C Limitations The evidence covers one benchmark and one backbone. Broader validation should examine these factors and training budgets beyond the repeated-seed 25-update setting. VIII Conclusion We presented PACE-MoE for domain-aware medical visual grounding. Its core idea is to use policy feedback to allocate training queries and the known domain label to assign their updates to domain-specific adapters. This coordinates data allocation and model adaptation while preserving shared pretrained representations and the original GRPO objective. Across seven imaging domains, the complete method reaches 36.12% mean box IoU after 25 updates, a 3.64% relative improvement over Vanilla with fixed sampling and a shared adapter. With the domain-specific adapter architecture held fixed, adaptive sampling provides a 2.07% relative gain over fixed sampling; a positive sampling gain also holds with shared adapters. Ablations support combining adaptive admission with replay and final balancing. These findings motivate further study of coordinated domain sampling and adaptation across backbones, datasets, and training budgets.
