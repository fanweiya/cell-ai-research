Given an input pathology patch x i x_{i} , 𝒜 P \mathcal{A}_{P} initializes the memory state m i m_{i} , 𝒜 R \mathcal{A}_{R} evaluates cell presence, stain quality, and histologic clarity, and 𝒜 V \mathcal{A}_{V} performs closed-loop verification to produce the final quality scores. II-A Overview As illustrated in Figure 2 , we propose a unified three-stage framework that tightly integrates automated data quality control, generative prior learning, and downstream segmentation. First, we introduce an agent-driven screening mechanism to evaluate and filter raw pathology patches, yielding a highly curated, large-scale dataset (TCGA-200K). Second, we leverage this refined dataset to train a Diffusion Transformer (DiT) via Flow Matching, enabling the backbone to learn robust structural and semantic generative priors rather than serving merely as a data synthesizer. Finally, we reformulate downstream semantic segmentation as a transfer learning paradigm, where a lightweight multi-level decoder is appended to the pre-trained DiT backbone to map the extracted multi-scale features to precise pixel-level predictions. The effectiveness and generalization ability of this unified pipeline are comprehensively validated on both the public PanNuke dataset and our newly proposed PathSeg-380K dataset. II-B Stage I: Agent-Driven Pathology Data Curation Current generative modeling in pathology is constrained by two major challenges: the limited scale of existing datasets, which often contain only a few thousand images, and the lack of rigorous quality control. As a result, low-quality samples, such as patches with sparse cellular content or out-of-focus blur, can substantially degrade model performance. To address this, we propose an agent-driven and scalable quality curation pipeline with a semi-supervised design. Specifically, we first construct a large-scale pathology dataset, denoted as 𝒟 \mathcal{D} , from multiple public TCGA cohorts [ 40 ] , and partition it into a small labeled pilot set 𝒟 P \mathcal{D}_{P} and a large unlabeled pool 𝒟 U \mathcal{D}_{U} . A Multi-dimensional Assessment Pathology Expert Agent is then employed to annotate 𝒟 P \mathcal{D}_{P} across three key quality dimensions, providing supervision for training a lightweight regressor. The trained regressor is subsequently used to score the much larger unlabeled pool 𝒟 U \mathcal{D}_{U} , enabling scalable quality estimation in a semi-supervised manner. Finally, a multi-dimensional quantile filtering strategy is applied to extract a high-fidelity subset 𝒟 ∗ \mathcal{D}^{*} , resulting in TCGA-200K , a curated dataset of over 200,000 high-quality pathology patches for generative modeling. II-B 1 Large-Scale Pathology Dataset Construction As illustrated in Fig. 1 (a), we source a diverse set of Whole Slide Images (WSIs) across five cancer types from TCGA: Breast Invasive Carcinoma (BRCA), Cervical Squamous Cell Carcinoma (CESC), Colon Adenocarcinoma (COAD), Kidney Renal Clear Cell Carcinoma (KIRC), and Liver Hepatocellular Carcinoma (LIHC). We employ the CLAM toolkit [ 41 ] to process these WSIs into a comprehensive dataset 𝒟 \mathcal{D} containing approximately 14 million 256 × 256 256\times 256 patches. While this preprocessing effectively removes the majority of the white background regions, the retained patches inevitably exhibit localized artifacts (e.g., tissue folding, out-of-focus blurring, and uneven staining) that can destabilize training dynamics. To facilitate our agent-driven quality curation, we randomly partition the full dataset 𝒟 \mathcal{D} into two disjoint subsets: a small pilot set 𝒟 P \mathcal{D}_{P} and a massive unannotated target pool 𝒟 U \mathcal{D}_{U} . II-B 2 Pathology Expert Agent for Quality Supervision To obtain reliable quality pseudo-labels without exhaustive manual annotation, we design a Multi-dimensional Assessment Pathology Expert Agent. The agent follows a structured Perception–Reasoning–Verification workflow to evaluate the visual quality and pathological content of each patch in the pilot set 𝒟 P \mathcal{D}_{P} . As shown in Fig. 3 , for each image x i ∈ 𝒟 P x_{i}\in\mathcal{D}_{P} , the agent’s perception module 𝒜 P \mathcal{A}_{P} first conducts a global inspection to identify tissue types and prominent artifacts ( e.g. , folding or blurring). This preliminary step initializes a contextual memory state m i = 𝒜 P ​ ( x i ) m_{i}=\mathcal{A}_{P}(x_{i}) that guides subsequent decision-making. Conditioned on m i m_{i} , the reasoning module 𝒜 R \mathcal{A}_{R} then autonomously analyzes three pathologically critical dimensions: cell presence ( C C ), stain quality ( S S ), and histologic clarity ( H H ). Instead of directly outputting ratings, the agent is mandated to explicitly generate a diagnostic rationale r i d r_{i}^{d} before taking a preliminary scoring action s ~ i d ∈ { 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 } \tilde{s}_{i}^{d}\in\{1,2,3,4,5\} for each dimension d ∈ { C , S , H } d\in\{C,S,H\} : ( r i d , s ~ i d ) = 𝒜 R ​ ( x i , m i , d ) . (r_{i}^{d},\tilde{s}_{i}^{d})=\mathcal{A}_{R}(x_{i},m_{i},d). (1) To ensure the robustness of the supervision, the agent enters a self-verification stage via the verification module 𝒜 V \mathcal{A}_{V} to examine internal consistency across dimensions. For example, high cellularity with low clarity may indicate a poorly focused region, prompting the agent to revisit its initial assessment. The verification module then jointly considers the image, preliminary rationales, and actions to resolve inconsistencies and produce the final quality score vector: 𝐬 i = 𝒜 V ​ ( x i , { ( r i d , s ~ i d ) } d ∈ { C , S , H } ) , \mathbf{s}_{i}=\mathcal{A}_{V}\left(x_{i},\{(r_{i}^{d},\tilde{s}_{i}^{d})\}_{d\in\{C,S,H\}}\right), (2) where 𝐬 i = [ s i C , s i S , s i H ] ⊤ \mathbf{s}_{i}=[s_{i}^{C},s_{i}^{S},s_{i}^{H}]^{\top} . This closed-loop mechanism reinforces reliability, yielding an expert-supervised pilot set 𝒟 P = { ( x i , 𝐬 i ) } i = 1 N \mathcal{D}_{P}=\{(x_{i},\mathbf{s}_{i})\}_{i=1}^{N} , where N N is the number of sampled images. II-B 3 Learning the Quality Regressor Applying our agent directly to the massive pool 𝒟 U \mathcal{D}_{U} incurs prohibitive computational overhead. To overcome this, we adopt a semi-supervised paradigm, training a lightweight convolutional neural network ( i.e. , ResNet-50 [ 32 ] ) as a quality regressor, denoted as f θ f_{\theta} , to approximate the expert agent. Using the labeled pilot set 𝒟 P \mathcal{D}_{P} , the regressor maps a raw input image x i x_{i} to continuous predicted scores 𝐬 ^ i = f θ ​ ( x i ) \hat{\mathbf{s}}_{i}=f_{\theta}(x_{i}) . The model is optimized to minimize the discrepancy between its predictions and the agent’s scores using the Mean Squared Error (MSE) objective: ℒ reg = 1 N ​ ∑ i = 1 N ‖ f θ ​ ( x i ) − 𝐬 i ‖ 2 2 . \mathcal{L}_{\text{reg}}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left\|f_{\theta}(x_{i})-\mathbf{s}_{i}\right\|_{2}^{2}. (3) Once optimized, f θ f_{\theta} can be deployed for real-time quality estimation across the entire target pool 𝒟 U \mathcal{D}_{U} , preserving expert-level accuracy while bypassing the Pathology Expert Agent’s inference latency. II-B 4 Scoring and Quantile-based Selection Fig. 7: Visualizing the refinement of feature activations in the DiT generative prior. Moving from shallow to deep layers, the model sequentially learns to disentangle semantic structures at various scales, such as individual cells, glandular tissues, and the surrounding background, which provides a crucial foundation for robust semantic segmentation. We deploy the trained regressor f θ f_{\theta} to evaluate all images in the unannotated pool 𝒟 U \mathcal{D}_{U} . For each x j ∈ 𝒟 U x_{j}\in\mathcal{D}_{U} , we obtain the predicted multi-dimensional scores: 𝐬 ^ j = [ s ^ j C , s ^ j S , s ^ j H ] ⊤ . \hat{\mathbf{s}}_{j}=[\hat{s}_{j}^{C},\hat{s}_{j}^{S},\hat{s}_{j}^{H}]^{\top}. (4) To establish a unified ranking criterion, we compute the empirical score distributions for each quality dimension across the entire target pool 𝒟 U \mathcal{D}_{U} . Rather than relying on rigid absolute thresholds, which can be sensitive to domain shifts, we implement a quantile-based selection strategy. Specifically, let τ d \tau_{d} denote the score threshold corresponding to the top k % k\% of the distribution for dimension d ∈ { C , S , H } d\in\{C,S,H\} . A patch is selected if and only if its predicted scores all satisfy this top k % k\% criterion across all three critical dimensions. Formally, the filtered subset 𝒟 ∗ \mathcal{D}^{*} is defined as: 𝒟 ∗ = { x j ∈ 𝒟 U ∣ s ^ j C ≥ τ C ∧ s ^ j S ≥ τ S ∧ s ^ j H ≥ τ H } . \mathcal{D}^{*}=\left\{x_{j}\in\mathcal{D}_{U}\mid\hat{s}_{j}^{C}\geq\tau_{C}\land\hat{s}_{j}^{S}\geq\tau_{S}\land\hat{s}_{j}^{H}\geq\tau_{H}\right\}. (5) This strict filtering ensures that no single severe defect (e.g., severe out-of-focus blur, despite adequate cellularity) compromises the overall utility of the image. Following this process, we distill the massive unannotated pool down to a highly refined subset comprising 217,030 premium-quality patches, which we designate as TCGA-200K . Quantitative analysis reveals a substantial enhancement in data quality: compared to the unfiltered pool 𝒟 U \mathcal{D}_{U} , as shown in Fig. 4 , the average predicted scores in TCGA-200K improved by approximately 48.7% (3.065 → \rightarrow 4.558), 22.4% (3.631 → \rightarrow 4.446), and 21.4% (3.607 → \rightarrow 4.379) across the cell presence, histologic clarity, and stain quality dimensions, respectively. III-B Implementation Details In Stage II, we train the pathology generative prior using a DiT backbone with the objective defined in Equation 7 . The model takes 256 × 256 256\times 256 patches as input, featuring a patch size of 8 8 , an embedding dimension of 768, 12 Transformer blocks, and 12 attention heads. We employ the AdamW optimizer with a learning rate of 1 × 10 − 4 1\times 10^{-4} and a weight decay of 1 × 10 − 4 1\times 10^{-4} . The model is trained for 200,000 iterations with a total batch size of 16. In Stage III, the segmentation framework is initialized with the DiT backbone pretrained in Stage II. We optimize the network using AdamW, explicitly applying a learning rate of 1 × 10 − 5 1\times 10^{-5} to the backbone and 3 × 10 − 4 3\times 10^{-4} to the newly initialized segmentation head. To ensure fair evaluation, we employ a 3-fold cross-validation strategy, training the model for 20 epochs per fold using the loss function defined in Eq. 12 . All experiments are conducted in parallel across four NVIDIA A6000 GPUs. III-C Reliability of Agent-based Quality Assessment We implement the pathology expert agent using GPT-4.1 mini (“gpt-4.1-mini-2025-04-14”) and evaluate its scoring reliability on an independent set of 600 pathology patches, which are excluded from the pilot set used to train the quality regressor. Two pathologists independently assess each patch in terms of cell presence, stain quality, and histologic clarity. Samples with a score difference of two or more points are jointly reviewed to obtain the final consensus score. We use the pathologist consensus as the reference and report Spearman’s correlation [ 56 ] for each quality dimension. Mean absolute error (MAE) and quadratic weighted Cohen’s κ \kappa [ 57 ] are averaged across the three dimensions. As shown in Table II , the two pathologists show consistent assessments, with Spearman’s correlations above 0.81 and an average weighted κ \kappa of 0.827. The agent reaches correlations of 0.782, 0.811, and 0.829 for cell presence, stain quality, and histologic clarity, respectively. Its average MAE is 0.326, while the weighted κ \kappa against the pathologist consensus is 0.814, close to the agreement between the two pathologists. The quality regressor shows a small decrease against the consensus, with an MAE of 0.378 and a weighted κ \kappa of 0.789. Meanwhile, its correlations with the agent are all above 0.90, with an MAE of 0.170 and a weighted κ \kappa of 0.912. These results indicate that the regressor closely reproduces the agent scores while retaining strong agreement with pathologist assessment, supporting its use for large-scale patch screening. These results indicate that training with higher-ranked samples improves distributional fidelity and pathological plausibility. The downstream results show a similar trend. Training the segmentation model from scratch achieves an mIoU of 47.19%, while transferring the generative prior trained without agent-based curation increases it to 53.41%. Using the prior trained on samples ranked within the top 10% in each quality dimension further improves mIoU to 55.46%, corresponding to gains of 8.27 and 2.05 percentage points over the two settings, respectively. Although the top 5% setting achieves the best FID and KID, its mIoU decreases to 55.03%, suggesting that overly strict filtering may reduce the diversity needed for transferable representation learning. We therefore adopt the top 10% setting in subsequent experiments. III-E Comparison on Downstream Segmentation III-E 1 Quantitative Comparisons We compare our framework with two groups of methods. The first group consists of task-specific segmentation models, including CNN-based U-Net [ 42 ] , HoVer-Net [ 44 ] , and CPP-Net [ 45 ] ; Transformer-based TransUNet [ 43 ] and CellViT [ 46 ] ; and SAM-based Un-SAM [ 47 ] . The second group evaluates pretrained encoders coupled with the same segmentation decoder as our framework, including SegDINO [ 33 , 34 ] and MAE [ 48 ] pretrained on natural images, as well as CONCH [ 38 ] and UNI [ 39 ] pretrained on pathology images. As shown in Table I , our framework achieves the best overlap-based segmentation performance on PanNuke, with an mIoU of 55.46%, an mDice of 68.81%, and a p-Acc of 92.77%. It surpasses the corresponding second-best results by 0.86, 1.48, and 0.70 percentage points in mIoU, mDice, and p-Acc, respectively, while reducing mASD from 5.37 to 4.81. These results demonstrate that the transferred generative representations improve segmentation accuracy across both datasets, particularly in terms of region overlap. PanNuke contains 5 nucleus categories: Inflammatory (32,276), Connective (50,585), Neoplastic (77,403), Non-neoplastic epithelial (26,572), and Dead (2,908). PathSeg-380K is our in-house pathology segmentation dataset, collected with ethical approval, covering 12 categories: Mast (3,253), DC (9,087), pDC (15,550), B (44,993), T (42,944), Endothelial (49,498), Plasma (82,850), Mono/Mac (59,319), Fibroblast (69,868), MSC (114), Cycling (1,436), and NA (2,720). Together, they cover diverse semantic categories and morphological patterns for evaluating segmentation performance. IV Conclusion In this work, we presented an agent-driven framework that integrates pathology data curation, generative prior learning, and downstream semantic segmentation. The pathology expert agent enables scalable quality screening and the construction of the curated TCGA-200K dataset. Using these high-quality samples, the DiT backbone learns pathology-specific representations that capture both cellular morphology and tissue-level semantics, which are effectively transferred to a lightweight segmentation head. Strong results on PanNuke and PathSeg-380K demonstrate the value of generative pretraining for both pathology image synthesis and downstream analysis, while future work will extend the framework to other clinically relevant tasks, including classification and survival analysis. We will also investigate more efficient agent-based curation strategies to reduce screening costs while preserving data quality across institutions and staining protocols.
