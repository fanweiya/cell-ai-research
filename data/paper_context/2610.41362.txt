2 Method Figure 2 provides an overview of TPR, illustrating the hierarchical routing mechanism and the feature alignment process across encoder stages. 2.1 Overview and Problem Formulation Given an input CT volume 𝐱 ∈ ℝ H × W × D \mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{H\times W\times D} and its ground truth segmentation mask 𝐲 ∈ { 0 , 1 , … , C } H × W × D \mathbf{y}\in\{0,1,...,C\}^{H\times W\times D} with C C organ classes, we train a student 𝒮 θ \mathcal{S}_{\theta} using M M frozen teacher models { 𝒯 m } m = 1 M \{\mathcal{T}_{m}\}_{m=1}^{M} . Each teacher is independently pre-trained under a different paradigm. Our goal is to enable the student to surpass the performance of individual teachers by effectively combining their complementary strengths across different spatial regions. Both teachers and student share an identical encoder-decoder architecture: SwinUNETR [ 5 ] for VoCo and SuPreM, and U-Net [ 18 ] for SuPreM and CLIP-Driven. They extract multi-scale features at L L stages. For the student, we denote the encoder features at stage l l as 𝐟 s l ∈ ℝ C l × H l × W l × D l \mathbf{f}_{s}^{l}\in\mathbb{R}^{C_{l}\times H_{l}\times W_{l}\times D_{l}} and the final output logits as 𝐋 s ∈ ℝ ( C + 1 ) × H × W × D \mathbf{L}_{s}\in\mathbb{R}^{(C+1)\times H\times W\times D} . Similarly, teacher m m produces features 𝐟 t m l \mathbf{f}_{t_{m}}^{l} and logits 𝐋 t m \mathbf{L}_{t_{m}} . All teacher models are completely frozen during training, requiring no gradient computation. We propose TPR, which computes adaptive routing weights 𝐰 m l , r \mathbf{w}_{m}^{l,r} for teacher m m at stage l l and region r r based on actual segmentation performance, in contrast to traditional MTKD methods that rely on spatially uniform or globally adaptive weights. 2.2 Task-Performance Router The TPR dynamically determines teacher weights based on actual prediction errors, comprising three components: hierarchical region division, routing weight generation, and hard/easy region adaptation. Hierarchical Region Division Medical image segmentation involves features at different abstraction levels—shallow layers capture fine-grained boundary details while deep layers encode global semantic context. To match routing granularity with feature hierarchy, we apply fixed 3D grid division at each encoder stage l l . Given a feature map 𝐟 l ∈ ℝ C l × H l × W l × D l \mathbf{f}^{l}\in\mathbb{R}^{C_{l}\times H_{l}\times W_{l}\times D_{l}} at stage l l , we divide it into N l N_{l} non-overlapping 3D grid regions. Here R l R_{l} denotes the number of grid divisions along the three spatial axes, rather than the voxel dimensions of an individual region. The grid resolution decreases from shallow to deep layers: R l = { 8 × 8 × 8 , l = 0 ​ (shallow) 4 × 4 × 4 , l = 1 2 × 2 × 2 , l = 2 1 × 1 × 1 , l = 3 ​ (deep) R_{l}=\begin{cases}8\times 8\times 8,&l=0\text{ (shallow)}\\ 4\times 4\times 4,&l=1\\ 2\times 2\times 2,&l=2\\ 1\times 1\times 1,&l=3\text{ (deep)}\end{cases} (1) Thus, 8 × 8 × 8 8\times 8\times 8 specifies 512 regions, whereas 1 × 1 × 1 1\times 1\times 1 specifies one global region. This hierarchical design reflects the intuition that shallow layers require fine-grained spatial routing to handle local variations in tissue contrast and boundary clarity, while deep layers benefit from coarse or global routing to maintain semantic coherence across organs. Routing Weight Generation We first compute a voxel-wise cross-entropy error map from the final logits of the student and each frozen teacher, then pool this map using the stage-specific 3D grid to obtain region-level errors e s r e_{s}^{r} and e t m r e_{t_{m}}^{r} at each encoder stage. Regions with larger student error are treated as hard regions ( e s r > τ hard e_{s}^{r}>\tau_{\text{hard}} , where τ hard \tau_{\text{hard}} is a threshold hyperparameter), motivated by the observation that hard examples contribute disproportionately to learning [ 12 ] . This hard/easy region distinction also draws on curriculum learning principles [ 2 ] , where harder examples receive more focused supervision. In hard regions, we favor teachers with lower errors using a softmax over each teacher’s relative advantage; in easy regions, we apply uniform weights to encourage the student to absorb complementary knowledge from all teachers rather than overfitting to a single one: w m l , r = { exp ⁡ ( e s r − e t m r τ ) ∑ m ′ = 1 M exp ⁡ ( e s r − e t m ′ r τ ) , e s r > τ hard 1 M , otherwise w_{m}^{l,r}=\begin{cases}\frac{\exp\left(\frac{e_{s}^{r}-e_{t_{m}}^{r}}{\tau}\right)}{\sum_{m^{\prime}=1}^{M}\exp\left(\frac{e_{s}^{r}-e_{t_{m^{\prime}}}^{r}}{\tau}\right)},&e_{s}^{r}>\tau_{\text{hard}}\\ \frac{1}{M},&\text{otherwise}\end{cases} (2) For a fixed region, e s r e_{s}^{r} is shared by all teachers and therefore cancels in the softmax normalization. The hard-region weights are equivalently softmax m ( − e t m r / τ ) \operatorname{softmax}_{m}(-e_{t_{m}}^{r}/\tau) ; the student error determines which routing branch is used. Routing uses ground truth during training. At inference, only the student is evaluated. 2.3 Multi-Component Loss Function The total training objective combines four terms: ℒ total = λ seg ​ ℒ seg + λ align ​ ℒ align + λ logits ​ ℒ logits + λ balance ​ ℒ balance \mathcal{L}_{\text{total}}=\lambda_{\text{seg}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{seg}}+\lambda_{\text{align}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{align}}+\lambda_{\text{logits}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{logits}}+\lambda_{\text{balance}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{balance}} (3) Segmentation loss provides direct supervision from ground truth using a combination of Dice and cross-entropy losses: ℒ seg = ℒ Dice ​ ( 𝐋 s , 𝐲 ) + ℒ CE ​ ( 𝐋 s , 𝐲 ) \mathcal{L}_{\text{seg}}=\mathcal{L}_{\text{Dice}}(\mathbf{L}_{s},\mathbf{y})+\mathcal{L}_{\text{CE}}(\mathbf{L}_{s},\mathbf{y}) . Feature alignment loss aligns student encoder features with teacher features using region-adaptive routing weights across all L = 4 L=4 encoder stages, from fine-grained shallow features to coarse semantic deep features. Here 𝐟 ¯ l , r \bar{\mathbf{f}}^{l,r} denotes the spatially averaged feature vector within region r r at stage l l : ℒ align = ∑ l = 0 L − 1 1 N l ​ ∑ r = 1 N l ∑ m = 1 M w m l , r ​ ‖ 𝐟 ¯ s l , r − 𝐟 ¯ t m l , r ‖ 2 2 \mathcal{L}_{\text{align}}=\sum_{l=0}^{L-1}\frac{1}{N_{l}}\sum_{r=1}^{N_{l}}\sum_{m=1}^{M}w_{m}^{l,r}\left\|\bar{\mathbf{f}}_{s}^{l,r}-\bar{\mathbf{f}}_{t_{m}}^{l,r}\right\|_{2}^{2} (4) Logits distillation loss transfers knowledge from teachers’ final predictions via KL divergence [ 17 ] on region-averaged logits, where 𝐋 ¯ r \bar{\mathbf{L}}^{r} denotes the spatially averaged logit vector within region r r : ℒ logits = 1 N out ∑ r = 1 N out ∑ m = 1 M w m L , r KL ( p s ( 𝐋 ¯ s r ) ∥ p t m ( 𝐋 ¯ t m r ) ) \mathcal{L}_{\text{logits}}=\frac{1}{N_{\text{out}}}\sum_{r=1}^{N_{\text{out}}}\sum_{m=1}^{M}w_{m}^{L,r}\text{KL}\left(p_{s}(\bar{\mathbf{L}}_{s}^{r})\|p_{t_{m}}(\bar{\mathbf{L}}_{t_{m}}^{r})\right) (5) Load balance regularization prevents routing collapse, inspired by load balancing in mixture-of-experts models [ 19 ] . We set λ seg = 1.0 \lambda_{\text{seg}}=1.0 , λ align = 0.2 \lambda_{\text{align}}=0.2 , λ logits = 0.2 \lambda_{\text{logits}}=0.2 , λ balance = 0.1 \lambda_{\text{balance}}=0.1 . 3 Experiments 3.1 Datasets and Evaluation Metrics We evaluate TPR on BTCV [ 10 ] and two MSD tasks [ 20 ] (Liver and Pancreas). For BTCV, we follow the commonly-used split (24 training / 6 testing) [ 5 ] . For MSD, we use the official training set provided by the challenge and create a held-out test split (80% for training and 20% for testing, random seed 42) within it for all comparisons. All volumes are resampled to 1.5 × 1.5 × 2.0 1.5\times 1.5\times 2.0 mm and intensities are clipped to [ − 175,250 ] [-175,250] HU. We report mean Dice (%, higher is better) and mean HD95 (mm, lower is better) over all foreground classes. 3.2 Teacher Models and Experimental Settings We use two teacher pairs: (VoCo [ 22 ] , SuPreM [ 11 ] ) with SwinUNETR and (SuPreM [ 11 ] , CLIP-Driven [ 13 ] ) with U-Net; the student shares the same architecture as its teachers to enable feature alignment. All models use a patch size of 96 × 96 × 96 96\times 96\times 96 . Since teacher models only release encoder weights, we train teacher decoders from scratch for 1200 epochs with frozen encoders. Students are trained for 1200 epochs using AdamW [ 15 ] (lr= 3 × 10 − 4 3\times 10^{-4} , weight decay= 10 − 5 10^{-5} , 20 warmup epochs), with τ hard = 0.5 \tau_{\text{hard}}=0.5 and τ = 0.7 \tau=0.7 . Data augmentation includes random flipping and rotation (p=0.2). All experiments use 4 NVIDIA RTX 4090 GPUs. The adaptive ensemble baseline applies softmax-normalized weights based on each teacher’s validation Dice score. 3.3 Comparison Study As shown in Table 1 , TPR consistently outperforms both individual teachers, adaptive ensemble, and all MTKD baselines across three datasets and both teacher pair settings. Notably, TPR surpasses even the adaptive ensemble method, demonstrating that our distillation approach achieves superior knowledge fusion rather than simply aggregating teacher outputs. The Student Advantage maps highlight local corrections beyond the predictions of either individual teacher. 4 Conclusion We propose Task-Performance Routing (TPR) for multi-teacher distillation in CT segmentation, assigning region-wise teacher weights based on task error at hierarchical spatial granularity. TPR effectively exploits complementary strengths of different foundation models and yields consistent improvements over individual teachers. One limitation is that TPR requires ground truth labels for error-based routing, making it less suitable for semi-supervised or label-scarce settings; pseudo-label-based routing remains future work.
