Task-agnostic foundation-model robustification. Recent work by Filiot et al. [ 6 ] reports a model-agnostic fine-tuning procedure applied to ten pathology foundation models. The study evaluates the resulting encoders with PathoROB, HEST, THUNDER, and Patho-Bench and reports improvements in both robustness and aggregate downstream performance. It released two adapted checkpoints: Phaet, derived from Phikon-v2, and Mascaret, derived from Midnight-12k. However, as of September 2026 the preprint does not include a method section or specify the optimization objective, robustness-training data, construction of training pairs, trainable parameters, or optimization hyperparameters. At the time of writing, no training implementation has been released. Consequently, the reported procedure cannot be independently reproduced, audited, or applied to the other eight evaluated backbones. SPECTRA addresses this reproducibility and accessibility gap by providing a fully specified, label-free InfoNCE objective, parameter-efficient LoRA adaptation, and an open training pipeline based on the public PLISM matched-acquisition dataset [ 22 ] . 3 Methods 3.1 Overview SPECTRA is a model-agnostic, parameter-efficient fine-tuning framework which utilizes registered views of the same tissue acquired under different scanner and staining conditions to improve the robustness of a base pathology foundation model. For each model, we freeze the original backbone and train LoRA adapters on the PLISM dataset (§ 3.2 – 3.5 ). Only PLISM supplies gradient updates. HEST, CPTAC, and THUNDER are used exclusively for downstream evaluation; PathoROB is used for stopping and checkpoint selection (§ 3.6 ). 3.2 Training data We use the publicly available PLISM dataset [ 22 ] , although SPECTRA is not specific to this dataset and requires only spatially matched tissue images acquired under different conditions. PLISM was designed to isolate acquisition-related variation using serial sections of a tissue microarray containing 46 human tissue types, stained under 13 distinct H&E protocols and imaged using seven whole-slide scanners and six smartphone cameras [ 22 ] . It comprises two spatially registered subsets: PLISM-wsi, containing 91 whole-slide images representing every combination of the 13 stains and seven scanners, and PLISM-sm, containing corresponding fields of view acquired using both scanners and smartphones. We use only PLISM-wsi in this study. We define each scanner–stain pair as an acquisition condition c = ( scanner , stain ) c=(\text{scanner},\text{stain}) . Rather than extracting tiles directly from the original whole-slide images, we use Owkin’s re-registered tile release derived from PLISM-wsi [ 4 ] . In this release, each of the 91 whole-slide images is represented by the same ordered set of N = 16,278 N=16{,}278 spatial locations, extracted as 224 × 224 224\times 224 -pixel tiles. We denote the tile from location i i under condition c c by x c , i x_{c,i} ; consequently, x c , i x_{c,i} and x c ′ , i x_{c^{\prime},i} depict corresponding tissue regions under different acquisition conditions. Across scanners at a fixed stain, corresponding tiles originate from the same physical tissue section. Across stains, they originate from adjacent serial sections, which preserve tissue identity and local architecture but do not contain exactly the same cells [ 22 ] . To evaluate generalization beyond the acquisition conditions observed during training, we hold out two scanners (GT450, S210) and three stains (HRH, KR, MY). The remaining five scanners and ten stains define 50 training conditions, while the other 41 conditions contain at least one held-out scanner or stain; the split is fixed across seeds, and tissue locations are shared between training and held-out acquisition conditions (Appendix C ). 3.3 Base models We apply SPECTRA to a diverse set of pathology foundation models to assess the consistency of its improvements and to compare robustness and downstream performance before and after fine-tuning. Two properties of the procedure are worth noting. First, every selected checkpoint lands at steps 100 100 – 200 200 (§ 3.6 ). The robustness gain therefore comes from an early update to the adapters, made while the learning rate is still ramping to its peak; running the full 500-step schedule is not what produces it. Second, because PathoROB is the selection criterion, its improvement is not an independent finding, whereas HEST, CPTAC, and THUNDER measure what the RI-selected checkpoints do to downstream utility. The regressions on TCGA-derived tasks (§ 5 ) could reflect reduced use of acquisition shortcuts, but could also indicate loss of biologically useful information. The current experiments do not distinguish these explanations. Whether that trade is acceptable depends on the deployment setting, and it is one reason we report the per-task tables rather than only the aggregate ranks. 8 Limitations SPECTRA is trained using a single tissue-microarray dataset acquired at one institution. Although PLISM provides unusually well-controlled scanner and staining variation, its specific contribution to SPECTRA ’s performance remains undetermined because we do not compare training on alternative matched-acquisition datasets. Consequently, we cannot establish whether PLISM is optimal or whether larger, more biologically diverse, or multi-institutional datasets would produce greater robustness and downstream transfer. The observed improvements may also depend on PLISM-specific tissue composition, staining protocols, and scanner coverage. Registration across stains introduces an additional limitation: stain-matched pairs are drawn from adjacent serial sections (§ 3.2 ), which may exhibit genuine local biological differences. Treating these regions as positive pairs therefore introduces correspondence noise and may encourage the model to suppress biologically meaningful variation. More precise cross-section registration, explicit uncertainty modeling, or objectives that distinguish exact from approximate correspondences could reduce this risk. Three further caveats apply. All of the evidence measures representation consistency across centres and probe performance, not diagnostic accuracy. All ablations are single seed (§ 6 ), and several of the reported differences are smaller than the selection tolerance ϵ \epsilon (§ 3.6 ), so the pseudo-tissue reweighting, batch-composition, and adapter-rank trends should be read as indicative rather than established; LoRA versus full fine-tuning, one versus two views, temperature, and the number of training conditions are untested. PathoROB is used for checkpoint selection, so its reported gains are optimistically biased relative to the other benchmarks. Finally, the improvements are not uniform: k k -NN class purity on TCGA, TCGA-uniform classification, and calibration on several backbones regress, and users should check the per-task tables for the tasks they care about before substituting a fine-tuned checkpoint. 9 Conclusion A useful pathology foundation model should capture tissue biology reliably across sites, staining protocols, and scanners. This study shows that existing encoders can move towards that goal with a small trainable update using publicly available images, while simultaneously improving performance on most downstream tasks. Matched acquisitions can therefore serve as a practical resource for improving foundation models after pretraining. We release our training and evaluation codebases, along with adapters and model weights for selected backbones so that our procedure can be reproduced, audited, and applied to other backbones.
