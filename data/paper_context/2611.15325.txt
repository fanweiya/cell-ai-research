Pathology foundation models and MLLMs. Vision-language pre-training on pathology image–text pairs produced CLIP-style models such as PLIP [ 26 ] , QuiltNet [ 27 ] , CONCH [ 40 ] and MUSK [ 63 ] , and self-supervised encoders such as UNI [ 15 ] . Building on instruction tuning [ 36 ] , pathology MLLMs adapt general models with pathology dialogue or caption data [ 51 , 41 , 56 ] , lately with reinforcement-learned reasoning [ 78 , 67 , 37 , 29 ] , knowledge retrieval or memory [ 77 , 31 ] and evidence-grounded agents [ 70 , 7 ] ; medical generalists [ 30 , 8 ] and frontier general MLLMs [ 4 , 83 ] are applied to pathology as well, also as autonomous slide-viewing agents [ 2 ] ; almost all are evaluated one image at a time. Multi-image evaluation. BLINK [ 20 ] , MuirBench [ 57 ] and MMIU [ 43 ] show that general MLLMs struggle with relations across images, and MMBench [ 38 ] introduced circular evaluation to expose answer-position bias. Medical multi-image benchmarks find that MLLMs treat a sequence of images as separate instances [ 73 ] and degrade as images are added [ 65 ] , also on rare-disease case reports [ 44 ] ; microscopy benchmarks [ 39 , 6 ] cover research imaging. We bring these concerns to diagnostic pathology, where the images are related (one lesion, several scales) and integrating them is necessary. We score with a hierarchical diagnostic score (HDS): 1 for the reference entity, any of its synonyms or an accepted alternate; 0.5 for another entity of the same family; 0 otherwise. The item score is the higher of an open-weights LLM judge (Qwen2.5-32B-Instruct, AWQ), which resolves free-text equivalence given the reference, its synonyms and family, and a deterministic ontology match that gives family credit only within the same behaviour class. Against blinded 2/1/0 grades of 297 responses, the final A1 grade agrees exactly on 80.5% (quadratic-weighted κ \kappa 0.76; LLM judge alone 0.73) and is stricter on average (mean grade 0.32 vs. 0.49), so A1 scores err on the low side. We also report Top-1 exact and Top-3 (diagnosis or differentials) accuracy. A2: diagnosis as multiple choice. Nine distractors are drawn from the ontology in priority order: curated differential partner, same family, same organ system with the same lineage and behaviour, same system with the same lineage, same system, same lineage. Every classic look-alike of the entity—178 cases have one among their options, from 63 curated textbook differentials and 40 pairs the library itself sets side by side—is always among the distractors unless the exclusions below forbid it, so that each case’s classic confusion is tested. Distractors may never be a synonym, parent or subtype, organ-site variant or a partner that H&E cannot separate (Sec. S8 ), and grading siblings are left to C; no model is used to mine them, so no evaluated model is adversarially disadvantaged. B: behaviour triage. Non-neoplastic, benign, intermediate or malignant, on the cases whose behaviour code is uncontroversial (we exclude entities such as GIST or thymoma whose coding is debated). We also derive the critical miss rate : malignant or in situ cases called benign or non-neoplastic. C: ordinal grading. Options are all levels of the case’s ordinal group, two to four per group: 17 groups such as DCIS nuclear grade, phyllodes tumour grade, Gleason pattern, squamous intraepithelial lesion grade, astrocytoma grade and endometrial dating. Besides circular accuracy we report quadratic weighted κ \kappa (QWK) on the levels. R1: finding verification. To ask whether models see the morphology behind a diagnosis, 195 cases (all 194 whose entity is diagnosable from H&E alone, plus the case of Fig. 1 c) carry a reference route from low-power architecture to high-power cytology, the differential and the conclusion. Each case gives two items, one per level, of four statements to judge, two true and two false: true ones are essential criteria of the labelled entity, false ones defining features of its look-alikes or distractors that the label excludes, so every truth value follows from the expert label, not from reading the image. † \dagger Routes and statements were generated by an LLM (DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash, release 1017) from the label and WHO criteria and subsequently reviewed by a pathologist; they never name the entity or the site, and wording heuristics score at chance (two shortest statements true: 50%; negated statements true: 51%). We report statement accuracy and the share of items with all four right, by level, and a text-only baseline. Atlas tasks. CaseScope-Atlas has A1 and A2 as above, B on the cases with a certain behaviour code (the ICD-O code of a WHO entity, a confident ontology code, or the agreement of both LLMs for a new entity), and two tasks taken from the captions. In R1-Atlas a true statement restates a finding the caption states and a false one contradicts it minimally; a statement is kept only if its source span occurs in the caption and a second LLM reaches the same truth value from the caption alone (LLM-written, verified by a pathologist). G1 asks which field shows a finding that the caption ties to one panel or figure, with the entity’s name removed (the options are the case’s fields, with CircularEval over them). Circular evaluation and statistics. Each multiple-choice item is asked once per rotation of its options (ten for A2, four for B, two to four for C, one per field for G1); an item counts as correct only if every rotation is answered correctly [ 38 ] . We also report vanilla (mean over rotations) accuracy. 95% confidence intervals and paired comparisons use bootstrap resamples of cases (2,000; 10,000 for the field-set ablations). Fig. 4 re-runs A2 for the open models with the same cases and options but less evidence (all values: Table S8 ). Answering from the highest-power field alone changes circular accuracy by −7.1 to +2.1 points, significantly lowering it for six of twenty-eight models and raising it for one. Removing the one-line specimen site, in contrast, lowers it for twenty-five of twenty-eight models, by up to 9.4 points—more than half of what most of them reach with it. The images are not ignored altogether: on R1 (Fig. S7 ) they raise statement accuracy over the text alone for eighteen of twenty-eight open models and lower it for one, but by a median +3.4 points (max +16.3); much of what they get right is in the text. The proprietary models, not re-run with fewer fields, gain more from the images on R1: +10.2 to +19.5 points, significantly for all four, over 63.7–83.0% from the text alone. 5.4 Where do models fail? Look-alikes attract the errors. For 178 cases a classic look-alike of the entity is among the nine distractors; valid wrong answers on these items pick a look-alike 26.6% of the time (18.5–37.7% across models), against 14.7% if they spread evenly over the distractors (Fig. S8 c; means across the 28 open models); the four proprietary models pick a look-alike 33.2–45.1% of the time. Single-pass accuracy falls from 34% for items without a distractor from the reference’s own family to 25% for items with five or more. Difficulty follows the annotation. Twenty-seven of twenty-eight open models answer cases diagnosable from H&E alone more accurately than cases where H&E is only suggestive (33.0% vs. 25.8%), and twenty-seven of twenty-eight answer cases with six or more fields less accurately than two-field cases (26.0% vs. 32.9%; Table S10 ); harder teaching cases get more fields, so the fields show no benefit rather than harm. The hardest organ systems are breast and lymph node & spleen, the easiest GI tract and endocrine, and A1 errors are mostly of the wrong behaviour class or another family of the same lineage, not near misses (Fig. S9 , Table S11 ). We conducted a reader study comparing model performance with that of residents and attendings; details are provided in Sec. S9 . 5.5 Does the picture hold on published atlases? The right half of Tab. 3 repeats the evaluation on CaseScope-Atlas, and Fig. S6 sets each model’s two scores side by side; ranges cover all models in Table S7 . The ranking broadly replicates, the level does not. Rankings agree between the sources ( ρ = 0.83 \rho=0.83 , 95% CI 0.59–0.96, for A2 over 32 models; 0.80 for A1), though less so within the open models alone (0.74 on A2), and proprietary models stay ahead on A2 (24.1–49.1% vs. 0.0–14.2%). We expected atlas figures, chosen to be typical, to be easier. Instead, on the 347 entities in both sources proprietary models change by −13.0 points on A2 and −11.5 on A1 (Atlas minus Teaching), and open models by +0.9 and −1.1. Fewer fields do not explain this alone: at equal numbers of fields the proprietary models change by −15.4 points on A2 with two fields and by −4.3 with five or more. The sources also differ in image reproduction and in the distractor pool, which this comparison cannot separate. New entities are harder to name, not to choose. Against the shared entities, the 453 Atlas-only entities score −11.4 points on A1 for proprietary and −6.7 for open models, significantly lower for 28 of 32 models, but A2 barely differs (+4.0 and −1.3; significantly lower for 3 of 32). Instability and missed malignancies recur. Requiring all ten rotations keeps 26% of the single-pass accuracy of open models (Teaching: 24%) and 61% of that of proprietary models (68%). Critical misses are more frequent than on CaseScope-Teaching (means of 32.2% vs. 18.6% for proprietary and 30.2% vs. 21.2% for open models). Non-neoplastic cases are not harder than neoplastic ones, contrary to our expectation: the difference on A2 is +10.2 points for proprietary and +5.3 for open models. Fields weigh as much as the site here. For the 28 open models with both ablations on both sources, on cases with three or more fields (425 on Atlas, 379 on Teaching), the highest-power field alone changes A2 by −2.2 points on Atlas against −1.0 on Teaching (significantly lower for 15 vs. 6 models), and removing the site by −2.9 against −4.7 (19 vs. 25). The finding of Sec. 5.3 therefore holds on the teaching cases but does not carry over in full: on atlas cases the further fields count about as much as the site. On G1 the options rotate while the images keep their order, so naming one image throughout is right on 31.5% of the items (independent guesses: 8.5%); the 95% interval lies above this for 4 of 4 proprietary (39.9–61.5%) but only 6 of 28 open models (0.7–42.7%; Table S7 ). R1-Atlas stays largely answerable from the text (up to 74.7%); the images add a mean +5.0 points for proprietary and +3.8 for open models, significantly raising accuracy for 3 of 4 and 14 of 28 and lowering it for one open model. Teaching cases and atlas figures show fields chosen to illustrate an entity, easier than those a pathologist must find on an unselected slide; CaseScope thus measures textbook-level reasoning across fields, necessary but not sufficient for diagnostic use. Most entities have a single case, so we report results by organ system, behaviour and task rather than per entity. What the results imply. Accuracy alone overstates what a model reads: rotation consistency and field and site ablations belong beside it. Limitations of the protocol. Fields come in a fixed, stated order, so field selection is untested; only H&E and site are given; R1 truth follows from labels (Teaching) or captions (Atlas), not annotated regions. Contamination. No teaching or atlas image has a near-duplicate among 228,899 public pathology images (Sec. S11 ), though a whole-image search misses crops. What the results imply. Accuracy alone overstates what a model reads: rotation consistency and field and site ablations belong beside it. Limitations of the protocol. Fields come in a fixed, stated order, so field selection is untested; only H&E and site are given; R1 truth follows from labels (Teaching) or captions (Atlas), not annotated regions. 7 Conclusion CaseScope turns a residency teaching library and published atlases into a benchmark of diagnosis from several fields of one lesion or, in atlas figure series, of one disease. Across twenty-eight open and four proprietary MLLMs, diagnoses change with the order of the options, extra fields matter far less than a one-line clinical hint on teaching cases and about as much on atlas cases with three or more fields, and findings are verified barely above chance by most open models and far better by proprietary ones, whose diagnoses are nonetheless unstable: current MLLMs do not yet read cases reliably. Rotated options and reduced field sets show what the answers rest on. R1: authoring specification and checks An LLM (DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash, release 1017) first generated the R1 routes and statements for each case according to the specification below. A pathologist then reviewed the generated content. All released items also passed the automated checks described below. Input per case: the reference entity (expert label) and its WHO criteria; the specimen site; the entities offered as distractors in the case’s A2 item, including its classic look-alikes; for graded entities, the levels of its group. Cases: every case whose entity is diagnosable from H&E alone, plus the case of Fig. 1 (195 cases). Write a route: low-power architecture -> high-power cytology -> differential (two look-alikes, each with the finding that separates it) -> conclusion. For each level (low power, high power) write four statements, exactly two true and two false: - true: an essential criterion of the reference entity that is seen at this level; - false: a defining finding of one of the case’s look-alikes or distractors (or of another level of the same grading group) that the reference excludes at this level; record the supporting diagnosis or grading level and its relation to the reference; - one finding per statement, phrased as what is seen ("At low power, ..."), in English and Chinese; - never name the reference entity, its synonyms, any option of the case’s A2 item, or the site; - no true/false pair that mirrors one another; true and false statements alike in length and in the use of negation. Automated checks: four statements per level with exactly two true; every false statement has a basis that names an ontology entity or grading level; differential entities exist in the ontology; no statement contains the name or a synonym (four characters or longer) of the reference or of any A2 option of the case; statement order is shuffled per item with a fixed seed. Answer-free heuristics then score at chance on the 1,560 statements (Sec. 4: two shortest statements true, 50%; negated statements true, 51%).
