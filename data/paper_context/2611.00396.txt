III Methodology III.1 Datasets We used three retinal fundus datasets, DRIVE [ 25 ] , HRF [ 26 ] and FIVES [ 1 ] , and one microscopy dataset, VessMap [ 27 ] . They were chosen to differ in two properties related to the expected weaknesses of a plain ViT (Section I ): the number of training images, from 16 to 600, and the image size, from 256 to 3504 pixels per side, which sets how many pixels a vessel spans relative to a patch. DRIVE is the standard benchmark of retinal vessel segmentation [ 2 ] , which allows our results to be related to the literature. It has 40 images of 565 × 584 565\times 584 pixels with an official split of 20 training and 20 test images. We held out four of the training images (images 37 to 40) for validation and trained on the other 16. DRIVE is therefore the dataset with the fewest training images, the setting in which a plain ViT trained from scratch is expected to suffer most. HRF has 45 images of 3504 × 2336 3504\times 2336 pixels, 15 each of healthy eyes and of eyes with diabetic retinopathy or glaucoma [ 26 ] . FIVES has 800 images of 2048 × 2048 2048\times 2048 pixels, 200 each of normal eyes and of eyes with age-related macular degeneration, diabetic retinopathy or glaucoma, with an official split of 600 training and 200 test images. With 600 training images, FIVES tests whether the gain from foundation pre-training becomes smaller when many training images are available. VessMap has 100 confocal fluorescence microscopy images ( 256 × 256 256\times 256 pixels) of the vasculature of the mouse brain cortex, with low contrast. They were selected from a larger set of images to be as heterogeneous as possible in contrast, noise, vessel density and the variation of intensity along the vessels [ 27 ] . VessMap tests whether our findings hold in a second imaging domain. HRF and VessMap have no official split, so we split each at random, with a fixed seed, into 70% training and 30% test images. On HRF, FIVES and VessMap, no separate validation set was held out, and checkpoint selection, early stopping and the hyperparameter search used the test images. This protocol was the same for every model. III.2 Models Table 1 summarizes the architectures used. MedSAM [ 11 ] is the plain ViT, a single-scale ViT without convolutional stages. It uses the ViT-B encoder of SAM, with 16 × 16 16\times 16 px patches and a 1024 × 1024 1024\times 1024 px input, and was trained on over one million medical image–mask pairs. Its training data include fundus images, but only from optic disc and cup datasets, and none of our four datasets is among them [ 11 ] . LiteMedSAM [ 34 ] is the hybrid model. Its TinyViT encoder [ 33 ] combines convolutional stages (mobile inverted bottleneck convolutions) with windowed attention and has a 256 × 256 256\times 256 px input. It was distilled and fine-tuned on the same data as MedSAM [ 34 ] . Both transformer-based models keep SAM’s prompt encoder and mask decoder [ 10 ] . In training and inference, we set the prompt as a fixed box covering the whole input window, and the prompt encoder was not trained. The decoder upsamples the 64 × 64 64\times 64 grid of encoder tokens by a factor of four. MedSAM therefore predicts at a quarter of its input resolution, and its logits are upsampled bilinearly to the size of the window. LiteMedSAM , whose encoder reduces its 256 px window to the same grid, predicts at its input resolution. The three CNNs span more than three orders of magnitude in size: ConvNeXt , a ConvNeXt-Large encoder [ 23 ] with a transposed-convolution decoder; ConvUNeXt , a U-Net built from ConvNeXt-style blocks with attention gates [ 24 ] ; and LW-Net , our implementation of Little W-Net [ 8 ] . The two transformer-based models were trained in two ways: fine-tuned from their published pre-trained weights [ 11 , 34 ] , with all weights updated except those of the prompt encoder, and trained from scratch. Comparing pre-trained transformer-based models with CNNs trained from scratch was deliberate, because these are the configurations in which each kind of model is usually applied. Compact segmentation CNNs are trained from scratch [ 3 , 8 , 24 , 42 , 43 ] , while transformer-based foundation models are fine-tuned from their pre-trained weights. ConvNeXt was included as a CNN in the same cost class as MedSAM . Although ImageNet weights exist for its encoder, pre-trained weights are rarely available for compact CNNs, so ConvNeXt followed the same protocol as the other two CNNs. Training the transformer-based models also from scratch separates the effect of pre-training from that of the architecture. For each configuration and dataset, a search of 100 trials chose the learning rate, the weight decay, the batch size, the base width of ConvUNeXt and LW-Net and the stochastic-depth rate of ConvNeXt . Table A.1 contains the full search space. We ran the searches with Optuna [ 44 ] (version 4.7.0), using its tree-structured Parzen estimator sampler [ 45 ] and median pruning on the validation Dice. In total, the 36 searches ran 3,602 trials in 2,093 GPU hours. The CNNs were trained on an NVIDIA RTX 3090 GPU (24 GB) and the transformer-based models on NVIDIA RTX PRO 5000 (48 GB) and RTX PRO 4000 (24 GB) GPUs. All models were trained with the soft Dice loss [ 46 ] and AdamW [ 47 ] , in PyTorch [ 48 ] (version 2.10.0) with PyTorch Lightning [ 49 ] (version 2.6.1). We kept the trial with the highest validation Dice. Training stopped after 50 epochs without improvement of the validation Dice. Each training sample was a random crop of the size of the model’s input (256 px, or 1024 px for MedSAM ), taken after random flips and rotations. Images smaller than the input, which is the case of the DRIVE and VessMap images for MedSAM , were upscaled to it, so MedSAM saw DRIVE at about 1.75 times and VessMap at 4 times their native scale. Appendix A gives the full training details. As an additional investigation, we tested whether smaller transformer-based models could reach the performance of the original ones. We trained, from scratch, a compact version of each transformer-based model, with a 256 px input and an architecture optimized on each dataset. For these models, the search of 100 trials covered the architecture in addition to the training hyperparameters. For compact MedSAM , the patch size, the embedding dimension, the depth, the number of attention heads, the MLP ratio and the attention window were optimized. For compact LiteMedSAM , the embedding dimensions, the depths of its stages, the number of heads, the attention windows and the MLP ratio were optimized. Appendix A describes the search space, and Table A.2 contains the selected architectures. III.4 Evaluation protocol and metrics We evaluated every model on the full test images at native resolution with the sliding-window inference of MONAI [ 50 ] (version 1.5.2). The windows had the size of the model’s input with an overlap of 25% and they were combined with Gaussian weighting. Images smaller than a window were padded to it. The sigmoid output was binarized at 0.5. Because MedSAM was trained on upscaled DRIVE and VessMap images, on these two datasets we upscaled each test image to its 1024 × 1024 1024\times 1024 px input, resized the logits back to the native resolution with bilinear interpolation, and scored them against the native ground truth. We call this the upscaled protocol, and the alternative, in which the native image is padded to the window, the padded protocol. The upscaled protocol evaluates MedSAM at its training scale, and we used it for every MedSAM result except the comparison of the two protocols. All metrics were computed for each test image from the binarized prediction Y ^ \hat{Y} and the ground truth Y Y . We used the Dice score over the full image as an overall measure of performance. The centerline metrics use the skeleton S ⁡ ( ⋅ ) S(\cdot) of a mask, computed with the skeletonize function of scikit-image [ 51 ] (version 0.26.0), which implements Zhang–Suen thinning [ 52 ] . Our main metrics are based on the centerline recall, R cl = | S ⁡ ( Y ) ∩ Y ^ | | S ⁡ ( Y ) | , R_{\mathrm{cl}}=\frac{|S(Y)\cap\hat{Y}|}{|S(Y)|}, (1) the fraction of ground-truth centerline pixels that fall inside the predicted mask. To check that high values of R cl R_{\mathrm{cl}} were not obtained at the cost of more false positives, we also computed the centerline precision, P cl = | S ⁡ ( Y ^ ) ∩ Y | | S ⁡ ( Y ^ ) | , P_{\mathrm{cl}}=\frac{|S(\hat{Y})\cap Y|}{|S(\hat{Y})|}, (2) the fraction of predicted centerline pixels that fall inside the ground-truth mask. R cl R_{\mathrm{cl}} and P cl P_{\mathrm{cl}} were used in [ 40 ] to define the clDice metric. To measure recall as a function of vessel caliber, we grouped the ground-truth centerline pixels by radius into the bins [ 0.5 , 1.5 ) [0.5,1.5) , [ 1.5 , 2.5 ) [1.5,2.5) , [ 2.5 , 3.5 ) [2.5,3.5) , [ 3.5 , 4.5 ) [3.5,4.5) and [ 4.5 , ∞ ) [4.5,\infty) px. The radius of a centerline pixel was taken as the value of the Euclidean distance transform of the mask at that pixel. We call a vessel thin when its radius is below 1.5 px. The centerline recall of thin vessels is the recall of the first bin, R thin = | S thin ​ ( Y ) ∩ Y ^ | | S thin ​ ( Y ) | , R_{\mathrm{thin}}=\frac{|S_{\mathrm{thin}}(Y)\cap\hat{Y}|}{|S_{\mathrm{thin}}(Y)|}, (3) where S thin ​ ( Y ) S_{\mathrm{thin}}(Y) is the set of ground-truth centerline pixels with radius below 1.5 px. The centerline precision of thin vessels, P thin = | S thin ​ ( Y ^ ) ∩ Y | | S thin ​ ( Y ^ ) | , P_{\mathrm{thin}}=\frac{|S_{\mathrm{thin}}(\hat{Y})\cap Y|}{|S_{\mathrm{thin}}(\hat{Y})|}, (4) considers only the predicted centerline pixels whose radius, measured in the prediction, is below 1.5 px. The two thin-vessel metrics are therefore computed on different sets of pixels: R thin R_{\mathrm{thin}} on the ground-truth thin centerline and P thin P_{\mathrm{thin}} on the predicted one. When a denominator is empty, the metric is undefined for that image and is left out of the mean. Note that P thin P_{\mathrm{thin}} is used in our analysis only as a sanity check to quantify if models are oversegmenting the vessels to improve their R thin R_{\mathrm{thin}} . This is because P thin P_{\mathrm{thin}} cannot be considered a cooutberbalance to R thin R_{\mathrm{thin}} the same way that precision is to recall. R thin R_{\mathrm{thin}} measures the capacity of a model to detect thin vessels present in the ground-truth, while P thin P_{\mathrm{thin}} measures false positives that happen to be thin structures. For instance, a model that predicts vessel for all pixels will have a R thin R_{\mathrm{thin}} of 1, but undefined P thin P_{\mathrm{thin}} , because it will have no predicted thin centerline pixels. We counted the parameters and the floating-point operations of each trained checkpoint with fvcore [ 53 ] (version 0.1.5), counting one multiply–add as one FLOP. As a measure of training difficulty, we report the number of epochs the selected run needed to first reach 95% of its best validation Dice. Significance tests used a two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test [ 54 ] on per-image paired differences, with the Holm correction [ 55 ] . A difference is called significant when its Holm-adjusted p-value is below 0.05. The result tables report the mean ± \pm standard deviation of each metric over the test images. Bold marks the best value of a comparison, underlining marks the values not significantly different from the best one, and an asterisk marks a significant difference in a pairwise comparison. IV.2 Segmentation performance on thin vessels Fig. 1 shows the mean centerline recall as a function of vessel radius. The compact models are not included because the figure focus on raw performance and also to reduce visual clutter. For every model and dataset, the recall was lowest in the first bin, radius below 1.5 px. On HRF and FIVES, the recall fell steadily as the radius decreased, while on DRIVE and VessMap it was nearly constant above 1.5 px and dropped only in the first bin. The recall of the first bin is the centerline recall of thin vessels, R thin R_{\mathrm{thin}} , compared below. Fine-tuned MedSAM had an R thin R_{\mathrm{thin}} within the range of the three CNNs, that is, between their lowest and highest means, on DRIVE and FIVES, and above this range on HRF and VessMap. Its R thin R_{\mathrm{thin}} was not significantly different from that of the best CNN, the CNN with the highest mean for the same dataset and metric, on any dataset. The hybrid LiteMedSAM had the highest mean R thin R_{\mathrm{thin}} on every dataset, significantly above every CNN on DRIVE and HRF. Table C.1 of Appendix C gives the p-values of these comparisons. A model can raise its R thin R_{\mathrm{thin}} by over-segmenting, that is, by trading precision for recall. To check whether this was the case for any of the models, Fig. 2 plots the centerline precision of thin vessels, P thin P_{\mathrm{thin}} , against R thin R_{\mathrm{thin}} . On DRIVE, MedSAM had an R thin R_{\mathrm{thin}} not significantly different from those of LW-Net and ConvUNeXt , but a significantly lower P thin P_{\mathrm{thin}} . On HRF, MedSAM and ConvUNeXt did not differ significantly in either metric. On FIVES, no model was significantly better than MedSAM in either metric, and each of the other four models was significantly worse in at least one. On VessMap, MedSAM and LW-Net did not differ significantly in either metric. Taken together, the results indicate that the high values of R thin R_{\mathrm{thin}} of MedSAM did not come at the cost of a low P thin P_{\mathrm{thin}} , when compared to CNNs with similar performance. The model had equivalent performance to a CNN on HRF and VessMap and a better overall performance on FIVES. For LiteMedSAM , the higher R thin R_{\mathrm{thin}} came with a lower P thin P_{\mathrm{thin}} than that of the best CNN on every dataset, with a significant difference on DRIVE, HRF and FIVES. IV.4 Pre-training and convergence speed Fig. 6 compares MedSAM and LiteMedSAM fine-tuned from their pre-trained weights with the same architectures trained from scratch, next to the three CNNs. Table B.1 in Appendix B provides the values used in the figure for MedSAM and LiteMedSAM . Pre-training raised the Dice of MedSAM significantly on every dataset. Its effect on the R thin R_{\mathrm{thin}} of MedSAM depended on the dataset. R thin R_{\mathrm{thin}} rose by 17.6 and 12.4 points on DRIVE and VessMap, by only 1.9 points on FIVES, and did not change significantly on HRF. Without pre-training, the R thin R_{\mathrm{thin}} of MedSAM was significantly lower than that of the best CNN on DRIVE, FIVES and VessMap. Pre-training also raised the P thin P_{\mathrm{thin}} of MedSAM on HRF and VessMap, and slightly reduced it on DRIVE. For the hybrid LiteMedSAM , pre-training raised R thin R_{\mathrm{thin}} significantly on every dataset. For MedSAM , the Dice gain from pre-training was smallest on FIVES, the dataset with the most training images. Interestingly, the gain in R thin R_{\mathrm{thin}} for MedSAM was lower on the high-resolution datasets. Fig. 7 shows the number of epochs until each selected run first reached 95% of its best validation Dice. Pre-trained MedSAM converged the fastest of all models on HRF and FIVES, where it reached this level at the first validation, and about as fast as LW-Net and ConvUNeXt on DRIVE and VessMap. Without pre-training, MedSAM needed more epochs on every dataset, and so did compact MedSAM . Pre-trained LiteMedSAM converged faster than every CNN, except on FIVES, where it was slower than ConvUNeXt . IV.5 Inference protocol of MedSAM: padding versus upscaling Upscaling the DRIVE and VessMap images to the 1024 px window of MedSAM adds no information, but it changes the scale at which the model sees the thin vessels. To measure the effect of this choice, we compared the upscaled and padded protocols of Section III.4 on these two datasets. The results are shown in Table 3 . The padded protocol gave a significantly lower Dice and R thin R_{\mathrm{thin}} than the upscaled protocol, both for fine-tuned MedSAM and for MedSAM trained from scratch. For fine-tuned MedSAM , R thin R_{\mathrm{thin}} fell by 17.9 points on DRIVE and 41.3 points on VessMap, below the range of the CNNs on both datasets. For MedSAM in these conditions, the results do not support the expectation that a plain ViT misses thin vessels. Importantly, this holds for the default 16 × 16 16\times 16 px patch of MedSAM , which is much wider than thin vessels. The drawback is cost. The cost per image of MedSAM was 2 to 18 times that of ConvUNeXt and more than 30 times that of LW-Net . A compact plain ViT trained from scratch did not keep the performance of the original model. Against the best CNN, its R thin R_{\mathrm{thin}} was significantly lower on DRIVE and FIVES, and its P thin P_{\mathrm{thin}} and Dice were significantly lower on all four datasets. The hybrid LiteMedSAM had the highest mean R thin R_{\mathrm{thin}} on every dataset, but a lower P thin P_{\mathrm{thin}} than the best CNNs. Where cost matters, compact CNNs remain good candidates on small datasets. LW-Net , with 0.07 M parameters, had a Dice and an R thin R_{\mathrm{thin}} not significantly different from those of fine-tuned MedSAM on DRIVE and VessMap. Pre-training was essential for MedSAM . Without it, MedSAM fell significantly below the best CNN in R thin R_{\mathrm{thin}} on three datasets. The Dice gain from pre-training was smallest on FIVES, the dataset with the most training images. Our study has limitations that suggest directions for future work. First, the CNNs were trained from scratch, while the transformer-based models were fine-tuned. As mentioned in Section III.3 , ImageNet weights exist for the ConvNeXt-Large encoder [ 23 ] , but smaller models, which are commonly used in practice, lack pre-trained encoders. Second, although the hyperparameters of each configuration were selected from 100 trials, the reported results come from a single training run, that of the best trial. Furthermore, the optimization protocol varied slightly among datasets and models, for instance in the validation interval and in the range of batch sizes (Appendix A ). Some design choices, such as the optimizer and the learning-rate schedule, were also kept fixed. Thin vessels are challenging to annotate, so metrics calculated on them can have high variability. The quality of biomedical annotations depends on factors such as the labeling instructions and the expertise of the annotators [ 56 ] . On DRIVE, for instance, the R thin R_{\mathrm{thin}} between the two annotators is 58.6 % ± 11.9 % 58.6\%\pm 11.9\% , close to the value obtained for MedSAM (56.9%). However, the influence of the annotator is challenging to quantify, since most retinal datasets do not contain multiple annotations. This constitutes an interesting direction for future work.
