Foundation-model encoders and MIL aggregation Both imaging modalities are increasingly represented by frozen foundation models. For pathology, self-supervised encoders such as UNI ( Chen et al., 2024 ) , CTransPath ( Wang et al., 2022 ) , and CONCH ( Lu et al., 2024 ) yield strong patch-level features that are pooled by multiple-instance learning (MIL) ( Maron and Lozano-Pérez, 1998 ; Ilse et al., 2018 ) , with transformer aggregators such as TransMIL ( Shao et al., 2021 ) and CLAM ( Lu et al., 2021 ) in wide use. For CT, self-supervised 3D encoders such as FMCIB ( Pai et al., 2024 ) provide tumor-centric representations. CRUX builds directly on these encoders, keeping them frozen and focusing on how heterogeneous features should be fused . Language models for clinical variables Biomedical language models such as BioBERT ( Lee et al., 2020 ) and BioLord ( Rémy et al., 2022 ) , together with vision–language pretraining ( Radford et al., 2021 ) , capture rich medical semantics. In most survival pipelines, however, clinical variables are still consumed as scalar tabular inputs ( Cox, 1972 ; Friedman, 2001 ) . We instead verbalize structured variables and encode them with a biomedical language model, exploiting the institution-invariance of standardized clinical vocabularies as an anchor for imaging fusion. 3 Methods This section describes the CRUX framework for multimodal survival prediction from CT, WSI, and clinical text. An overview of the architecture is shown in Figure 1 . The framework consists of three components: text-anchored encoding (Section 3.2 ), where clinical text tokens and learnable query tokens serve as semantic axes through which imaging features are organized via iterative bidirectional cross-attention; a text-mediated cross-modal bridge (Section 3.3 ), where the text summaries from both modalities exchange cross-modal information and the resulting enriched summaries are broadcast back to the imaging tokens; and multimodal aggregation followed by survival prediction (Section 3.4 ). CT Preoperative CT volumes are encoded using FMCIB ( Pai et al., 2024 ) , a foundation model pretrained on a large corpus of medical imaging data via self-supervised learning. A tumor-centered sub-volume of 50 × 50 × 50 50\times 50\times 50 voxels (resampled to 1.0 ​ mm 1.0\,\text{mm} isotropic spacing) is cropped from each scan and passed through the 3D ResNet backbone, which produces a feature tensor of shape 4096 × 6 × 6 × 6 4096\times 6\times 6\times 6 corresponding to the spatial grid after successive downsampling. This tensor is reshaped into N C = 216 N_{C}=216 spatial tokens of dimension 4096 4096 . A two-layer MLP projects these tokens to the shared embedding space, yielding 𝐂 ∈ ℝ N C × D \mathbf{C}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{C}\times D} . Pathology Gigapixel WSIs are partitioned into non-overlapping patches at diagnostic magnification, and each patch is encoded using UNI ( Chen et al., 2024 ) , a vision transformer pretrained on over 100 million histopathology images. This produces N P N_{P} patch-level features of dimension 1536 1536 , where N P N_{P} varies per slide (typically 500 500 – 4000 4000 ). A linear layer with dropout projects these features to 𝐏 ∈ ℝ N P × D \mathbf{P}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{P}\times D} . Clinical text The meaning of “text” in this framework warrants clarification at the outset, as the term can be misleading in a clinical context. The text modality is not a free-form clinical note, radiology report, or pathology report; it is a templated natural-language representation of structured, routinely available clinical descriptors (a “language-encoded clinical descriptor”). Specifically, a small set of standardized categorical and numerical variables is verbalized into a fixed-template sentence and encoded by a biomedical language model. No free-text electronic medical record content is used. A key design decision is the exclusion of AJCC TNM staging variables and histological subtype from the text input. TNM staging (overall stage, T, N, M) is derived from expert interpretation of CT images, while histological subtype is determined by pathological examination of WSIs—both represent the output of imaging analysis, not independent sources of information. Including these variables would provide the model with expert-derived conclusions that render the imaging encoders redundant. Instead, the text modality is restricted to pre-diagnostic patient information: demographics (age, sex, smoking history) and anatomical tumor location, which is known prior to imaging interpretation as a prerequisite for tissue acquisition. These variables are composed into a natural language paragraph using a template: “A 65 -year-old male patient was diagnosed with non-small cell lung cancer with no smoking history. The primary tumor was identified in the right superior lobe of the lung.” where the bold segments indicate patient-specific values populated from the clinical record. The paragraph is encoded using BioLord ( Rémy et al., 2022 ) , a biomedical sentence embedding model trained on clinical ontologies, producing N T N_{T} token-level embeddings of dimension 768 768 . A two-layer MLP projects these to 𝐓 ∈ ℝ N T × D \mathbf{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{T}\times D} . 3.2 Text-Anchored Encoding The text-anchored encoding module can be understood as a low-rank, text-guided self-attention over the imaging tokens. In standard self-attention, each imaging token attends to all other tokens, forming an N X × N X N_{X}\times N_{X} attention matrix. In contrast, this module routes all interaction through a small set of text tokens that serve as semantic axes: each text token first summarizes the imaging features relevant to its clinical concept, and then each imaging token retrieves information from these summaries. The result is equivalent to self-attention constrained to a rank determined by the number of text tokens, where the axes correspond to clinically meaningful concepts such as patient demographics and tumor location. Learnable query tokens The N T N_{T} patient-specific text tokens provide domain-invariant anchoring axes, but they are limited to the clinical concepts present in the input paragraph. To expand the representational capacity, N Q N_{Q} learnable query tokens 𝐐 ∈ ℝ N Q × D \mathbf{Q}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{Q}\times D} are introduced as additional axes that are free to discover survival-relevant imaging patterns directly from the training signal. Direct cross-attention between CT and pathology tokens is computationally expensive ( N C × N P N_{C}\times N_{P} attention matrix) and semantically challenging given the representational heterogeneity. CRUX addresses this by mediating all cross-modal interaction through the text semantic space. Text hub The text-query summaries 𝐓𝐐 C \mathbf{TQ}_{C} and 𝐓𝐐 P \mathbf{TQ}_{P} from the text-anchored encoding carry visual evidence from their respective imaging modalities, but each summary only knows about its own modality. This is the core contribution of CRUX over the prior text-anchored framework ( Kim et al., 2024 ) : whereas the prior work processes each imaging modality independently through text, CRUX introduces an explicit cross-modal exchange mechanism that operates entirely within the compact text semantic space ( 2 ​ ( N T + N Q ) ≪ N C + N P 2(N_{T}+N_{Q})\ll N_{C}+N_{P} ). Notably, 𝐩 T \mathbf{p}_{T} is not a prediction from clinical text alone; it is the class-token output of a self-attention encoder that has jointly processed text-query summaries from both CT and pathology. Its role in the final prediction is to provide a stable, domain-invariant summary that complements the potentially noisy but informationally rich imaging representations. Training objective The model is trained with the discrete-time negative log-likelihood survival loss ℒ surv \mathcal{L}_{\text{surv}} ( Zadeh and Schmid, 2021 ) . In addition to the main prediction from the concatenated representation (Equation 6 ), auxiliary survival losses are computed independently from each of the three pooled representations 𝐩 C \mathbf{p}_{C} , 𝐩 P \mathbf{p}_{P} , and 𝐩 T \mathbf{p}_{T} through separate linear heads, weighted by α aux = 0.1 \alpha_{\text{aux}}=0.1 . These auxiliary objectives encourage each representation stream to maintain independent prognostic capacity, preventing the model from relying exclusively on a single modality. 4 Experiments 4.1 Datasets Data source and inclusion criteria This study uses lung cancer prognosis prediction data from the Open AI Dataset Project (AI-Hub, South Korea), a publicly accessible multi-institutional dataset collected across university hospitals. From the original collection, patients meeting the following criteria were included: (1) histologically confirmed adenocarcinoma or squamous cell carcinoma, which together constitute the majority of non-small cell lung cancer cases; (2) surgical resection performed as primary treatment; (3) availability of paired pretreatment CT, histopathological WSI, and structured clinical information; and (4) valid clinical variable entries across all staging, demographic, and histological fields. Patients whose death was attributed to non-cancer causes were excluded to ensure that the survival endpoint reflects cancer-specific prognosis. Additionally, a small number of cases with data quality issues—such as CT–pathology identifier mismatches or corrupted imaging data—were removed after manual inspection. No restriction was imposed on minimum follow-up duration; patients who were alive at last contact or lost to follow-up were treated as right-censored observations in accordance with standard survival analysis conventions. After applying these criteria across five hospitals, the final cohort comprised 2,157 patients with complete multimodal data. Table 1 summarizes the cohort composition. CT features are extracted using FMCIB ( Pai et al., 2024 ) from tumor-centered 50 × 50 × 50 50\times 50\times 50 sub-volumes, yielding 216 216 tokens of dimension 4096 4096 . Pathology features are extracted using UNI ( Chen et al., 2024 ) from non-overlapping 224 × 224 224\times 224 patches at 10 × 10\times magnification, with a maximum of 2048 2048 patches per slide to limit memory consumption. Clinical text features are extracted per token using BioLord ( Rémy et al., 2022 ) from the paragraph template described in Section 3.1 , producing N T N_{T} tokens of dimension 768 768 where N T N_{T} depends on the tokenizer output for each patient’s paragraph (typically 30 30 – 40 40 tokens). Training All models share a common embedding dimension of D = 512 D=512 and are trained with the AdamW optimizer ( β 1 = 0.9 \beta_{1}=0.9 , β 2 = 0.999 \beta_{2}=0.999 , weight decay 5 × 10 − 4 5\times 10^{-4} ) using a cosine annealing learning rate schedule with 5 5 epochs of linear warmup, a peak learning rate of 5 × 10 − 5 5\times 10^{-5} , and a minimum learning rate of 1 × 10 − 6 1\times 10^{-6} . Training proceeds for a maximum of 30 30 epochs with early stopping triggered after 10 10 consecutive validations without improvement. Gradient clipping (max norm 1.0 1.0 ) is applied. Due to the variable sequence lengths across patients, all models are trained with batch size 1 1 on a single NVIDIA A6000 GPU per fold. For methods employing text-anchored encoding, the bidirectional cross-attention uses L = 2 L=2 layers with 8 8 attention heads and an MLP hidden dimension of 2048 2048 . CRUX additionally uses N Q = 4 N_{Q}=4 learnable query tokens and an auxiliary loss weight of α aux = 0.1 \alpha_{\text{aux}}=0.1 applied to the per-modality and cross-modal consensus predictions. All self-attention encoders used for token aggregation follow the Nyström attention architecture ( Xiong et al., 2021 ) with positional encoding adapted to the spatial structure of each modality (3D grid for CT, 2D grid for pathology). Cross-validation The development cohort ( N = 1,711 N=1{,}711 ) is divided into 5 5 folds using censorship-stratified splitting: within each hospital, patients are stratified by their censorship status (event vs. censored) to ensure that the low event rate ( 6.5 % 6.5\% ) is approximately balanced across folds. For each fold, four folds serve as training data and one fold as the validation set. Model selection is based on the best validation concordance index (C-index). The held-out external validation cohort (PNUH, N = 446 N=446 ) is evaluated using each fold’s best checkpoint independently, and the results are reported as the mean and standard deviation across folds. 4.3 Baseline Methods To evaluate the contributions of multimodal fusion and text-anchored interaction, this study compares a comprehensive set of baselines spanning unimodal, bimodal, and trimodal configurations. All baseline methods share the same pretrained feature extractors, projection layers, and training protocol described in Section 4.2 , differing only in the fusion architecture. Unimodal baselines Each modality is evaluated independently to establish per-modality performance bounds. For CT and pathology, extracted features are aggregated via self-attention with class-token pooling and passed through a linear prediction head. For clinical text, two settings are considered: (1) the BioLord token embeddings aggregated via self-attention, and (2) traditional machine learning methods—Cox proportional hazards (CoxPH) ( Cox, 1972 ) and gradient boosted machines (GBM) ( Friedman, 2001 ) —applied to scalar-valued clinical features. The comparison between deep learning and traditional approaches on the same clinical variables isolates the contribution of the pretrained text encoder’s semantic knowledge. Bimodal baselines Bimodal fusion is evaluated for all three modality pairs (CT+pathology, CT+text, pathology+text) using the following architectures. Concatenation serves as the simplest baseline, where features from both modalities are aggregated independently, concatenated, and passed through a joint prediction head. MCAT ( Chen et al., 2021 ) applies genomic-guided cross-attention where one modality attends to the other; in this setting, the smaller modality (text or CT) serves as the query attending to the larger modality. SurvPath ( Jaume et al., 2024 ) employs pairwise co-attention where the smaller modality attends to the concatenation of both modalities, while the larger modality attends to the smaller one. CMTA ( Zhou and Chen, 2023 ) applies bidirectional cross-attention between the two modalities followed by modality-specific aggregation, producing both direct and cross-modal representations that are combined for prediction. Trimodal baselines Methods integrating all three modalities are compared under a unified evaluation: 1. Concatenation : each modality is independently aggregated, the three pooled representations are concatenated, and a joint prediction head produces the final output. This serves as the simplest feature-level fusion baseline. 2. Text-anchored bag ( Kim et al., 2024 ) : the prior text-anchored framework, where bidirectional cross-attention between text and each imaging modality produces text-contextualized image tokens and image-enriched text tokens. All four token groups are concatenated into a single multimodal bag and jointly aggregated by a shared self-attention encoder. 3. SurvPath (trimodal) ( Jaume et al., 2024 ) : extended to three modalities by applying pairwise co-attention between all modality pairs (CT–pathology, pathology–text, text–CT), with each pair producing pooled representations that are concatenated for prediction. 4. CRUX : the proposed method (Section 3 ). 4.4 Evaluation Protocol Model performance is assessed using the concordance index (C-index), which measures the probability that, for a randomly selected pair of patients, the model assigns a higher risk score to the patient who experiences the event first. The C-index is computed separately on the development cohort (mean across 5-fold cross-validation) and on the external validation cohort (PNUH), with standard deviations reported across folds in both cases. For external evaluation, predictions from the five fold-trained models are averaged to produce a single ensemble prediction per patient, yielding a single C-index value without standard deviation. The primary evaluation criterion is external validation performance, as it reflects the model’s ability to generalize to an unseen hospital with different disease severity distributions. The gap between development and external performance is reported as a relative indicator of generalization robustness across methods, noting that the two cohorts differ in disease severity and event rate, so the gap should not be interpreted as an absolute measure of generalization quality. Statistical significance of risk stratification is assessed using the log-rank test on Kaplan-Meier survival curves, where patients are divided into high-risk and low-risk groups based on the median predicted risk score. The comparison between deep learning and traditional approaches on the same clinical variables isolates the contribution of the pretrained text encoder’s semantic knowledge. Bimodal baselines Bimodal fusion is evaluated for all three modality pairs (CT+pathology, CT+text, pathology+text) using the following architectures. Concatenation serves as the simplest baseline, where features from both modalities are aggregated independently, concatenated, and passed through a joint prediction head. MCAT ( Chen et al., 2021 ) applies genomic-guided cross-attention where one modality attends to the other; in this setting, the smaller modality (text or CT) serves as the query attending to the larger modality. SurvPath ( Jaume et al., 2024 ) employs pairwise co-attention where the smaller modality attends to the concatenation of both modalities, while the larger modality attends to the smaller one. CMTA ( Zhou and Chen, 2023 ) applies bidirectional cross-attention between the two modalities followed by modality-specific aggregation, producing both direct and cross-modal representations that are combined for prediction. Trimodal baselines Methods integrating all three modalities are compared under a unified evaluation: 1. Concatenation : each modality is independently aggregated, the three pooled representations are concatenated, and a joint prediction head produces the final output. This serves as the simplest feature-level fusion baseline. 2. Text-anchored bag ( Kim et al., 2024 ) : the prior text-anchored framework, where bidirectional cross-attention between text and each imaging modality produces text-contextualized image tokens and image-enriched text tokens. All four token groups are concatenated into a single multimodal bag and jointly aggregated by a shared self-attention encoder. 3. SurvPath (trimodal) ( Jaume et al., 2024 ) : extended to three modalities by applying pairwise co-attention between all modality pairs (CT–pathology, pathology–text, text–CT), with each pair producing pooled representations that are concatenated for prediction. 4. CRUX : the proposed method (Section 3 ). 4.4 Evaluation Protocol Model performance is assessed using the concordance index (C-index), which measures the probability that, for a randomly selected pair of patients, the model assigns a higher risk score to the patient who experiences the event first. The C-index is computed separately on the development cohort (mean across 5-fold cross-validation) and on the external validation cohort (PNUH), with standard deviations reported across folds in both cases. For external evaluation, predictions from the five fold-trained models are averaged to produce a single ensemble prediction per patient, yielding a single C-index value without standard deviation. The primary evaluation criterion is external validation performance, as it reflects the model’s ability to generalize to an unseen hospital with different disease severity distributions. The gap between development and external performance is reported as a relative indicator of generalization robustness across methods, noting that the two cohorts differ in disease severity and event rate, so the gap should not be interpreted as an absolute measure of generalization quality. Statistical significance of risk stratification is assessed using the log-rank test on Kaplan-Meier survival curves, where patients are divided into high-risk and low-risk groups based on the median predicted risk score. This evaluation is performed on the external validation cohort to assess whether the model’s risk predictions translate to meaningful survival differences in an independent population. 5 Results 5.1 Main Results Table 2 presents the comparison across unimodal, bimodal, and trimodal configurations. The unimodal and bimodal results provide strong evidence for this premise. Language-model encoding, not added variables or model capacity, drives the gain The text-only model using BioLord embeddings achieves an external C-index of 0.732 from only four non-staging variables (age, sex, smoking history, location). As shown in Table 3 , trained models applied to the same four variables as raw scalars achieve substantially lower performance (external 0.684/0.689/0.638 for CoxPH/GBM/MLP); the same four variables thus yield at most 0.689 as raw scalars but 0.732 when language-encoded. Three observations show that this gain stems from the representation rather than from added information, more variables, or a more flexible model. First, it is not added variables : smoking and location are individually weak (external 0.613 for smoking, and tumor location is a nominal lobe label with essentially no transferable ordering, ≈ 0.557 \approx 0.557 ), and adding them to age and sex barely changes the scalar CoxPH (0.680 to 0.684, with an internal decrease), whereas under language encoding the same additions lift BioLord-based TransMIL from 0.658/0.662 (age+sex) to 0.714/0.732 (all four)—a gain of + 0.070 +0.070 externally. Second, it is not model capacity : a tabular MLP on the same four scalars reaches only 0.638, well below the language-encoded model, so the improvement is not attributable to deep learning alone. Third, the encoding injects pretrained medical knowledge : language-model encoding captures population-level knowledge that scalar features cannot access, closing a substantial portion of the gap between non-staging and full-staging predictions ( + 0.048 +0.048 over CoxPH with the same four variables). The remaining gap to staging-inclusive CoxPH ( − 0.036 -0.036 ; 0.732 vs. 0.768) reflects the genuine prognostic information in staging variables, which the model must instead extract from imaging—validating the decision to exclude staging from the text input. Age and sex are informative but insufficient for ranking A clinical observation raised in review is that age and sex alone already convey substantial prognostic information. This is borne out, and the directions recovered from the data agree with established clinical knowledge: ranking patients purely by age (no model fitting) yields an external C-index of 0.657 (older patients at higher risk), and by sex 0.600 (male patients at higher risk; the external event rate is 16.2 % 16.2\% in men versus 7.1 % 7.1\% in women). These single variables are therefore strong, clinically valid hints, but they are insufficient for precise risk ranking: age and sex jointly reach only 0.680, and all four non-staging variables 0.684–0.689—far below staging-inclusive CoxPH (0.768) and multimodal CRUX (0.761). Two clarifications follow. First, survival discrimination is measured by the C-index, a censoring-aware ranking metric rather than classification accuracy; a C-index near 0.68 is clearly above chance (0.5) but far from a deployable ceiling, so an intuition phrased as high “accuracy” does not transfer to the ranking task. Second, a single variable’s natural ordering (age, 0.657) exceeds a flexible model trained on all four raw scalars only when that model is under-regularized; with proper regularization the trained models recover the expected ordering (GBM 0.689, above age alone), confirming that the limitation is the weak, distribution-shift-prone signal in raw tabular variables rather than any individual feature being harmful—the limitation that language encoding overcomes. Direct image fusion degrades under domain shift All CT+Pathology fusion methods—Concat, MCAT, SurvPath, and CMTA—exhibit external C-indices of 0.552–0.608, consistently below the text-only baseline (0.732). These methods perform direct cross-attention between CT and pathology tokens, allowing institution-specific artifacts in one modality to propagate to the other. The absence of a domain-invariant reference frame means that the cross-attention patterns learned on the development cohort do not transfer reliably to the external cohort. Text-mediated fusion preserves generalization When text is included as a fusion partner, the pattern changes qualitatively. CT+Text models achieve 0.746–0.757 externally, and Pathology+Text models achieve 0.655–0.758 with reduced gaps compared to the imaging-only counterparts. This consistent improvement across all fusion architectures (Concat, MCAT, SurvPath, CMTA) confirms that the benefit derives from the text modality itself—specifically its institution-invariant embedding space—rather than from any particular fusion mechanism. This finding motivates the CRUX design: rather than allowing CT and pathology to interact directly, all cross-modal communication is routed through the text semantic space. 5.4 Trimodal Comparison CRUX achieves the best external performance CRUX achieves an external C-index of 0.761 \mathbf{0.761} , the highest among all trimodal configurations and all methods evaluated in this study. This result surpasses the simple concatenation baseline (0.749), the text-anchored bag ( Kim et al., 2024 ) (0.711), and SurvPath-Tri (0.698). Notably, CRUX’s external performance also exceeds the best bimodal results—C+T CMTA (0.757) and P+T CMTA (0.758)—demonstrating that the text hub mechanism successfully extracts complementary information from both imaging modalities without suffering the generalization degradation observed in other trimodal methods. The text hub resolves the trimodal overfitting problem A consistent pattern across the baseline trimodal methods is that interaction-based approaches achieve high internal C-indices ( > 0.80 >0.80 ) but degrade substantially on external validation, performing below both the text-only baseline and the best bimodal configurations. This pattern indicates that cross-modal interactions learned on the development cohort propagate domain-specific artifacts across modalities, amplifying overfitting. CRUX breaks this pattern: by routing all cross-modal exchange through the compact text semantic space and broadcasting the result back to imaging tokens via residual connections, the text hub preserves the institution-invariant anchoring while enabling effective information exchange.
