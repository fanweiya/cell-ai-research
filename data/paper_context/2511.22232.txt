2 Results 2.1 PMC-MI as a training resource for medical multi-image learning PMC-MI was used as the training resource to develop M 3 LLM. Its construction and quality-assessment procedures are described in Methods (Section 4.1 ). PMC-MI comprised 234,956 compound-figure-derived instances spanning four instruction families: multi-image visual question answering (VQA), compound-figure VQA, text-only question answering (QA), and multiple-choice VQA. Multi-image VQA constituted the largest component, with 115,807 instances (49.3%), comprising 40,261 single-subimage VQA, 35,275 multi-subimage VQA, and 40,271 relative-position VQA instances. Compound-figure VQA, text-only QA, and multiple-choice VQA comprised 40,365 (17.2%), 38,516 (16.4%), and 40,268 (17.1%) instances, respectively. The data-processing and PMC-MI composition results are shown in Figure 2 a,b. Figure 2: Construction and quality assessment of the PMC-MI training resource. a , Source and task-specific filtering of compound figures. b , Composition of the four instruction families; multi-image VQA comprises single-subimage, multi-subimage, and relative-position instances. c , Manual assessment of PMC-MI data quality across the five instruction-construction steps (mean ± \pm standard deviation (SD), 1–5 scale). d , Manual assessment of structured reference trajectories used for policy refinement (mean rating with 95% instance-level bootstrap confidence interval (CI), 1–5 scale). PMC-MI supported both supervised fine-tuning and selection-aware reinforcement learning. Of the 234,956 instances, 10,555 multi-subimage VQA instances were allocated to the dedicated policy-refinement partition, comprising 10,355 instances for Stage II policy optimization and 200 for validation; the remaining 224,401 instances formed the supervised instruction corpus. The policy-refinement instances retained the instruction-derived reference subimage identifiers associated with each question and reference answer, providing reference signals for visual-evidence selection during Stage II. Construction details are provided in Methods (Section 4.1.2 ). Next, we assessed the quality of the constructed PMC-MI data through independent medical review. Three medical reviewers participated in the evaluation of 140 sampled outputs spanning the five construction steps, with each output independently rated by two reviewers. Correctness, completeness, and clarity were assessed separately using a prespecified rubric. Across the five steps, mean correctness scores ranged from 4.3 to 4.9, completeness scores from 4.4 to 4.9, and clarity scores from 4.5 to 5.0. Step 3 (subimage-level medical visual description) had the lowest observed correctness and clarity scores, at 4.3 and 4.5, respectively, whereas Step 1 (inline-text summarization) had the lowest completeness score, at 4.4. Step 5 (context refinement to reduce answer leakage) received mean scores of 4.8 for correctness, 4.9 for completeness, and 5.0 for clarity. The manual assessment results are shown in Figure 2 c and Supplementary Table 1. Moreover, we assessed the reliability of the medical review ratings among the 78 construction outputs that received three independent ratings. The dimension-specific intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs) were 0.867 for correctness, 0.751 for completeness, and 0.720 for clarity. Reviewers assigned identical scores in 74.8% of assessments, and 98.3% of ratings differed by no more than one rating category. We additionally assessed the quality of the structured reference trajectories used for policy refinement. Five medical reviewers collectively evaluated a simple random sample of 100 trajectories with complete local image assets using the reviewer-assignment scheme described in Methods. The mean ratings were 3.35 (95% CI, 3.09–3.62) for visual grounding and image triage, 3.96 (95% CI, 3.76–4.15) for biomedical reasoning, and 3.98 (95% CI, 3.77–4.18) for final clinical correctness and safety. Ordinal Krippendorff’s α \alpha was highest for visual grounding and image triage (0.55; 95% CI, 0.31–0.70), followed by final clinical correctness and safety (0.40; 95% CI, 0.15–0.59) and biomedical reasoning (0.33; 95% CI, 0.09–0.53). A secondary low-score-triggered review confirmed material visual-grounding errors in 9 of 100 trajectories, biomedical-reasoning errors in 6 of 100, and final-answer correctness errors in 8 of 100. These non-mutually-exclusive values are screen-confirmed counts within the sampled trajectories. Detailed results and representative low-scoring cases are reported in Supplementary Table 2 and Supplementary Figure 22, respectively. 2.2 PMC-MI-Bench as a manually reviewed evaluation benchmark In addition to PMC-MI as the training resource, we constructed PMC-MI-Bench as a separate evaluation benchmark. The benchmark curation and review procedures are described in Methods (Section 4.2 ). Of the original 300 examples, 298 were retained; one compound-figure VQA instance and one text-only QA instance were excluded. We then compared source PMCIDs across the retained benchmark and all model-development partitions and removed 117 PMC-MI records whose source articles occurred in the benchmark. Each retained benchmark example had a distinct sample identifier linking its source PMCID and compound-figure image, and no source-article overlap remained. The final PMC-MI-Bench comprised 50 single-subimage VQA, 50 multi-subimage VQA, 50 relative-position VQA, 49 compound-figure VQA, 49 text-only QA, and 50 multiple-choice VQA examples. Benchmark records contained a mean of 4.81 constituent subimages, while their compound-figure captions and linked inline text contained means of 102.2 and 191.1 words, respectively. In addition to source-article separation, we assessed potential verbatim memorization using both prefix-conditioned continuation and question-only response analyses (Methods, Section 4.6 ). In the prefix-conditioned analysis, M 3 LLM produced no continuation satisfying either exact@30 or exact@50 when provided with 50, 100, or 150 reference-answer tokens as context (all observed rates, 0%). Verbatim matches were also uncommon in the question-only analysis (Supplementary Table 4). Point estimates were equal-weight means of the four eligible task-specific match rates, with 95% source-article-clustered bootstrap CIs. M 3 LLM had an exact@30 estimate of 1.01% (0.00–2.58%), compared with 0.50% (0.00–1.67%) for its Qwen3-VL-8B backbone. The paired difference was 0.51 percentage points (0.00–1.67). At the stricter exact@50 threshold, the corresponding estimates were 0.54% (0.00–1.79%) and 0.00% (0.00–0.00%), with a paired difference of 0.54 percentage points (0.00–1.79). 2.3 Performance on PMC-MI-Bench Overall performance. We evaluated M 3 LLM on the 298-question PMC-MI-Bench (Figure 3 a) against five general-purpose MLLMs: LLaVA-7B [ 44 ] , LLaVA-NeXT-7B [ 39 ] , Qwen2.5-VL-7B [ 68 ] , InternVL3-8B [ 19 ] , and Qwen3-VL-8B [ 3 ] ; and six medical MLLMs: LLaVA-Med-7B [ 38 ] , HuatuoGPT-Vision-7B [ 14 ] , Lingshu-7B [ 75 ] , HealthGPT-14B [ 43 ] , MedGemma-27B [ 60 ] , and MedGemma 1.5-4B [ 59 ] . M 3 LLM achieved the highest point estimates across all three multi-image tasks, with Semantic Textual Similarity (STS) scores of 81.6 (95% task-stratified bootstrap CI, 78.8–84.2) on single-subimage VQA, 84.6 (81.8–87.1) on multi-subimage VQA, and 85.2 (82.3–87.8) on relative-position VQA. These point estimates were 9.8, 9.0, and 11.3 points above those of the Qwen3-VL-8B backbone, respectively, and 6.0, 5.5, and 7.6 points above the highest-scoring alternative model in the corresponding task. Across the three multi-image tasks, M 3 LLM achieved an overall multi-image VQA (MI-VQA) score of 83.8 (95% task-stratified bootstrap CI, 82.2–85.4), compared with 73.8 for the backbone and 75.1 for the highest-scoring alternative, HuatuoGPT-Vision-7B. For the remaining instruction formats, M 3 LLM yielded the highest point estimate on compound-figure VQA (STS, 80.5; 95% task-stratified bootstrap CI, 77.5–83.4) and multiple-choice VQA (48/50 correct, 96.0%; 95% Wilson CI, 86.5–98.9). On text-only QA, its STS score was 85.2 (95% task-stratified bootstrap CI, 83.7–86.7), 0.1 points above Lingshu-7B. Relative to the Qwen3-VL-8B backbone, the corresponding point-estimate gains were 5.7 points on compound-figure VQA, 0.7 points on text-only QA, and 10.0 percentage points on multiple-choice VQA. Thus, the higher multi-image scores did not coincide with lower point estimates than the backbone on the other PMC-MI-Bench formats. We additionally report BLEU-4, ROUGE-L, and BERTScore F1 as complementary reference-based measures in Supplementary Table 5. Representative comparisons across relative-position, compound-figure, multi-subimage, text-only, and single-subimage tasks are provided in Supplementary Figs. 23–27. Thus, evaluator convergence was strongest for correctness and completeness, whereas clarity ratings showed greater variability across both human and model-based evaluators. Stage-level analysis. We next examined how each stage of the M 3 LLM training procedure affected performance on PMC-MI-Bench. The procedure comprised Stage I, multi-image supervised fine-tuning (MI-SFT); Stage II, selection-aware reinforcement learning using Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO); and Stage III, full-mixture supervised fine-tuning (Full-SFT), as described in Methods (Section 4.3 ). Table 1 a summarizes performance after each stage. Stage I increased the point estimates across all three multi-image tasks relative to the Qwen3-VL-8B backbone, which served as the initialization for M 3 LLM. The score increased from 71.8 to 73.9 for single-subimage VQA, from 75.6 to 78.7 for multi-subimage VQA, and from 73.9 to 78.6 for relative-position VQA. Stage II produced further gains across all three multi-image tasks. The score increased from 73.9 after MI-SFT to 81.1 after GRPO for single-subimage VQA, from 78.7 to 84.4 for multi-subimage VQA, and from 78.6 to 83.7 for relative-position VQA. To compare GRPO with additional supervised optimization on the same prompt set, we included a continued structured-SFT condition initialized from the same Stage I checkpoint. The control model achieved a score of 74.8 for single-subimage VQA, 79.8 for multi-subimage VQA, and 74.5 for relative-position VQA. The GRPO condition exceeded the continued structured-SFT condition by 6.3 points for single-subimage VQA, 4.6 points for multi-subimage VQA, and 9.2 points for relative-position VQA. Stage III retained the gains on the multi-image tasks, with a final score of 81.6 for single-subimage VQA, 84.6 for multi-subimage VQA, and 85.2 for relative-position VQA. It also increased the point estimates from 77.2 to 80.5 for compound-figure VQA and from 82.9 to 85.2 for text-only QA, while multiple-choice VQA accuracy increased from 92.0% to 96.0%. Thus, the final full-mixture supervised stage retained the Stage II multi-image gains while increasing the point estimates on the other instruction formats. For target-finding identification, the model received the radiograph pair together with a queried finding and predicted, for each of five radiographic findings in turn, whether that finding was the annotated target. For progression classification, the model received the same radiograph pair together with the name of the annotated target finding and predicted whether that finding was improving, stable, or worsening. We evaluated models both without target-task adaptation and after task-specific fine-tuning. The comparison included the same general-purpose and medical MLLM baselines used above, as well as specialized chest-radiograph models. For task-specific adaptation, all models were evaluated using the same patient-level training and test split to enable direct comparison. Although MLLMs are rapidly showing promise across a range of medical applications, multi-image data resources and modelling approaches remain comparatively less developed. In this study, we investigated both the data and methodological challenges involved in developing MLLMs for multi-image understanding and reasoning. Specifically, we investigated whether compound figures from biomedical literature could provide a scalable source of multi-image supervision, developed corresponding benchmarks, proposed training approaches to improve the integration of information across multiple medical images while preserving broader model capabilities, and systematically evaluated the resulting model across complementary benchmark and clinical settings. A primary finding of this study is that medical compound figures can be systematically transformed into a scalable source of supervision for medical multi-image learning. Starting from biomedical literature and leveraging community-curated resources [ 33 ] , we developed a data-construction pipeline that preserves relationships among subimages and uses their surrounding biomedical context to construct complementary supervision for both instruction tuning and policy refinement. This process yielded PMC-MI, comprising 234,956 instruction instances across multi-image, compound-figure, text-only, and multiple-choice tasks. Among these, 10,555 multi-subimage instances retaining instruction-derived supporting-subimage information were allocated to policy refinement to support optimization of visual evidence selection and answer generation. In parallel, we constructed PMC-MI-Bench as a separate, manually reviewed evaluation benchmark with source-article-level separation from PMC-MI to mitigate potential training–evaluation leakage. Manual evaluations provided complementary evidence for the quality of the resulting supervision. Across the instruction-construction steps, mean correctness, completeness, and clarity scores ranged from 4.3–4.9, 4.4–4.9, and 4.5–5.0, respectively. Within the policy-refinement data, biomedical reasoning and final clinical correctness and safety received mean ratings of 3.96 and 3.98, whereas visual grounding and image triage received a lower mean rating of 3.35. This finding highlights the identification and selection of relevant visual evidence as a comparatively challenging component of multi-image reasoning and is consistent with prior studies reporting difficulties in identifying and prioritizing relevant medical evidence [ 32 , 34 ] . Together, these results support the feasibility of transforming biomedical compound figures into scalable and structured supervision for medical multi-image learning. A second main finding is that the proposed three-stage training strategy improved medical multi-image understanding while maintaining performance across broader tasks. Existing medical MLLMs have been developed largely through supervised instruction tuning, with more recent studies also exploring reinforcement-learning-based post-training [ 76 , 26 , 38 , 14 , 75 , 43 , 60 , 59 ] . However, these approaches generally do not explicitly address challenges specific to multi-image understanding. The proposed framework first adapts the model to medical multi-image instructions, subsequently uses selection-aware reinforcement learning to optimize visual evidence selection together with answer quality, and finally returns to supervised fine-tuning on mixed multi-image and non-multi-image instructions to maintain broader capabilities. Controlled stage-wise analyses characterized the performance changes observed across the training procedure. Starting from the same Qwen3-VL-8B backbone, multi-image SFT increased the overall PMC-MI-Bench multi-image VQA score from 73.8 to 77.1, followed by a further increase to 83.1 after selection-aware reinforcement learning. To contextualize the GRPO results against additional supervised optimization, we compared this model with a continued structured-SFT condition initialized from the same Stage I checkpoint and trained on the same policy-refinement prompts. The continued-SFT control achieved an overall multi-image VQA score of 76.4, compared with 83.1 after selection-aware reinforcement learning. The final full-mixture SFT stage retained these multi-image gains, yielding an overall score of 83.8, while also improving performance on compound-figure VQA, text-only QA, and multiple-choice VQA. The reward-ablation results were consistent with contributions from the individual training signals; after the same full-mixture consolidation stage, removing the subimage-selection reward reduced the overall multi-image VQA score from 83.8 to 80.0. These findings support a training strategy in which explicit optimization of visual evidence selection complements answer-level supervision, while subsequent full-mixture fine-tuning helps balance multi-image specialization with performance across broader task formats. These comparisons characterize training-stage effects on a fixed PMC-MI corpus; data scale, curation quality, and instruction content are represented jointly in that corpus. A third main finding was that performance differences relative to the unadapted backbone were also observed across public benchmarks and real-world patient data. M 3 LLM achieved the highest overall accuracy among the evaluated models on the provenance-screened OmniMedVQA set (88.6%, compared with 77.0% for Qwen3-VL-8B) and on MMMU-Med (68.0%, compared with 61.3%). Similar patterns were observed in the two clinical evaluations, which differed substantially from the PMC-derived compound-figure setting. In longitudinal chest-radiograph analysis, M 3 LLM achieved progression-classification accuracies of 27.1% without target-task adaptation and 60.8% after adaptation, the highest observed values among the evaluated models under the corresponding settings. In the retrospective dermatology cohort, it achieved the highest observed balanced accuracy for differential-diagnosis validity under both evaluation settings. Task-specific adaptation produced substantial gains in both clinical evaluations. For longitudinal progression assessment, M 3 LLM accuracy increased from 27.1% without adaptation to 60.8% after adaptation. For dermatology differential-diagnosis validity, balanced accuracy increased from 69.9% to 77.1%. These findings support literature-derived multi-image training as a scalable initialization that can be refined using task- or domain-specific clinical data. In the clinical evaluation, we also compared M 3 LLM and other representative MLLMs with task-adapted domain-specific representation models widely used for chest-radiograph analysis [ 64 , 74 , 5 ] . CXR Foundation and BioViL-T achieved progression-classification accuracies of 48.6% and 50.4%, respectively, after task-specific adaptation. M 3 LLM was evaluated directly in a generative setting and reached 60.8% after adaptation. Related comparisons between generative LLMs or MLLMs and task-specific encoder models have highlighted trade-offs in labelled-data requirements, computational demands, and downstream task characteristics [ 25 , 20 ] . Future systematic evaluations across clinical specialties, applications, and computational budgets will be valuable for characterizing these trade-offs and identifying the settings in which each modelling paradigm is most appropriate. Another important observation relates to how open-ended responses from medical MLLMs should be evaluated. Unlike multiple-choice tasks, for which accuracy can be directly calculated, the optimal evaluation of free-text MLLM responses remains an open challenge [ 17 , 6 , 15 ] . In this study, to provide a more comprehensive evaluation, we combined reference-based automatic metrics, LLM-as-judge evaluation, and manual medical review, all of which have been widely adopted in prior studies [ 6 , 63 ] . The results suggest that these approaches provide complementary information. Automatic metrics provided scalable measures of similarity to the reference answers, whereas human and LLM-based ratings assessed response completeness, clarity, and correctness relative to the manually verified references. Agreement between human reviewers and LLM judges was strongest for correctness, with Spearman correlations ranging from 0.77 to 0.83, compared with 0.55–0.72 for completeness and 0.27–0.48 for clarity. To reduce potential bias in the manual evaluation, each response was independently assessed by at least two medical reviewers, and model identities were concealed. Nevertheless, expert manual evaluation remains resource intensive. Further work is needed to develop more scalable and standardized approaches for evaluating medical MLLMs, including expert evaluation that considers both the underlying medical images and the clinical significance of model errors. Limitations Several limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings of this study. First, PMC-MI was constructed from biomedical literature and therefore reflects the characteristics of published medical evidence. Although the scale and heterogeneous composition of PMC provide broad coverage across medical domains, compound figures are curated for scientific communication and cannot fully represent the distribution or complexity of routine clinical imaging workflows. We partially addressed this limitation by evaluating the resulting model beyond PMC-MI-Bench, including a provenance-screened public benchmark, an examination-derived public benchmark, and two clinical datasets with substantially different image sources and task formulations. Nevertheless, future work should further expand multi-image supervision using additional clinical and non-literature data sources and evaluate performance across broader institutions, patient populations, specialties, and imaging settings. Rare conditions, unusual presentations, and other long-tail subgroups may be sparsely represented in published compound figures; aggregate benchmark results therefore do not establish comparable performance in these subgroups. Second, the construction of large-scale multi-image supervision necessarily involves automated processing. To support scalability while maintaining data quality, our construction pipeline was designed to accommodate different language and multimodal models and incorporated independent manual review of samples from both the instruction-construction and policy-refinement data. The results showed generally high ratings across the evaluated dimensions. However, visual grounding and image triage received comparatively lower ratings, consistent with the broader challenge of reliably identifying and prioritizing relevant medical evidence [ 32 , 34 ] . Further improvements in automated quality control and expert verification could strengthen the construction of multi-image supervision and, in turn, support further improvements in model development. The longitudinal chest-radiograph evaluation used the released MS-CXR-T annotations and therefore inherits variation in their source-label quality. These annotations were not independently re-adjudicated in the present study. Third, throughout the study, we implemented source-article separation between PMC-MI and PMC-MI-Bench, record-level linkage through source PMCIDs and sample identifiers, source-dataset provenance screening for OmniMedVQA, and a complementary verbatim-regeneration analysis. The complete pretraining and post-training corpora of many evaluated models are not publicly available, which leaves uncertainty regarding prior model exposure to public evaluation data. This remains a general challenge in the evaluation of contemporary LLMs and MLLMs [ 51 , 15 ] . Future evaluations using newly collected datasets and broader cross-dataset similarity analyses would provide additional evidence of generalizability. We have also made the related data and code available to facilitate independent evaluation with emerging models and datasets and to support further development by the research community. Fourth, the clinical evaluations were retrospective and were designed to assess transfer to real-world medical imaging data rather than prospective clinical utility. Although the longitudinal chest-radiograph and retrospective dermatology evaluations extended the study beyond literature-derived data and included both fixed-pair and variable-cardinality multi-image settings, they covered a limited number of clinical domains and institutions. Moreover, the improvements observed after task-specific adaptation indicate that additional clinical supervision may remain important for particular applications. These results should therefore not be interpreted as establishing readiness for clinical deployment or improvements in patient outcomes. Future studies should extend these retrospective evaluations to pilot prospective validation and more systematic assessment in real-world clinical workflows [ 8 , 69 , 23 ] . PMC-MI was constructed from biomedical compound figures and their associated text. PubMed Central (PMC) provides a large-scale source of medical image–text data, and several multimodal resources have previously been curated from PMC [ 78 , 46 , 33 ] . For PMC-MI, we used the initial release of MedPMC [ 33 ] , which contains approximately 11 million curated medical image–text pairs extracted from 6.1 million articles in the PMC Open Access collection through June 2024. We selected MedPMC as the source resource for two main reasons. First, it preserved the source-article provenance required for article-level overlap and licence screening. Source records were matched by PMCID to official PMC metadata and retained when the source article carried a CC BY, CC BY-NC, CC BY-NC-SA, CC0, or CC BY-SA licence. Each released record includes its source PMCID, compound-figure identifier, article-level licence, attribution and reuse fields, and an evidence status; PMID and citation metadata are supplied where available in the accompanying sample-level licence evidence keyed by PMCID. Second, MedPMC provided compound-figure records, separated subimages, bounding boxes, and aligned subcaptions as fixed upstream inputs. MedPMC generated the subimage boxes using a YOLOv10 detector trained on ImageCLEF and reported a figure-separation mAP of 89.8; it separately reports validation of compound-figure identification and subimage–caption alignment [ 33 ] . This allowed the present study to focus on constructing instruction data for medical multi-image understanding. Data processing. The MedPMC snapshot used to construct PMC-MI contained 2,177,293 compound figures and defined the fixed source pool for this study. Later MedPMC releases expanded the source resource; PMC-MI was constructed exclusively from this snapshot. Compound figures can contain heterogeneous subimages, including medical images, flow charts, diagrams, and other graphical content. To enrich the corpus for predominantly medical visual content, we applied the medical-image classifier released with MedPMC to each subimage. This classifier is a fine-tuned Vision Transformer with a reported F1 score of 96.5%, a metric distinct from the figure-separation mAP reported above [ 33 ] . We retained compound figures in which at least 75% of the constituent subimages were classified as medical images. Eligible records also required sufficient textual support for instruction construction. Compound-figure captions were required to contain more than 50 words, and each available aligned subimage caption was required to contain at least 10 words. We then assessed each record against the input and structural requirements of the individual instruction formats. These requirements included at least two eligible subimages for multi-subimage VQA and the spatial metadata needed to derive relative-position labels. Records were routed only to formats for which the required images, captions, aligned subimage information, and task-specific metadata were available. Generated outputs were parsed according to the task-specific schemas defined in Step 4. We retained outputs containing all required fields, including a question and reference answer, auxiliary context where applicable, and any required answer options or reference subimage identifiers. Records with generation failures, missing required fields, parsing failures, or invalid output schemas were removed before final assembly. Following eligibility and article-licence screening, instruction construction, parsing, quality control, and source-article separation from PMC-MI-Bench, PMC-MI contained 234,956 instruction instances with unique sample identifiers linked to their source compound figures. Of these, 10,555 eligible multi-subimage VQA instances were assigned to the policy-refinement partition, comprising 10,355 training and 200 validation instances. The remaining 224,401 instances formed the supervised instruction corpus used in Stages I and III. The construction and quality assessment of both partitions are described below. 4.1.1 Instruction construction Instruction-following data were constructed from the eligible compound figures and associated text retained after the preceding processing steps. The supervised instruction partition provided the training targets for Stages I and III, whereas the policy-refinement partition was used for Stage II. The instruction-construction pipeline was model-agnostic at the workflow level, such that its processing steps were not tied to a specific language-model or multimodal-model architecture. Complete prompt specifications and representative transformations are provided in Supplementary Methods S1.1, Supplementary Figs. 1–10, and Supplementary Figs. 11–15. PMC-MI is publicly available at https://huggingface.co/datasets/Yale-BIDS-Chen/M3LLM-data-v1.0.0 . Instruction formats. To provide complementary supervision across different input and task formats, four instruction families were constructed: multi-image visual question answering (VQA), compound-figure VQA, text-only question answering (QA), and multiple-choice VQA. Multi-image VQA contained three task formats. In single-subimage VQA , the compound figure together with its individual subimages was provided as input, while the answer was primarily supported by one relevant subimage. This format was designed to require question-conditioned localization of relevant visual evidence within a multi-image input. In multi-subimage VQA , complementary evidence from at least two subimages was required to answer the question. In relative-position VQA , the spatial relationship between two specified subimages in the parent compound figure was queried, including above, below, left, and right relationships. Reference spatial relationships were derived from the subimage bounding boxes provided by MedPMC; bounding-box coordinates were not required to be predicted. Compound-figure VQA presented the compound figure as a single visual input without separately supplying its individual subimages. No visual input was provided for text-only QA. For multiple-choice VQA, either a compound-figure or multi-image input was used, depending on the constructed instruction, and an answer was selected from a predefined set of options. Five-step instruction-construction pipeline. These instruction formats were constructed using a five-step pipeline in which source-associated textual information, medical background knowledge, and subimage-level visual descriptions were progressively assembled before task-specific instructions were constructed and their auxiliary context was refined.
