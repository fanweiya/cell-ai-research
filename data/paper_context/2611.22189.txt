3 Method Our SRED learns patch representations in two stages (Fig. 2 ). First, we capture the high-magnification evidence/events by training an SAE on 20 × 20\times teacher features to decompose them into a sparse dictionary. For each 5 × 5\times patch, we aggregate event detections from the sparse codes of its aligned 20 × 20\times patches into a regional occurrence target. We transfer this high-magnification evidence by fine-tuning a pretrained UNI2-h encoder [ 8 ] with a temporary head to predict each target from the corresponding 5 × 5\times patch. After distillation, we use the encoder to produce embeddings from 5 × 5\times patches for downstream evaluation. 3.1 Decomposing Features into Events We train an SAE to learn a sparse dictionary from 20 × 20\times patch embeddings to decompose them into sparse semantics, which removes unnecessary information and keeps only high-level semantics as events (Fig. 2 a). First, feature embeddings are extracted by the UNI2-h encoder [ 8 ] that maps each patch y y to t = E T ​ ( y ) ∈ ℝ d t=E_{T}(y)\in\mathbb{R}^{d} . Our SRED generally outperforms native 5 × 5\times features and the other distillation baselines, particularly on classification tasks. It improves the average macro-F1 by 1.1 1.1 points relative to native 5 × 5\times and by 0.4 0.4 points relative to native 20 × 20\times . It achieves the best results on CAM16 and Private-CRC and leads the linear-probe comparison on IMP. On PANDA, it closes much of the gap between native 5 × 5\times and 20 × 20\times . Compared with others, SRED also shows lower variation. On BRACS and IMP-Cervix, where native 5 × 5\times already outperforms native 20 × 20\times , our SRED retains this advantage over 20 × 20\times and also outperforms the other distillation baselines. For survival prediction, our SRED improves on native 5 × 5\times across all cohorts, increasing the average C-index by 1.7 1.7 points, with the largest gain on TCGA-LUAD. XMAG’s encoder pretraining includes these TCGA cohorts [ 52 ] , which may contribute to its strong performance, whereas SRED are excluded them from training. Despite this difference in training exposure, SRED still achieves the highest C-indices on TCGA-KIRC and UCEC. The gains on CAM16 and TCGA-UCEC further show that distillation using SAE occurrence targets can improve the 5 × 5\times representation even when native 5 × 5\times already outperforms native 20 × 20\times . Overall, using only 5 × 5\times inputs, our SRED achieves close performance to native 20 × 20\times , with an 11.58 × 11.58\times speedup over native 20 × 20\times processing (see Supplementary Sec. for details). These results suggest that regional occurrence supervision can benefit different MIL models. 6 Conclusion and Limitations We introduced SRED, a novel cross-magnification distillation framework that fine-tunes an encoder to predict regional events from aligned high-magnification patches using low-magnification patches as inputs. Combining sparse coding with logical OR pooling retains both localized and shared detections in the regional targets, while the dictionary provides a way to inspect the tissue patterns underlying the supervision. During distillation, a temporary prediction head maps the student’s 5 × 5\times features to event occurrence probabilities, while a cosine anchor constrains changes to the pretrained feature directions. Neither SAE training nor distillation requires slide labels, which enables the large-scale training of efficient patch encoders. At inference, the adapted encoder extracts 5 × 5\times patch embeddings for downstream prediction. We evaluated SRED on 12 clinical cohorts, demonstrating strong classification and grading performance and improved survival prediction over native 5 × 5\times . These findings motivate broader evaluation of regional event supervision and further reductions in the resources required for adaptation and inference. First, we use UNI2-h with a TopK SAE for transfer from 20 × 20\times to 5 × 5\times . Evaluating alternative combinations across magnification pairs and encoders would clarify whether the gains extend beyond the current configuration. In addition, survival gains vary across MIL architectures. Studying how the distilled patch features interact with aggregation and survival optimization could help explain this variation and identify settings with more consistent gains. Moreover, the student encoder is a fully fine-tuned UNI2-h architecture, so other architectures or methods with low fine-tuning cost could be explored. These extensions could broaden the applicability of regional event supervision in WSI analysis. A.4 Downstream models Each slide is represented by a bag of patch embeddings for MIL training. We standardize each feature dimension using the mean and standard deviation estimated from the training partition. ABMIL [ 23 ] first projects each embedding to 512 dimensions with ReLU, then computes its weight through gated attention with 128 hidden units. Classification and grading use cross-entropy, while survival uses four discrete hazards with equal total training weight per patient. All shared MIL comparisons use AdamW with learning rate 2 × 10 − 4 2\times 10^{-4} , weight decay 10 − 4 10^{-4} , effective batch size 16 and microbatch size one. For a fair comparison, ABMIL, TransMIL [ 49 ] , nnMIL [ 31 ] and RRT-MIL [ 54 ] start with the same 30-epoch training budget. A new validation maximum at the final epoch indicates that further training may help. We extend these runs by 20 epochs, then by 50 epochs whenever the same condition holds at the next boundary. All reported runs finished within 100 epochs. Checkpoints are selected over the full training history, with earlier epochs preferred on exact ties. For nnMIL, we use its aggregation architecture under this protocol. For IMP and IMP-Cervix, we average the patch embeddings of each slide and evaluate linear probes using L2-regularized logistic regression. Features are standardized using the training partition, and validation selects C ∈ { 0.01 , 0.1 , 1 , 10 , 100 } C\in\{0.01,0.1,1,10,100\} . We evaluate the probes on the common development evaluation set for each dataset. Appendix B Method Details B.1 SAE input normalization We apply z-score normalization with a shared scale factor to center teacher features and place SAE inputs on a common numerical scale. Given M M training features t r ∈ ℝ d t_{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{d} , the mean and scale are μ = 1 M ​ ∑ r = 1 M t r , s 2 = 1 M ​ d ​ ∑ r = 1 M ‖ t r − μ ‖ 2 2 , \mu=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{r=1}^{M}t_{r},\qquad s^{2}=\frac{1}{Md}\sum_{r=1}^{M}\|t_{r}-\mu\|_{2}^{2}, (8) and the SAE input is t ~ = ( t − μ ) / s \widetilde{t}=(t-\mu)/s . These statistics are estimated from the SAE training features and fixed throughout dictionary training and regional target construction. Centering expresses each input as a deviation from the average teacher representation. The shared scale gives the training features unit average coordinate variance. Thus, reconstruction error is measured relative to the total variation in the training inputs. Scaling all coordinates equally preserves relative feature distances, variation among coordinates, and the relative lengths of centered vectors.
