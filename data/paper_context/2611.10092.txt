Annotation tools with a model in the loop. CVAT [ 18 ] , Label Studio [ 69 ] and Roboflow [ 56 ] embed SAM-family interactors, as do desktop and bioimage tools [ 75 , 19 , 21 , 49 , 48 ] . In these tools one public checkpoint answers every prompt in every project: the annotator still prompts each object, and cost grows with the object count. Pre-labeling followed by human correction is equally established [ 57 , 2 , 72 ] , and Fluid Annotation [ 2 ] edits a full-image proposal set with a few actions per object. Where a project model exists, as in MONAI Label [ 20 ] and related tools [ 67 , 44 , 83 ] , it is a full network retrained each round, with a confidence slider as the main cost control. We fit SnapHead in seconds on a frozen encoder, with an explicit background class that lets a proposal be rejected and an operating point that minimizes the annotator’s expected effort ( Secs. 3.3 and 3.4 ). Proposals and effort evaluation. R-CNN and its successors [ 26 , 53 , 29 ] established propose-then-classify. Annotation cost is commonly counted in annotator actions: vertex corrections [ 14 , 1 ] , clicks to a quality target in interactive segmentation [ 79 , 61 ] , and actions per object in large annotation campaigns [ 9 , 35 ] . Timed studies supply the relative cost of each action, from drawing and refining a mask to verifying a proposal [ 71 , 49 , 2 , 51 ] . RClicks [ 3 ] shows that click-position oracles overstate interactive performance, a bias our simulated annotator avoids, since it places no clicks. For choosing which image to label next, standard active-learning strategies [ 60 , 25 ] rarely beat random selection at low budgets [ 28 , 80 ] . We build on both lines. Our learned proposer follows CenterNet’s formulation [ 87 , 36 ] on cached encoder features, and our effort model counts every action of a simulated annotator (accept, refine, relabel, delete, draw) and prices each one with weights drawn from these timed studies. 3 Method Figure 2 : Overview of SNAP.
