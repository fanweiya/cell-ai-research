II Results II.1 Astrocyte datasets and experimental setup We evaluate the robustness of AstroSeg-vEM across different ultrastructural conditions using four independent vEM datasets (D1, D2, D3, and D4 in Fig. 1 ). All datasets were acquired from the mouse hippocampus by array tomography, and key acquisition parameters, including strongly anisotropic voxel spacing, image and physical volumes, are summarized in Fig. 1 B. Note that the datasets exhibit pronounced voxel anisotropy with axial thicknesses (70 or 100 nm) that are 14 to 20 times larger than the in-plane resolution (5 × \times 5 nm 2 ). They capture expansive in-plane fields of view of up to 60 × \times 60 µm 2 (D1) and 55 × \times 70 µm 2 (D4), while their axial coverage is limited by the finite number of sections. Based on these characteristics, the datasets encompass two distinct astrocyte profiles: normal and stress-associated astrocyte ultrastructural states (Fig. 1 A). Here, astrocyte ultrastructural states (AUS) refer to distinct electron microscopy-resolvable three-dimensional configurations of astrocyte morphology, including process architecture under normal (N-AUS) and stress-associated (S-AUS) conditions. Three distinct experimental settings (Table 1 ) are defined to systematically evaluate the robustness of 2D segmentation and post-processing pipeline in AstroSeg-vEM across N-AUS and S-AUS. Experiments 1 (Exp 1) and 2 (Exp 2) establish a morphological cross-validation by training on S-AUS datasets and testing on N-AUS datasets and vice versa. Experiment 3 (Exp 3) assesses the full AstroSeg-vEM framework for biological analysis, training on three datasets (D1, D2, D3) and reserving D4 as a held-out test dataset for 3D reconstruction and quantitative morphological analysis. Segmentation performance was evaluated using four metrics: Dice score (Dice), weighted Dice (wDice), precision, and recall; the mathematical definition of wDice is written in Methods. Table 2 summarizes that the proposed model achieves higher Dice and precision, while maintaining comparable performance in wDice and recall. It attains the highest Dice score of 0.70 ± \pm 0.02 and precision of 0.58 ± \pm 0.03, outperforming both SegFormer and Swin-Unet, with a modest but consistent paired advantage over SegFormer across sections (Fig. S3), whereas U-Net exhibits markedly degraded performance (Dice = 0.36 ± \pm 0.05). Consistently high performance of AstroSeg-vEM with wDice (0.94 ± \pm 0.02) and recall (0.89 ± \pm 0.04) indicates that primary astrocyte structures are consistently captured. Improvements in Dice and precision reflect enhanced delineation of fragmented astrocytic processes, highlighting the advantage of transformer-based architectures in modeling fine-scale spatial dependencies. Despite superior segmentation performance, our AstroSeg-vEM introduces only a small increase in model size (+11.7%) and computational cost (+28.7% GFLOPs) relative to SegFormer, while achieving 7.5% faster inference time per section. Compared with U-Net, it reduces computational cost by 89.2% and inference time by 50.8% with 20.1% fewer parameters. Relative to Swin-Unet, AstroSeg-vEM further reduces parameter count by 33.3%, computational cost by 39.4%, and inference time by 14.0%. These results demonstrate that AstroSeg-vEM optimizes the trade-off between segmentation accuracy, runtime efficiency, and model complexity, making it well-suited for capturing the complex and highly ramified astrocyte morphology. On the 83 sections of D4, post-processed Dice decreased from 0.92 ± \pm 0.02 in Exp 3 to 0.71 ± \pm 0.04 in LOVO fold 1 (Supplementary Table S1), and raw Dice decreased from 0.70 ± \pm 0.02 to 0.58 ± \pm 0.04, which we interpret as limited robustness when fewer training volumes are available. D4 served as a test-only volume in Exp 3 and LOVO. AstroSeg-vEM incorporates three post-processing modules designed to refine segmentation outputs and improve structural coherence: soma-guided component refinement (SCR), volumetric structural consolidation (VSC), and in-plane morphological correction (IMC). A systematic ablation study (Table 3 ) shows that the AstroSeg-vEM post-processing pipeline consistently improves Dice in all five evaluated settings, but not always wDice. Already high at baseline, wDice is unchanged or slightly higher in Exp 1 and Exp 3 and decreases in Exp 2, from 0.91 to 0.82 in D1 and from 0.94 to 0.89 in D4. Although the baseline segmentation model already achieves high wDice (0.87–0.94), indicating that primary astrocyte structures are largely captured, Dice remains low due to false predictions. The SCR module reduces spatial inconsistencies, leading to higher Dice, especially in Exp 1 D2 and Exp 2 D1. The VSC module provides the largest gain by enforcing global 3D connectivity, raising Dice from 0.72 to 0.88 in Exp 1 D2 and from 0.43 to 0.67 in Exp 2 D1. The IMC module further refines topology, yielding smaller but consistent gains (e.g. 0.88 to 0.90 in Exp 1 D2). Note that wDice remains relatively stable throughout the ablation process in Exp 1 and Exp 3, suggesting that core astrocytic structures are already identified by the segmentation model, whereas post-processing primarily restores structural continuity rather than improving pixel-wise overlap. These results highlight the importance of morphology-preserving post-processing for producing structurally coherent representations suitable for 3D reconstruction and expert-guided proofreading. Consistent with this, a per-section decomposition of residual errors into the false discovery rate (FDR) and the miss rate (Methods) shows that post-processing lowers the FDR markedly in all five settings (Fig. S2A), whereas the miss rate is similar or lower in Exp 1 and Exp 3 and higher in Exp 2 (Fig. S2B). Because the two rates have different denominators, predicted-positive pixels for the FDR and ground-truth-positive pixels for the miss rate, they are presented as complementary error measures. Component-level analysis of the Exp 3 D4 volume gives the same picture (Supplementary Table S2). Post-processing dramatically reduced the mean number of predicted connected components per section by approximately 90%, from 655.7 to 66.3 (ground truth, 29.1), and the mean number of spurious components per section by approximately 95%, from 599.9 to 30.3. The mean number of split ground-truth components per section also decreased from 10.0 to 5.0, whereas missed ground-truth components increased from 0.9 to 3.4 and merges were rare and unchanged (0.5 per section). This high precision in the primary and secondary branching structures provides the necessary confidence to proceed with the full 3D reconstruction of the 2D stacks. By preserving the integrity of the main morphological scaffold, our pipeline enables volumetric representations that reflect the underlying biological structure rather than reconstruction artifacts. II.3 3D reconstruction and comparative morphological analysis To visually assess the reconstruction quality, we generated a 3D surface mesh from the ten times downsampled post-processed segmented vEM stack in Exp 3 (Fig. 4 B) and anisotropically stretched it back to the original voxel scale. The AstroSeg-vEM-derived mesh successfully extracts the complex, hyper-ramified morphology of astrocytes, demonstrating high visual fidelity and structural congruence with the ground-truth manual annotations across both somatic regions and complex distal processes. Sholl analysis is commonly used to quantify astrocyte morphological complexity 11 , 49 , 50 , 51 , 52 , 53 . For evaluating whether our predicted labels preserve astrocyte morphology for downstream analysis, we performed Sholl analysis (Figs. 4 C and 4 D). Intersection counts were computed using three approaches with the Fiji SNT plugin 54 : Direct analysis of segmented images (original), analysis after auto-skeletonization (thinning), and analysis after manual skeletonization (manual) in Figs. 4 C and 4 D. Auto-skeletonization resulted in an overestimation of mean intersections and a shift in the Sholl profile along the radial axis, likely due to spurious branches introduced by simple thinning-based algorithms. In contrast, direct mask-based analysis and manual skeletonization produced similar mean intersections and comparable Sholl plot profiles. These results indicate that the AstroSeg-vEM framework preserves astrocyte morphology and is suitable for downstream morphometric analysis. We position AstroSeg-vEM not as evidence of a general superiority of 2D over 3D segmentation, but as a substantially lighter, more memory-efficient alternative suited to accelerating native-resolution, manual vEM annotation workflows. The approach demonstrates excellent performance across different ultrastructure and morphological conditions, achieving high segmentation accuracy with efficient computation. Astrocytes are increasingly recognized as structurally complex partners of synapses rather than passive supporting cells. In particular, perisynaptic astrocytic processes occupy nanometer-scale domains around excitatory synapses and regulate neurotransmitter clearance, ionic homeostasis, extracellular diffusion, and metabolic support 5 , 12 , 47 . Classical serial EM studies in hippocampal CA1 demonstrated that astrocytic processes contact only a subset of synapses and often cover only part of the synaptic perimeter, indicating that astrocyte-synapse relationships are spatially selective rather than uniform 5 , 6 . Therefore, the ultrastructural differences detected in the present study may reflect remodeling of the local synaptic microenvironment rather than global astrocyte hypertrophy alone. By enabling 3D reconstruction of astrocytic processes under normal and stress-associated conditions, AstroSeg-vEM provides a practical framework for quantifying astrocyte process architecture, synaptic apposition, and spatial occupancy at a resolution that cannot be achieved by light microscopy. Note that S-AUS should not be interpreted as synonymous with molecularly defined reactive astrogliosis or as direct evidence of causal remodeling. Instead, we operationally define structural remodeling as nanoscale reorganization of astrocytic processes within the synaptic microenvironment, including changes in perisynaptic process geometry, synaptic apposition, and spatial occupancy. These features are not readily accessible using light microscopy 5 , 11 , but can be evaluated by vEM-based reconstruction. Accordingly, our results describe vEM-defined structural states characterized by altered astrocytic process organization, providing a foundation for future studies to investigate how these structural changes influence spatial relationships with neighboring dendrites. Future studies integrating vEM with molecular markers of astrocyte identity, reactivity, metabolic stress, and inflammatory signaling will be required to determine these ultrastructural states in terms of transcriptomic or immunohistochemical astrocyte subtypes. For better structural fidelity assessment, we introduced wDice as a complementary metric whose formal definition is provided in the Methods. By placing greater emphasis on local structural agreement while reducing the influence of spatially distant artifacts, wDice is particularly informative for evaluating preservation of the main morphological structures of astrocytes. According to the ablation study (Table 3 ), wDice remains high (0.81–0.95) across all experimental stages and changes by at most 0.02 in Exp 1 and Exp 3, whereas it decreases in Exp 2, indicating consistent preservation of astrocytic structure, even in branches with peripheral noise. The biological relevance of wDice is further supported by the 3D reconstruction and Sholl analysis results (Fig. 4 ), which show that the predicted segmentations preserve the overall morphological profiles of manual annotations. For possible condition batch confounding, all samples were processed leveraging the same solutions and processing time to minimize preparation-related variability. Although section thickness differed between datasets (70 or 100 nm), comparison of one control and one stress dataset at each thickness did not reveal an apparent thickness-dependent effect on segmentation performance, and we therefore consider section thickness unlikely to be the main source of between-dataset differences. Nevertheless, biological variability among animals and technical variability related to sectioning and imaging cannot be completely excluded. D1 and D2 were prepared on the same day, as were D3 and D4, and the four datasets were therefore prepared in two separate batches. D3 and D4 were imaged in the same session, whereas D1 and D2 were each imaged in a separate session. No apparent batch- or imaging-session-level effect was observed. Because an independent inter-annotator agreement analysis could not be performed retrospectively for the full dataset, the ground-truth annotations may contain annotator-specific uncertainty, and annotator and dataset effects cannot be completely separated in the current dataset. Finally, cross-volume robustness is limited when fewer training volumes are available. Post-processed Dice on D4 decreased from 0.92 ± \pm 0.02 with three training volumes (Exp 3) to 0.71 ± \pm 0.04 with two (LOVO fold 1), and all experiments rest on four volumes from four animals. The AstroSeg-vEM framework also demonstrates high computational efficiency. Across Exp 1 and Exp 2, the per-section inference time ranged from 23.63 s to 102.47 s depending on dataset size and image dimensions (Table 4 ). In Exp 3, our transformer-based model maintains superior segmentation performance while achieving substantially lower inference time than U-Net and Swin-Unet on D4 (Table 2 ). This high throughput is achieved on consumer-grade hardware (NVIDIA RTX 2060, 12 GB VRAM), and the morphology-preserving post-processing adds minimal total overhead (Table 5 ). By bypassing the prohibitive memory requirements of native 3D CNNs, AstroSeg-vEM efficiently leverages the rich in-plane resolution of 2D sections to recover 3D structures. A medium-weight, 3D block-wise system is a promising alternative. In a feasibility experiment, a 3D nnU-Net trained at 20 nm in-plane resolution with four times downsampled input, under the LOVO protocol with training on D1 and D2, reached a per-section Dice of 0.696 ± \pm 0.055 on D4 without post-processing, compared with 0.916 ± \pm 0.020 for the complete AstroSeg-vEM pipeline on the same 20 nm grid (Methods). Because the two models were not trained on the same volumes and nnU-Net received downsampled input and had no post-processing stage, we do not attribute this difference to the architecture. The 3D run required an NVIDIA A100 data-center GPU with 40 GB of memory and about 5.1 hours for 250 training epochs, and we did not attempt a 3D pipeline at native 5 nm resolution. We regard this as a limitation of the present study and a direction for future work. Fok et al. 55 recently introduced a curvature-consistency deep learning approach that accelerates astrocyte reconstruction 12-fold and enables cross-species comparison of astrocyte nanoarchitecture. It was developed for near-isotropic FIB-SEM data (4 × \times 4 × \times 8 nm 3 ), whereas our array-tomography data are strongly anisotropic, with 5 nm pixels and 70–100 nm sections, which motivated our 2D design. Extending such 3D approaches to anisotropic data is a promising direction for future work. While AstroSeg-vEM provides a high-fidelity foundation for mouse hippocampus astrocyte reconstruction, several aspects of the framework could be further improved. Further improvement in scalability for larger vEM volumes may be achieved by incorporating astrocyte-localization modules before high-resolution segmentation, thereby reducing computationally redundant processing of non-target tissue. Extending AstroSeg-vEM toward multi-class segmentation may also broaden its utility for studying astrocyte-synapse interactions in the hippocampal nano-environment. We envision that in future biological applications, analyses of process thickness distribution, synapse proximity, and organelle inclusion or contact-site quantification could further link astrocyte ultrastructural changes to functional alterations in neuron-glia communication. IV Methods IV.1 Sample preparation for vEM imaging Two controls and two with stress-associated astrocyte morphological changes identified by immunohistochemical analysis were used for electron microscopy (EM) analysis. These morphological changes were used to define the stress-associated condition and were not intended to indicate molecularly defined reactive astrogliosis. All animal experiments were conducted in accordance with the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) guidelines and were approved by the IACUC under approval number IACUC-24-00067-M1. Mice were anesthetized with isoflurane and euthanized by cervical dislocation prior to tissue collection. They were subsequently perfused with 0.15 M cacodylate buffer (pH 7.4), followed by fixation with 2% paraformaldehyde (PFA) and 2.5% glutaraldehyde in the same buffer. The brains were removed and further fixed overnight at 4 °C in the same fixative. The fixed brains were then sectioned into 150-µm-thick hippocampal slices using a vibratome (Leica). For scanning electron microscopy (SEM), tissue slices were post-fixed with 2% osmium tetroxide (OsO 4 ; EMS) and 1.5% potassium ferrocyanide (Sigma) in 0.15 M cacodylate buffer for 1 h on ice. The tissues were subsequently incubated in 1% thiocarbohydrazide (TCH; TCI) for 20 min, followed by 2% OsO 4 in distilled water for 30 min at room temperature. The samples were then stained with 2% uranyl acetate (EMS) overnight at 4 °C, followed by incubation in lead aspartate solution for 30 min. The tissues were dehydrated through a graded ethanol series (20%, 50%, 70%, 90%, and 100%) on ice. Following dehydration, the samples were sequentially infiltrated with 100% acetone, mixtures of acetone and epoxy resin at ratios of 3:1, 1:1, and 1:3, and finally 100% epoxy resin. The samples were then embedded using an EMbed-812 embedding kit (EMS) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. For SEM imaging of the mouse hippocampus, serial thin sections (70 or 100 nm thick) were prepared from the resin-embedded samples using an ultramicrotome (EM UC7; Leica) and mounted on indium-tin-oxide-coated glass coverslips. Serial sections were imaged using a GeminiSEM 300 scanning electron microscope (Zeiss) equipped with Atlas 5 software at an accelerating voltage of 3.5 kV. Astrocytes in the stratum radiatum (SR) of the CA1 region were imaged at approximately 100–150 µm from the CA1 pyramidal cell layer. Images were acquired at an x–y pixel size of 5 nm. D1–D4 were obtained from four independent mouse samples. D1 and D4 were tissue blocks from the hippocampus SR region exhibiting a stress-associated astrocyte phenotype, as identified by immunohistochemical analysis, and were sectioned at 70 nm and 100 nm thickness, respectively. D2 and D3 were tissue blocks from the hippocampus SR region without this phenotype, sectioned at 70 nm and 100 nm thickness, respectively. IV.2 Manual annotation procedure vEM datasets were aligned using Fiji software TrakEM2 56 plug-in. The ground-truth annotations were generated using Reconstruct or PyReconstruct 57 . Astrocytes were identified based on characteristic ultrastructural features, including spongiform cytoplasm, the absence of synaptic inputs, and, when present, electron-dense cytoplasmic filaments. Annotators then traced membrane-enclosed cellular processes continuously across serial sections to reconstruct connected astrocytic processes throughout the volume. D1 and D2 were initially annotated by one annotator, and D3 and D4 by a second annotator. All four datasets were subsequently proofread by the same primary annotator, who was different from the initial annotator of each dataset and who checked the continuity of astrocytic processes across consecutive sections, excluded clearly non-astrocytic profiles, corrected disconnected regions, and ensured consistency with adjacent sections. Annotations judged ambiguous were additionally reviewed jointly by multiple annotators. The finalized annotations were subsequently exported as 2D TIFF masks using custom scripts for model training and evaluation. IV.3 Overview of the vEM analysis and reconstruction pipeline Our lightweight and morphology-aware pipeline of astrocyte segmentation and reconstruction was designed as an astrocyte-centric framework to overcome the extreme spatial sparsity, complex branching, and the anisotropic resolution of vEM datasets. Instead of relying on computationally intensive 3D segmentation models, our framework leverages high-resolution 2D planar details and efficiently recovers 3D structures of astrocytes. The overall workflow consists of four integrated stages: (i) Foreground-aware patch sampling to address class imbalance (Fig. 2 A); (ii) Transformer-based 2D segmentation (Fig. 2 B) with sliding-window inference (Fig. 2 C) to extract astrocyte ultrastructures; (iii) Morphology-preserving 2D-to-3D hybrid post-processing (Fig. 2 D) to restore inter-section continuity and ensure volumetric coherence; (iv) Mesh reconstruction and Sholl analysis results (Fig. 4 ). IV.4 Foreground-aware patch sampling from vEM data Foreground of astrocyte vEM data occupies only a small fraction of the tissue volume and appears as sparse, spatially fragmented features in 2D sections, resulting in severe class imbalance and background-dominated sampling. This challenge is further exacerbated by anisotropic voxel spacing. To address this, we employ a foreground-aware patch sampling strategy illustrated in Fig. 2 A. Fixed-size patches (224 × \times 224 pixels) are extracted from each 2D section, with patch centers sampled using a probabilistic mixture: 30% from foreground pixels, and 70% from the entire section. We also apply boundary-aware filtering to improve sample quality. Patches with more than 10% low-intensity background are discarded, and patches consisting entirely of foreground or background are largely excluded, with 5% retained to preserve extreme cases. This astrocyte-centric sampling strategy increases exposure to rare foreground and boundary regions where thin processes are most likely to be missed by random patch extraction. IV.5 Transformer-based astrocyte segmentation Astrocyte segmentation is performed using a transformer-based U-Net architecture with a MiT-B2 (Mix Transformer) encoder (Fig. 2 B) originally introduced in SegFormer, which was initialized with ImageNet pretrained weights. The MiT encoder utilizes overlapping patch embeddings and a hierarchical multi-stage design, enabling progressive feature abstraction while maintaining spatial coherence. Its multi-scale representations capture both coarse somatic regions and fine distal astrocytic processes, facilitating segmentation of sparse and morphologically complex structures. Self-attention modules in transformer blocks model long-range spatial dependencies, integrating spatially distributed ultrastructure beyond the local convolutional receptive field. To improve computational efficiency on large vEM sections, attention is implemented with spatial reduction of key and value features in the first three stages. Subsequently, a U-Net–based decoder with skip connections and squeeze-and-excitation attention modules refines high-resolution features, improving boundary delineation of thin astrocytic processes. Model training is performed on 224 × \times 224 patches extracted from individual vEM sections. Given the strong spatial continuity of astrocyte morphology along the z-axis, particular care is taken to avoid information leakage between training and validation data. Sections within each dataset are sequentially sorted and divided into 80% training and 20% validation subsets, ensuring that all patches from a given section were assigned exclusively to one split. This partition was applied only during training designated for each experiment (Table 1 ). Grayscale images are replicated across three channels to match the transformer encoder (Fig. 2 B), normalized using fixed mean and standard deviation values of [0.5, 0.5, 0.5], and converted to PyTorch tensors. No additional random geometric or intensity data augmentation was applied during training. For scalable inference on high-resolution vEM sections, we adopt an overlapping sliding-window strategy (Fig. 2 C). IV.6 Morphology-preserving post-processing Post-processing is a critical step for astrocyte segmentation due to the complexity of astrocyte morphological features and the reconstruction failure modes associated with thin, spatially fragmented processes. Although transformer-based 2D segmentation produces spatially coherent probabilistic maps, section-by-section predictions often inevitably contain fragmented components, spurious foreground regions, and discontinuities that hinder accurate 3D reconstruction. To enforce structural consistency across sections and preserve biologically meaningful astrocyte morphology, we implement an efficient, multi-stage, morphology-aware post-processing pipeline (Fig. 2 D) that operates collaboratively in both the 2D and 3D domains. Soma-guided component refinement: Section-wise segmentation lacks explicit 3D contextual information, leading to inconsistent false positives. This soma-guided refinement suppresses large, spatially inconsistent connected components arising from the absence of volumetric context, while preserving soma-centered structures and their associated distal processes. By enforcing joint size and spatial distance constraints within a soma-centered reference frame, the procedure enhances volumetric coherence without introducing artificial morphological alterations. Volumetric structural consolidation: While soma-guided component refinement enforces cross-sectional consistency and removes prominent outliers, isolated false predictions may persist due to the inherently limited spatial context of section-wise segmentation. To further ensure global structural consistency, we perform volumetric connectivity analysis on the refined vEM stack. The segmentation is treated as a 3D binary volume, and unsupported single-section artifacts are first suppressed based on their lack of continuity across adjacent sections. Connected components are then identified in voxel space under 26-connectivity, and the k components with the largest volumetric support are retained as the principal astrocytic structures. This volumetric consolidation step removes residual disconnected fragments and segmentation artifacts that lack axial continuity and enforces global morphological coherence across the reconstructed astrocyte volume. In-plane morphological correction: The preceding soma-guided component refinement and volumetric consolidation effectively suppress spatially inconsistent false positives and noise, making the remaining cross-sectional masks highly confident and sufficiently clean. To further improve morphological plausibility, a minimal in-plane morphological operation is applied following volumetric consolidation. Specifically, a standard 2D binary hole-filling algorithm is independently employed on each section to eliminate small, artificial internal cavities caused by local segmentation inconsistencies. This final refinement ensures the solid topological structure of the reconstructed astrocyte without altering its external boundaries and distal processes. IV.7 3D reconstruction and morphology analysis To facilitate high-fidelity visualization and qualitative structural assessment, the marching cubes algorithm 59 implemented by mcubes (PyMCubes) library 60 is applied to the stacked and post-processed 2D vEM sections, converting the resulting 3D volume into a continuous geometric surface model. This reconstruction process employs isosurface extraction to transform volumetric stacks of 2D binary masks into triangular mesh representations. With an intensity threshold of 0.5, a high-resolution geometric model was generated to capture the complex branching morphology of astrocytes. To control the computational complexity of grid generation for large-scale vEM datasets, we directly implement isosurface extraction based on the post-processed 2D stack (downsampled ten times on the xy-plane). The resulting surfaces are further processed and represented using PyVista 61 . To preserve morphological fidelity, a stretch-and-scale transformation is applied to mesh vertices to compensate for anisotropic voxel resolution and any sampling factors introduced during segmentation. IV.8 Evaluation metrics Dice, precision and recall are applied to quantitatively evaluate 2D segmentation performance. While these metrics provide a general evaluation of spatial overlap in plane, they exhibit two critical limitations in the context of astrocyte vEM segmentation. First, their volume-centric nature causes the large soma to dominate the score, potentially masking errors in thin astrocytic processes. Second, all false positives are penalized uniformly, despite distant artifacts being easily removed during post-processing.
