Terminology: OOD - out of distribution; EORTC - European Organisation for Research and Treatment of Cancer; Train - training split; Val - validation split; Test - test split. 2.1 CHIMERA Challenge design and organization The CHIMERA challenge was organized as part of MICCAI 2025 workshops, and the challenge was conducted on the Grand Challenge platform Meakin et al. [2025] at Chia et al. [2026] . We referred to the Biomedical Image Analysis Challenges (BIAS) reporting guidelines Maier-Hein et al. [2020b] in designing and reporting the challenge results. The challenge consisted of three tasks and this work presents two that pertained to bladder cancer. The challenge was conducted in three phases: training and debugging (April 10, 2025), validation (June 13, 2025), and final testing (August 22, 2025) (full schedule at Supplementary S3 ). During training, participants accessed the full training dataset hosted on AWS, while a small subset (three cases per task) was available on the Grand Challenge platform for algorithm debugging and pipeline validation. The validation and test datasets remained hidden and were accessible only through the platform during their respective phases. Supporting data derived from the training data (such as tumor mask and feature embeddings) were additionally provided. All the data publicly released by the challenge was under the Creative Commons Attribution-NonCommercial-ShareAlike (CC BY-NC-SA) license. There was no restriction on using all or partial of the available data modalities, and no requirement or restriction on using the supporting data or external data (including foundation models), provided they are freely and publicly accessible under a permissive open-source license. Participants submitted their algorithms to the platform as Docker images or Dockerfiles, and only fully automatic algorithms were allowed. Each submission consisted of a containerized implementation containing all required code and dependencies to perform inference on the hidden datasets within the Grand Challenge environment. Once deployed, the algorithm container processed each case provided by the platform. All preprocessing, feature extraction and inference steps were executed within the container without internet access to ensure data security and reproducibility. The number of allowed submissions was unlimited during the training and debugging phase, limited to five during the validation phase and restricted to a single submission during the final test phase. During the test phase, the participants were required to submit a 6-page manuscript detailing the methods and usage of any external data, and a public or private Github URL containing the source code of the algorithm. Validation and test case labels were held exclusively by the organizers and were not released publicly nor to the participants during the challenge. Algorithm evaluation was performed using a separate evaluation container maintained by the organizers. The evaluation metrics and submission templates are described in Section 2.5 . Each participating team received the model validation results directly on Grand Challenge after the submission during the debugging and validation phases, and the final test phase results were announced publicly through one public leaderboard for each task (Task BRS: https://chimera.grand-challenge.org/evaluation/brs-prediction-final-test/leaderboard/ , Task Progression: https://chimera.grand-challenge.org/evaluation/bladder-cancer-survival-prediction-final-test/leaderboard/ ). Other challenge rules and requirements: all participants were required to form teams of minimum one member, and teams found using duplicated Grand Challenge profiles were disqualified. Anonymous participation were not permitted. Members of sponsoring or organizing institutes were allowed to participate but were not eligible for awards. During the workshop, cash prizes of €1,000, €600 and €400 were awarded to the top first, second and third teams, respectively. Post-challenge, the top-ranked teams were invited to join the CHIMERA Consortium (Supplementary S2 ), with up to five co-authors per team included as consortium authors. The participants were encouraged to publish their results obtained using the public training dataset after an embargo period, and were required to cite the CHIMERA journal publication thereafter. 2.2 Dataset 2.2.1 Tumor characteristics The challenge was designed around the tasks for prognosis predictions in the target cohort of high-risk non-muscle-invasive bladder cancer (HR-NMIBC) patients. The challenge cohort consisted of H&E tumor slides obtained from two independent HR-NMIBC patient groups. The first group (Erasmus MC) included 328 patients profiled by de Jong et al. de Jong et al. [2023] , each with a single tumor, who received at least five of six BCG induction instillations between 2000 and 2018 across five hospitals: Cohort A (177 patients) from three Dutch hospitals (Erasmus MC, Franciscus Gasthuis & Vlietland, and Amphia); and Cohort B (151 patients), with half drawn equally from these Dutch centers and half from Reinier de Graaf (the Netherlands) and Stavanger University Hospital (Norway). The second group (UroLife) consisted of 94 patients (94 tumors) meeting the same selection criteria, drawn from Urolife De Goeij et al. [2019] and the Nijmegen Bladder Cancer Study Grotenhuis et al. [2014] , Khoraminia et al. [2026] . All tumors samples (T1 and Ta) containing regions with at least 80% tumor purity were used Khoraminia et al. [2026] . 2.3 Data modalities pairing and data splits A case was defined by each tumor associating with one or more of three data modalities: structured data, RNA-seq and digitized H&E slides. In total, 49 cases from the Erasmus group and 5 from Urolife lacking one of the three modalities were excluded. Additionally, 4 Urolife cases with tumors labeled as "intermediate risk" according to EORTC criteria were excluded, as this category was not present in the Erasmus Cohort. The remaining multimodal cases were split into training/validation/test sets for Task BRS (182/17/169) and Task Progression (176/17/165). All cases were included in Task BRS, whereas six recurrent cases were excluded from Task Progression to avoid confounding effects introduced by prior recurrences and treatments. (See Fig. 3 .) 2.3.1 Structured data and reference standard Structured clinicopathological data (hereafter, structured data) were defined as standardized information derived from clinical records and pathology images. Structured data for the Erasmus cohorts were obtained from Supplementary Table S2 of de Jong et al. [2023] , and the Urolife cohort from De Goeij et al. [2019] . The stage and substage variables in both cohorts were harmonized. In Urolife cohort, "T1HGIS" in stage variable was converted to "T1HG", while "T1a" and "T1b" in substage were converted to "T1m" (lamina propria invasion in ≤ \leq 2 HPF) and "T1e" (more extensive invasion), respectively. Number of BCG instillations ( no_instillations ), a treatment-exposure variable, was not available for the Urolife cohort. This variable and all other missing values across the variables were encoded as "-1". Progression was defined as documented advancement from non-muscle-invasive bladder cancer to muscle-invasive disease ( ≥ \geq T2), regional lymph-node metastasis (N+), or distant metastasis (M+) Lamm et al. [2013] . BCG treatment failure, treatment discontinuation, or radical cystectomy without documented muscle-invasive disease did not by themselves constitute progression. BCG failure and the progression endpoint used in CHIMERA therefore represent related but non-equivalent longitudinal clinical events Gontero et al. [2025] . Detailed definitions of other variables are provided in de Jong et al. [2023] . For the analyses below, variables were grouped as image-derived (pathology-derived) and non-image-derived (clinical-derived) categories (Tables 1 and 2 ). EORTC was considered as an image-derived variable, although it is a composite score that combines both clinical and pathological factors Sylvester et al. [2006] . For Task BRS, the biomarker-derived BRS obtained from RNA-seq served as the reference standard. For Task Progression, BRS was included as an input variable, while progression status and time to progression or end of follow-up ( time_to_prog_or_FUend ) served as the reference standard. [2023] was performed separately for Cohorts A and B. Cohort B samples were sequenced at a higher depth (80 million paired-end 150 base pair (bp) read length, NEBNext Ultra protocol, New England Biolabs) than Cohort A (50 million paired-end 150 read length, NEBNext Ultra II protocol, New England Biolabs). No batch effect adjustment was performed on these two cohorts. For Urolife, Takara SMARTer Stranded Total RNA-seq Kit v3 - Pico Input Mammalian Library Prep Kit (80 million paired-end 150 bp read length) was used following the manufacturer’s protocol (Cat. No. 634485, Takara Bio). All cohorts were processed for RNA raw counts by pseudoalignment with Kallisto v0.48.0 Bray et al. [2016] using GRCh38 as the reference human genome, and GENCODE release 39 for gene annotation. Raw counts were subjected to transformation, normalization and variance stabilizing transformation using the DESeq2 Love et al. [2014] pipeline. The resultant data was in log-like scale with stabilized variance across expression levels. Only protein-coding (mRNA) genes were included, and all 3 datasets have the same final list of mRNA genes. 2.3.3 Histopathology whole slide images All H&E stained slides were collected and digitized at a resolution of 0.25 µm/pixel using a 3DHistech Pannoramic 1000 scanner (3DHistech, Hungary). Raw MIRAX (*.mrxs) files were converted to TIFF format using ASAP Litjens [2023] to remove metadata for full anonymization. 2.4 Data quality inspection Structured data. Missing clinical values and the standard mean difference (SMD) of the variables between Cohort B and U, are summarized in Table 1 and 2 . Histopathology image data. H&E slides in the training set, were annotated as good quality, presence of holes (punches for RNA-seq) and tumor presence in the tissue sections. The annotations were published as supporting data (Section 2.4.1 ). Potential cohort shift was inspected via projecting patch feature embeddings (UNI, Chen et al. [2024] ) on to a dimensionally reduced space using TSNE (TSNE, scikit-learn v1.8.0) (Fig. 3 B). RNA-seq data. Because housekeeping genes are expected to be expressed at stable, comparable levels across samples regardless of underlying disease biology, their expression pattern provides a control for detecting technical batch effects between cohorts independent of true biological variation. Seven housekeeping genes previously evaluated in human bladder cancer Ohl et al. [2006] were therefore used to assess batch effects via PCA (number of components = 7): ACTB , ALAS1 , G6PD , HMBS , HPRT1 , SDHA , and TBP (Fig. 3 C). As this check was restricted to a small panel of stably expressed genes and might not be sensitive to express global technical shifts arising from differences in library preparation or sequencing depth, we further modeled the data cohort shift by fitting the top 2000 variable genes in an L2-regularized logistic-regression classifier (LogisticRegression, scikit-learn v1.8.0) via a five-fold stratified cross-validation to distinguish cohort membership. The following attributes were assessed: WSI quality (tissue preparation, staining or scanning quality; Good/Poor), presence of holes (Yes/No); presence of tumor (Yes/No). 2.5 Evaluation metrics In Task BRS. , the weighted F1-score was used to evaluate the binary classification between BRS1/2 and BRS3, due to class imbalance in the dataset. The reference standard was based on molecular analysis de Jong et al. [2023] . Because each patient contributed a single tumor and a single WSI, patient-, tumor- and slide-level evaluations are equivalent here for the algorithm target. For each participating team, the F1-score was computed once over the full test set, and submissions were ranked in a descending order. The evaluation script did not handle missing results, and therefore no score was computed for the submission. The full evaluation script is available at https://github.com/nadieh/CHIMERA_minimal_baseline/blob/main/Evaluation/Task_2/evaluate.py . In Task Progression. , the algorithm target was at patient level. Model performance was assessed using censored concordance index (C-index) Harrell et al. [1982] , which measures the proportion of comparable patient pairs that are correctly ordered by predicted risk. Predicted risk is defined as the negation of predicted time-to-progression. In other words, the algorithms were evaluated on their ability to rank patients by the predicted time to progression. This choice allowed a fair comparison of survival models despite the heterogeneous follow-up conditions in the dataset. Two patients were considered comparable if both experienced the event at different times, or if one experienced the event and the other remained event-free with a longer follow-up; pairs with identical event times were excluded. A pair was concordant when the patient with the higher predicted risk had a shorter time-to-progression. The C-index ranges from 0.5 (random prediction) to 1.0 (perfect concordance). For each participating team, the C-index was computed once over the full test set, and submissions were ranked accordingly. Similar to Task BRS, the evaluation script did not handle missing results and did not compute a score for that submission. The full evaluation script is available at https://github.com/nadieh/CHIMERA_minimal_baseline/blob/main/Evaluation/Task_3/evaluate.py . 2.6 CHIMERA benchmarking design To benchmark the top-performing models, we conducted a six-part analysis. 1. Summary of algorithms . We summarized the approaches based on manuscripts and code repositories submitted by the team. Solutions from the top eight teams from Task BRS and top five from Task Progression were included. Methods were summarized according to different categories: data preprocessing, missing data handling, feature encoding, and modality fusion approaches. 2. Robustness . We evaluated robustness to domain shift via separate inferences on the Erasmus (Cohort B) and Urolife datasets, the latter excluded from the training data. Task BRS predictions were evaluated using bootstrapped F1 scores and area under the ROC (AUROC) (10,000 resamples within each cohort), and for each team the bootstrap distribution of between-cohort differences yielded a two-sided p-value. C-indices in Task Progression were bootstrapped similarly. Since the cohorts shared no patients to pair on, two independently resampled bootstrap distributions were combined element-wise to obtain the sampling distribution of the difference. P-values were adjusted across all teams using the Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) procedure, with significance threshold set at α \alpha = 0.05 throughout this study. Additionally, we explored whether the cohort-associated RNA-seq signal was accompanied by cohort-dependent changes in downstream multimodal model performance. Team TIA-Pegasus voluntarily submitted two additional Task Progression sub-models: an H&E-only unimodal model and an H&E+RNA-seq bimodal model. Both were evaluated on the same Task Progression test set, and cohort-specific C-indices were compared using the bootstrap method described above. 3. Structured data: missing values . We assessed robustness to incomplete structured data by randomly masking 25%, 50%, or 75% of structured-data entries at inference and replacing them with the challenge missing-value code (-1). Analyses were performed separately for Erasmus Cohort B and Urolife. Complete removal of individual variables was evaluated separately in the single-variable ablation analysis described below. 4. Structured data: missing variables . We conducted an ablation study by removing structured data variables, evaluated separately for Cohort B and Urolife. Each variable was masked entirely during inference by assigning "-1" across all samples. Performance was compared against the original cohort-specific test results, with statistical significance of each ablation effect assessed using a permutation test comparing ablated model outputs against baseline (unablated) outputs. 5. Reduced models: removing modality-overlapping variables . We evaluated multimodal architectures using reduced structured data containing only non-image-derived clinical variables, to assess whether models could leverage complementary information from the image modality when overlapping, pathology-derived features were excluded. Statistical comparison between the full and reduced models, for both Task BRS (weighted F1, AUROC) and Task Progression (C-index), used a paired patient-level bootstrap. On each of 10,000 replicates, patients were resampled with replacement once, and both the full and reduced model were re-evaluated on that identical resampled set, so that the two models were always compared on matched patients rather than resampled independently. This yielded a 95% percentile confidence interval directly on the full-vs-reduced difference and a two-sided bootstrap p-value from the proportion of the difference distribution crossing zero, with Benjamini-Hochberg False Discovery Rate (FDR) correction applied across all team-cohort comparisons within each task. 6. Error analysis . We performed patient-level cross-model error analyses to identify test cases that were consistently difficult across the evaluated algorithms. For Task BRS, each model prediction was compared with the RNA-seq-derived reference label and categorized as correct, false positive, or false negative. For each patient, we counted the number of models that produced an incorrect prediction and ranked patients according to this count. Patients misclassified by more than half of the evaluated models were considered shared, or consensus, error cases. Their predicted BRS3 probabilities and error directions were examined to distinguish consistent high-confidence misclassification from disagreement near the classification threshold. For Task Progression, patient-level error was quantified as the proportion of comparable patient pairs involving a given patient that were incorrectly ordered according to predicted progression risk. Patients with consistently high discordance across models were identified as shared difficult cases and examined for common clinicopathological characteristics. These analyses were intended to distinguish patient-specific prediction difficulty from errors confined to individual model architectures. 2.7 Models and model output used in benchmarking Benchmarking analysis (2) used the predictions of test submissions retrieved form the Grand Challenge platform.
