2 Method 2.1 Overview Bg-Mix randomly extracts foreground objects and object-free backgrounds from training images in a given dataset and subsequently mixes them back together to form new training samples (cf. Fig. 1 ). Figure 1: Bg-Mix . The background of a random training image (a) and the foreground objects of another random training image (b) are extracted and blended to form the background-augmented image (c). 2.2 Background Extraction From a randomly drawn training image, the background is extracted in one of the following three ways: A. Selecting the largest object-free square in the image and resizing it back to the image size, B. Removing its objects using segmentation masks and setting the pixels of the mask to zero, C. Removing its objects using segmentation masks and applying biharmonic inpainting [ 2 ] , which fills the mask by smooth interpolation based on the surrounding pixel values. 2.3 Object Blending Bg-Mix incorporates two modes to blend extracted foreground objects and backgrounds: D. Hard edges: The foreground objects are pasted onto the background as they are, E. Blurred edges: The pasted foreground objects’ boundaries are blurred using a Gaussian kernel of size 3 × 3 3\times 3 with standard deviation σ = 0.8 \sigma=0.8 . 2.4 Object Placement We consider six object placement methods depending on the foreground, background and the placement order: F. Foreground objects from one image, hence one class, are pasted onto a background from any class at their original positions, G. Foreground objects from one image, hence one class, are pasted onto a background from the same class at their original positions, H. Foreground objects from multiple images of a single class are pasted onto a background from any class at random non-overlapping positions, I. Foreground objects from multiple images of a single class are pasted onto a background from the same class at random non-overlapping positions, J. Foreground objects from multiple images of any class are pasted onto a background from any class at random non-overlapping positions, K. At least one foreground object from each class is pasted onto a background from any class at random non-overlapping positions. 3 Experiments Table 1: Mask R-CNN and RTMDet-Ins models with Copy-Paste and Bg-Mix on EMViR and VISN datasets. In both datasets, each image contains objects of only one class, which enabled us to use class-matched backgrounds for experiments. The EMViR dataset [ 7 ] contains 1084 negative-staining STEM images with segmentation masks of four classes of respiratory viruses (Adeno, Corona, Orthomyxo and Paramyxo), split into 762 training, 157 validation and 165 test images. The virus dataset of [ 16 ] , abbreviated VISN in the following, contains 622 negative-staining TEM images with segmentation masks of six virus classes (SARS-CoV-2, H1N1, Respiratory syncytial, Herpes simplex, Adeno and Vaccinia), divided into 499 training and 123 test images. As pre-processing steps, (1) the EMViR object masks were converted to COCO json annotations to match the VISN format, (2) a VISN validation set was created from a stratified 15% of the original training set, and (3) all images across both datasets were resized to 1024 × 1024 1024\times 1024 pixels and their annotations were rescaled proportionally. 3.2 Training We evaluated Bg-Mix on both datasets with two object detection/instance segmentation architectures: Mask R-CNN [ 6 ] with a ResNet-50-FPN backbone and RTMDet-Ins-L [ 13 ] . Both models were initialized with weights pretrained on COCO [ 11 ] , implemented using the MMDetection toolbox [ 1 ] and fine-tuned on each dataset. We kept each architecture’s published optimizer and learning rate schedule and extended it to 500 epochs: SGD with momentum 0.9 and weight decay 10 − 4 10^{-4} , with the learning rate divided by 10 at epochs 333 and 458, for Mask R-CNN, and AdamW with weight decay 0.05 and cosine decay from epoch 250 for RTMDet-Ins. With a uniform batch size of 8, we scaled the published learning rates linearly: from 0.02 (batch size 16) to 0.01 for Mask R-CNN, and from 0.004 (batch size 256) to 1.25 × 10 − 4 1.25\times 10^{-4} for RTMDet-Ins. Bg-Mix was applied independently to each training image with probability p p , so the number of training images per epoch stayed fixed. To observe the effect of Bg-Mix alone, no other augmentation was used. We compared Bg-Mix using the same architectures without augmentation (baseline) and with Copy-Paste [ 3 ] , the latter applied with the same probability as Bg-Mix : p = 0.5 p=0.5 on EMViR and p = 0.01 p=0.01 on VISN. This choice was made based on our observation that for the EMViR dataset, 0.25 ≤ p ≤ 0.75 0.25\leq p\leq 0.75 increased performance, but for the VISN dataset, p > 0.01 p>0.01 decreased the performance. We evaluated on the validation set every 10 epochs and kept the checkpoint with the best validation box mAP for testing. All runs were averaged over three random seeds. 3.3 Results Bg-Mix obtained the largest gain on the EMViR dataset for both architectures (+2.6 and +3.3 box mAP) (cf. Table 1 ). For the VISN dataset, Bg-Mix had a small increase (+0.8 and +0.8 box mAP) for both models. The small increase for Bg-Mix (and the corresponding decrease for Copy-Paste) for the Mask R-CNN architecture and the VISN dataset can be attributed to the already saturated baseline (77.7 box mAP). From the ablation study involving the Mask R-CNN architecture and the EMViR dataset (cf. Table 2 ), we observe the following, reporting gains of box mAP in points averaged over all runs using the corresponding option: (1) Background Extraction: inpainting ( + 1.2 +1.2 ) and zeroing the object pixels ( + 1.1 +1.1 ) perform best, (2) Blending: hard edges are slightly better than blurred edges ( + 1.1 +1.1 vs. + 0.8 +0.8 ), and (3) Object Placement: original objects at their original positions work best ( + 2.0 +2.0 ). Table 1 . 4 Conclusion This study describes Bg-Mix , a novel background augmentation method for virus detection in negative-staining STEM/TEM images: it extracts the objects from one training image, the object-free background from another and pastes the extracted objects onto this extracted background. Compared to the baseline, the performance of models improved significantly by incorporating the Bg-Mix method. Our study is limited to object detection of viruses in EM images, however, we believe our findings can be transferred to other modalities with target objects on content-rich backgrounds.
