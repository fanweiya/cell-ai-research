III Methods Fig. 3: PTSN Structure III-A paradigm—Position-Time Series Network (PTSN) The core advantage of Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs) lies in their recursive structure, which uses the output of previous time steps as input for the current step, thereby gradually accumulating contextual information from different locations. When processing images, RNNs can effectively capture the spatial relationships between patches in the image and model long-range dependencies. Building on this advantage, we propose a novel general paradigm—Position-Time Series Network (PTSN). As shown in Figure 3 , this design employs a gated mechanism combined with the σ \sigma ( s ​ i ​ g ​ m ​ o ​ i ​ d sigmoid ) and t ​ a ​ n ​ h tanh activation functions to dynamically filter and suppress feature information. At the same time, by leveraging the complementary relationship between the ” 1 − 1- ” operation and the sigmoid output, the network dynamically adjusts the flow of features, dividing them into key parts to be preserved and ineffective parts to be suppressed, thus enabling more refined feature selection. This design helps balance information flow, preventing features from being overly concentrated or completely lost, while enhancing the model’s nonlinear expressiveness by separating the retained and suppressed information. Additionally, the mechanism reduces noise interference, lowers the risk of overfitting, and improves the model’s stability and adaptability to complex data through the diversion operation during gradient backpropagation. In contrast, convolution operations can effectively smooth input data, filter out high-frequency noise, and remove redundant details, thus mitigating this problem. This characteristic helps reduce the burden on the PTSN layer, allowing it to focus more on learning the key features of the sequence. By applying convolution operations to the input data, extreme values in the data can be smoothed or weakened, thereby reducing the magnitude of gradient fluctuations during backpropagation. As a result, we introduced an optimization in the embedding operation of VIT by adopting the KAN convolution method. Fig. 4: FKAC Structure The main difference between KAN convolution and the conventional convolution used in traditional CNNs lies in the construction of the convolution kernel. In traditional CNNs, the convolution kernel is composed of weight parameters, whereas in KAN convolution, each element φ \varphi of the convolution kernel is a learnable nonlinear function represented using B-splines. Formally, each φ \varphi is a univariate nonlinear function with trainable parameters. The main issue with KAN convolution lies in the basis functions within the model, which require more computational resources during training and increase the risk of overfitting. To address these issues, we propose the Fourier-based KAN Convolution (FKAC), as shown in Figure 4 . Before applying the basis functions to the input data, we suggest first using a compression convolution with a kernel size of 1, followed by the application of the basis functions, and then an expansion convolution with a kernel size of 1. Intuitively, this process can be seen as a single-layer encoder, which first extracts meaningful features from the input data, then processes them through the chosen basis functions, and finally restores them via a single-layer decoder. The residual activation helps preserve important details that may be lost during the encoding and decoding process. However, due to the introduction of spline functions, training KAN is more difficult than training an MLP, which contradicts the more efficient and effective alternative we propose. The essence of KAN is to approximate any function through the sum of multiple nonlinear functions. Our goal is to decompose a complex function into several relatively simple nonlinear functions. Its primary goal is to segment hepatic vessels and tumors from Hepatic CT images. The dataset contains 443 cases of 3D CT data, with each slice image having a resolution of 512x512 pixels. Due to the elongated and intricate structure of hepatic vessels, the HepaticVessel dataset is particularly suitable for analyzing the performance of models in long-distance segmentation tasks. IV-B Experiment Setup All experiments were conducted on a device equipped with an NVIDIA 3090 GPU (24GB memory) using the PyTorch framework. For the MSD dataset, five-fold cross-validation was employed for evaluation. The initial learning rate was set to 0.01, the batch size was 2, and the model was trained for 1000 iterations. For performance evaluation, the Dice similarity coefficient was used as the primary metric to measure the overlap between the predicted results and the ground truth labels. IV-C Experimental Results The experimental results on the Hepatic Vessel dataset indicate that, due to the large volume of the dataset, models based on self-attention mechanisms significantly outperform pure convolutional models, such as nnUNet, especially in the recent nnFormer and TransUNet models. Thanks to the introduction of the self-attention mechanism, they achieved excellent scores of 66.29% and 67.24%, respectively. This demonstrates that the self-attention mechanism can effectively model global context on high-resolution images. However, these models overlook the learning of relative positions between patches, failing to fully leverage the advantages of long-range dependency modeling. To further improve segmentation performance, our method enhances the modeling of positional information between patches based on the self-attention mechanism, achieving an average DICE score of 69.02%. Particularly in the segmentation accuracy of hepatic vessels, our model achieved 65.90%, an improvement of 1.32% over TransUNet, as shown in Table I and Figure 1 . By incorporating the PTSN module, the model transforms the spatial relationships between patch blocks into temporal relationships and enhances the relative positional relationships between patch blocks through time-series modeling. To prevent issues such as gradient explosion, we propose the FKAC module, which optimizes the traditional embedding module. Based on this, we combine the KAN network, known for its excellent fitting capabilities, and replace the traditional embedding layer with residual convolution, successfully achieving long-distance segmentation in complex environments. Extensive experimental results on the Hepatic Vessel dataset show that the SegKAN model outperforms existing state-of-the-art methods. Future research will further explore the potential applications of SegKAN in more complex and diverse medical image environments.
