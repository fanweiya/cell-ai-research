Localization Gains from Explanation Regularization Should Not Be Interpreted as Faithfulness Gains Amrin Akter1, Mauro Dalla Mura1, Diana Mateus2, and Dawood Al Chanti1 1 Univ. Grenoble Alpes, CNRS, Grenoble INP, GIPSA-lab, Grenoble, France {amrin.akter,mauro.dalla-mura,dawood.al-chanti}@grenoble-inp.fr 2 Nantes Université, École Centrale Nantes, LS2N, Nantes, France diana.mateus@ls2n.fr Abstract. Training-time explanation regularization is often evaluated by whether saliency maps become more concentrated within annotated regions. We argue that this criterion alone is insufficient: transformation consistency, lesion localization, and faithfulness to the classifier are dis- tinct properties and should be evaluated separately, particularly in med- ical imaging. We examine this distinction through a case study of Con- trastive Grad-CAM Consistency (CGC) in three-class brain tumor MRI classification. Using a single-channel ResNet-50 with five-fold patient- grouped cross-validation, we compare a cross-entropy baseline with CGC. CGC changes overall Pointing Game (PG) from 27.24% to 36.02% and Relevance Mass Accuracy (RMA) from 11.17% to 18.01%, while clas- sification accuracy changes from 93.63% to 94.87%. Perturbation-based faithfulness metrics show inconsistent changes: Insertion Area Under the Curve (IAUC) changes from 0.73 to 0.78, whereas Deletion Area Under the Curve (DAUC) favors the baseline (0.36 versus 0.41; lower is better), and overall Iterative Removal Of Features (IROF) is 0.57 for both mod- els. These results support our position that improved spatial agreement with pathology should not be interpreted as a general improvement in explanation faithfulness. We therefore recommend property-specific eval- uation in which the property targeted by the explanation objective, spa- tial agreement with reference regions, and model reliance are measured and reported separately. Keywords: Explainable AI · Grad-CAM · Faithfulness · Explanation evaluation · Brain tumor MRI · Medical imaging 1 Introduction High predictive accuracy in medical imaging does not ensure that a classifier relies on clinically relevant image evidence. Saliency maps are commonly used to identify image regions associated with the model prediction. These expla- nations, however, require independent evaluation. A visually plausible heatmap does not establish that the highlighted regions contribute substantially to the model prediction [1,3,23]. arXiv:2610.26292v1 [eess.IV] 19 Oct 2026 2 A. Akter et al. This distinction becomes particularly important when explanations are ex- plicitly regularized during training. Greater spatial concentration of Grad-CAM saliency within a lesion does not, by itself, establish greater interpretability or faithfulness. We therefore distinguish three properties: 1. Transformation consistency: whether an explanation responds consis- tently to a label-preserving input transformation. 2. Localization: whether saliency aligns spatially with a clinically or seman- tically meaningful reference region. 3. Faithfulness: whether regions ranked as important affect the model output under a specified intervention. These properties are not interchangeable. A transformation-consistent heatmap may still consistently highlight shortcut features. A heatmap may overlap a tu- mor without capturing all image evidence used by the classifier. Conversely, a perturbation-based metric may assign a more favorable score to an explana- tion whose influential regions extend beyond the annotated lesion. Using one property as a proxy for another can therefore support claims that exceed the corresponding evidence. We examine this position through a case study of Contrastive Grad-CAM Consistency (CGC) [19] on the Brain Tumor MRI dataset [7]. CGC explicitly en- courages consistency between Grad-CAM maps under geometric transformations without requiring pixel-level explanation annotations. Using patient-grouped cross-validation, we compare CGC with a cross-entropy baseline in terms of lesion localization and perturbation-based faithfulness. The case study provides empirical evidence for this distinction; it does not introduce a new XAI method or constitute clinical validation. CGC yields higher mean lesion-localization scores, whereas the three perturbation-based faithful- ness metrics do not indicate a consistent faithfulness gain. This empirical sepa- ration motivates the following position: Explanation-aware training should be evaluated using separate, explicitly named criteria for the property targeted by the objective, spatial agree- ment with reference regions, and model reliance. Improvement on one criterion should not be interpreted as improvement on the others. The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews expla- nation regularization and its evaluation, Section 3 presents the CGC case study, and Section 4 reports the empirical evidence. Section 5 discusses the implications for XAI evaluation, followed by the limitations in Section 6 and the conclusion in Section 7. 2 Explanation Regularization and Its Evaluation Post-hoc visual explanation methods highlight input regions associated with model predictions without modifying the trained model. Gradient-based meth- ods derive feature importance from class-score gradients [25] or path-integrated Localization Is Not Faithfulness 3 gradients [27]. Class-activation methods construct spatial explanations from intermediate convolutional feature maps. Class Activation Mapping (CAM) combines these maps using classifier weights [31], whereas Grad-CAM uses class-specific gradients to determine their weights and applies to a broader range of convolutional neural network (CNN) architectures [24]. Subsequent variants modify the weighting or aggregation of the feature maps, includ- ing Grad-CAM++ [5], Score-CAM [29], LayerCAM [12], and Eigen-CAM [4]. Perturbation-based approaches estimate importance from changes in model predictions under input perturbations [9,18]. Visually plausible saliency maps, however, do not necessarily provide reliable accounts of model behavior. Ran- domization tests show that some saliency methods are insensitive to model- parameter or label randomization [1], while medical imaging studies report substantial variation among explanation methods and limited agreement with annotated pathology [3,23]. Explanation-aware training incorporates explanation-related objectives di- rectly into model optimization. Approaches include attribution regulariza- tion [22,21], spatial supervision of attention or attribution maps such as Grad- Mask and GAIN [26,16], and weakly supervised localization based on image- level labels or pseudo-masks [15,14,13]. Depending on their formulation, these methods may require additional supervision, spatial annotations, architectural modifications, or domain-specific priors. CGC [19] does not require pixel-level explanation supervision; instead, it applies contrastive learning to encourage agreement between Grad-CAM explanations of an image and its transformed counterpart. Spatial agreement with annotated regions is one criterion used to evaluate the resulting explanations, including in the original CGC study [19]. Localization, perturbation-based faithfulness, and transformation consistency nevertheless quantify distinct properties and should not be treated as inter- changeable. 2.1 Localization and Model Reliance Localization metrics quantify spatial agreement between a saliency map and a reference region. Pointing Game (PG) [30] records whether the maximally salient pixel lies within the reference region, whereas Relevance Mass Accuracy (RMA) measures the fraction of total saliency contained within that region [2]. Both metrics therefore measure agreement with a spatial annotation rather than dependence of the model output on the annotated region. However, lesion overlap is not equivalent to faithfulness. A classifier may use both lesion and non-lesion evidence. Greater concentration of a heatmap within the lesion does not establish that the corresponding pixels are the most influential under a specified perturbation. This distinction is particularly relevant in medical imaging because acquisition and dataset-construction artifacts can themselves carry predictive information. The Cheng brain tumor dataset used in our case study provides a concrete example of this distinction. Prior work showed that tumor type can be predicted from non-tumor information in this benchmark, including cues associated with 4 A. Akter et al. representative-slice selection and anatomy [28]. High classification accuracy on this dataset therefore does not establish that the classifier relies primarily on clinically relevant tumor information. Accordingly, improved lesion overlap sup- ports a localization claim but does not, by itself, establish increased reliance on clinically relevant evidence. 2.2 Protocol Dependence of Perturbation-Based Faithfulness Metrics Perturbation-based faithfulness metrics evaluate how the model output changes when input features are modified according to their attribution ranking. DAUC [18] progressively perturbs pixels from highest to lowest saliency while recording the target-class probability. IAUC [18] progressively restores pixels from a specified baseline image in the same saliency order while recording the target-class probability. IROF [20] partitions the input into regions, ranks them by mean attribution, and progressively removes them while recording the target-class probability. The interpretation of these metrics is necessarily conditional on the per- turbation protocol. Different baselines, replacement values, region definitions, or perturbation orders can produce different outcomes. Perturbations may also pro- duce inputs outside the training distribution. We therefore regard these metrics as complementary evidence of model reliance rather than definitive measures of explanation quality [8,11]. 2.3 Direct Evaluation of the Targeted Explanation Property When an explanation-aware objective targets a particular property, that prop- erty should be evaluated directly. CGC, for example, explicitly encourages agreement between the heatmap of a transformed image and the correspondingly transformed heatmap of the original image [19]. Localization and faithfulness are downstream properties; neither substitutes for a direct transformation- consistency measure. Our case study includes qualitative examples under transformation but does not report a quantitative transformation-consistency metric. We therefore treat transformation consistency as quantitatively unmeasured and do not infer it from improved localization. More generally, an explanation-aware objective should not be evaluated solely through a different downstream property. 3 Case Study: Contrastive Grad-CAM Consistency in Brain MRI 3.1 CGC Formulation Let D = {(xi, ℓi)}N i=1 denote a dataset of N single-channel MRI slices xi and corresponding class labels ℓi. We use a ResNet-50 [10] with one input channel Localization Is Not Faithfulness 5 and three output classes. For an input image x and target class t, Grad-CAM [24] is computed from the activations and class-score gradients of a selected convolutional layer. Let F k ∈RHf ×Wf denote the k-th feature map of this layer, with activation F k p,q at spatial location (p, q), and let yt denote the pre-softmax logit for class t. The Grad-CAM weight associated with feature map k is αt k = 1 HfWf Hf X p=1 Wf X q=1 ∂yt ∂F k p,q , (1) and the corresponding class-specific heatmap is gt(x) = ReLU X k αt kF k ! . (2) The ReLU retains the positive values of the weighted activation map. Following the original CGC formulation [19], we use gi(·) to denote Grad- CAM computed with respect to the top predicted class of the original, untrans- formed image xi. This class is held fixed when Grad-CAM is computed for the corresponding transformed image. For a mini-batch {xj}n j=1, each image is asso- ciated with an independently sampled geometric transformation Tj(·). For query image xi, the transformed explanation of the original image, Ti(gi(xi)), and the explanation of the transformed image, gi(Ti(xi)), form the positive pair. The explanations {gj(Tj(xj))}j̸=i form the negative pairs. The per-query CGC loss is Li = −log exp(sim(Ti(gi(xi)), gi(Ti(xi))) /τ) Pn j=1 exp(sim(Ti(gi(xi)), gj(Tj(xj))) /τ), (3) where sim(·, ·) denotes cosine similarity and τ > 0 is the temperature parameter. With each image serving once as the query, the batch-level CGC loss is LCGC = n X i=1 Li. (4) The training objective is L = LCE + λLCGC, (5) where LCE is the cross-entropy classification loss and λ controls the contribution of the CGC term. Tumor masks are not used in the training objective. 3.2 Dataset and Experimental Design The Brain Tumor MRI dataset [7] contains 3,064 contrast-enhanced T1-weighted MRI slices from 233 patients, with corresponding binary tumor masks delineated by experienced radiologists. We use five-fold stratified patient-grouped cross- validation, with all slices from each patient assigned to a single fold. Within each training partition, a second patient-grouped split reserves approximately 6 A. Akter et al. 10% of the training data for validation. Across folds, the training splits contain 2,147–2,270 images from 166–170 patients, the validation splits contain 225–278 images from 18–20 patients, and the test splits contain 531–687 images from 45–48 patients. The baseline and CGC models use the same ResNet-50 architecture and iden- tical patient-level data splits. Models are trained for 20 epochs using stochastic gradient descent with an initial learning rate of 0.01, momentum 0.9, weight de- cay 10−4, and batch size 16. The learning rate is reduced by a factor of 0.1 every five epochs. The model input resolution is 384 × 384 pixels. Training augmen- tation consists of random resized cropping with scale range (0.08, 1.0), followed by random horizontal flipping. CGC uses λ = 0.25 and τ = 0.5. Grad-CAM is computed from the final ResNet-50 residual stage, layer4. All experiments are run with a fixed random seed of 42. The crop scale range is broad for brain MRI and may exclude clinically rel- evant anatomy. We therefore do not assume that all sampled transformations preserve all clinically relevant anatomy, and we interpret the qualitative trans- formation examples accordingly. 3.3 Evaluation We report classification accuracy as the predictive-performance measure. Ex- planations are evaluated along two distinct dimensions: lesion localization and perturbation-based faithfulness. Localization is quantified using Pointing Game (PG) [30] and Relevance Mass Accuracy (RMA) [2]. Perturbation-based faithful- ness is evaluated using Insertion AUC (IAUC) and Deletion AUC (DAUC) [18], together with Iterative Removal Of Features (IROF) [20]. Higher values are pre- ferred for PG, RMA, IAUC, and IROF, whereas lower values are preferred for DAUC. IAUC, DAUC, and IROF are computed using the ExplainableAIImage- Measures implementation [17], with 70 superpixels used for IROF. Results are reported as mean ± standard deviation across the five patient-grouped folds. We interpret these fold-level statistics descriptively and make no inferential claims from small differences in mean performance. 4 Empirical Results 4.1 Classification and Localization Table 1 reports classification accuracy and Grad-CAM localization results. Over- all classification accuracy changes from 93.63±1.91% for the baseline to 94.87± 1.56% with CGC. We report this 1.24-percentage-point difference descriptively and do not interpret it as evidence of improved classification performance. Overall PG changes from 27.24 ± 3.76% to 36.02 ± 4.00%, an absolute differ- ence of 8.78 percentage points. RMA changes from 11.17±1.60% to 18.01±2.70%, an absolute difference of 6.84 percentage points. Mean PG and RMA are higher with CGC for all three tumor classes. Property-specific interpretation. Improvement within a single metric family sup- ports a claim about the corresponding measured property rather than explana- tion quality as a whole. Higher PG and RMA indicate stronger spatial agreement with the annotated region according to these localization criteria. Changes in Localization Is Not Faithfulness 9 IAUC or DAUC characterize model behavior under the corresponding insertion or deletion protocol. Claims should therefore remain aligned with the property and protocol being measured. Direct evaluation of targeted properties. An explanation-aware objective that targets a specific property should be evaluated directly on that property. For CGC, transformation consistency should therefore be quantified directly in ad- dition to localization and perturbation-based faithfulness. More generally, eval- uation should include the property explicitly targeted by the training objective. Interpretation of metric disagreement. Disagreement across metrics should be reported rather than collapsed into a single favorable conclusion. In our case study, IAUC, DAUC, and IROF rank the two models differently, so the higher localization scores cannot be interpreted as a uniform improvement in model reliance. Metric disagreement is therefore informative about the scope of the observed differences. 6 Limitations and Scope The case study uses one 2-D MRI dataset, one CNN backbone, one attribution method, and one fixed random seed. The five patient-grouped folds reflect varia- tion across patient-level splits but do not characterize variability across training seeds. The broad crop scale range may exclude clinically relevant anatomy in some transformed images. Transformation behavior is examined qualitatively, without a quantitative measure of transformation consistency. IAUC, DAUC, and IROF are conditional on their respective perturbation protocols. A documented bias in the Cheng dataset permits tumor classification from non-tumor information [28]. Accordingly, we do not interpret the reported classi- fication accuracy as evidence of reliance on clinically relevant tumor information. Spatial agreement with tumor annotations therefore remains distinct from evi- dence of model reliance. Our results do not establish that CGC is generally preferable for medical imaging. Our results instead support the more limited conclusion that local- ization and perturbation-based faithfulness should be evaluated and reported separately. 7 Conclusion We argue that transformation consistency, localization, and faithfulness should be treated as distinct evaluation targets. Our brain MRI case study using CGC provides empirical support for this distinction. Mean PG changes from 27.24% for the baseline to 36.02% with CGC, and mean RMA from 11.17% to 18.01%. The perturbation-based metrics show different overall patterns: IAUC is higher with CGC, DAUC is lower for the baseline, and IROF is unchanged at the reported precision. 10 A. Akter et al. CGC yields higher mean lesion-localization scores in this benchmark, whereas the selected perturbation-based faithfulness metrics do not show a consistent improvement. We therefore argue that XAI studies should directly evaluate the property targeted by an explanation-aware objective, report spatial agreement and model reliance as separate evaluation criteria, and explicitly report dis- agreement across metrics. This distinction is particularly important in medical imaging, where visually plausible explanations do not by themselves establish reliance on clinically relevant evidence. Future work will explore whether the difference between localization and faithfulness remains consistent across other medical imaging datasets, model architectures, and attribution methods. Alternative formulations of the CGC objective, including distribution-alignment approaches [6] and optimal transport, could be explored as well. Acknowledgments.
