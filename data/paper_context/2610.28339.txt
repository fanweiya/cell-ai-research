Spatial Context Modeling and Multi-Scale Refinement Spatial modeling connects local morphology to broader tissue organization. Hist2ST ( Zeng et al. 2022 ) combines global attention with graph-based neighborhood interactions, while TRIPLEX ( Chung et al. 2024 ) integrates target, neighborhood, and global features. HiST ( Wu et al. 2026 ) propagates information through a sparse hierarchical encoder–decoder with spatial coarsening, recovery, and skip connections. Spatial dependencies also enter generative models through local spatial attention in STFlow ( Huang et al. 2025a ) and graph encoding in FLAG ( Si et al. 2026 ) . These approaches offer different mechanisms for selecting spatial evidence and propagating it across locations and resolutions. Building on these advances, we investigate whether coupling persistent spot-state refinement with context reconstruction provides benefits beyond access to multiple spatial scales alone. HistoLoop maintains persistent spot states and reconstructs context at subsequent resolutions from the states updated by earlier contextual interactions. This recurrent coupling allows previously incorporated spatial evidence to influence the construction of subsequent contextual messages. Pathology Foundation Models Pathology foundation models learn transferable representations from large-scale histology data. UNI ( Chen et al. 2024 ) provides general-purpose patch-level features, while GigaPath ( Xu et al. 2024 ) combines patch-level pretraining with whole-slide representation learning. Language supervision further enriches these representations: CONCH ( Lu et al. 2024 ) learns from histopathology image–caption pairs, and TITAN ( Ding et al. 2025 ) combines visual self-supervision with region-level caption and slide-level report alignment. HEST-Bench ( Jaume et al. 2024 ) evaluates pretrained pathology representations through spatial gene expression prediction, connecting foundation model development to this task. Foundation models provide powerful histological representations, but they primarily define how visual features are encoded rather than how spatial evidence should be progressively interpreted during prediction. HistoLoop builds on pretrained histological features and investigates this downstream inference process through recurrent spot-state refinement, complementing advances in pathology representation learning. Overview Given pretrained histological features 𝐗 = { 𝐱 i } i = 1 N \mathbf{X}=\{\mathbf{x}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N} and their spatial coordinates 𝐏 = { 𝐩 i } i = 1 N \mathbf{P}=\{\mathbf{p}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N} , HistoLoop predicts the spatial gene expression matrix 𝐘 ^ ∈ ℝ N × G \hat{\mathbf{Y}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times G} . As illustrated in Fig. 1(a), HistoLoop consists of an input encoding stage, a recurrent multi-resolution spatial loop, and an output prediction stage. In the input encoding stage, we fuse histological features with positional information and apply a full-resolution pre-spatial Transformer to obtain contextualized spot representations 𝐇 pre \mathbf{H}_{\mathrm{pre}} . Following MeSH ( Yu et al. 2026 ) , we initialize a persistent state 𝐇 ( 0 ) \mathbf{H}^{(0)} and an S S -slot memory buffer 𝐌 ( 0 ) \mathbf{M}^{(0)} for each spot. The memory-mediated transition integrates retrieved spatial evidence into the persistent states through a write–read operation, enabling the states to evolve across refinement steps. Details of the initialization and memory operations are provided in the subsequent sections and appendix. The recurrent core performs T T refinement steps according to a coarse-to-fine context-token ratio schedule. At step t t , the current spot states 𝐇 ( t ) \mathbf{H}^{(t)} are grouped into spatial cells, where state-dependent weights aggregate spot representations and their normalized coordinates to produce context tokens 𝐂 ( t ) \mathbf{C}^{(t)} and weighted coordinates 𝐏 C ( t ) \mathbf{P}_{C}^{(t)} . A local spatial graph is constructed over the context coordinates, and a shared spatial Transformer produces contextualized representations 𝐙 ( t ) \mathbf{Z}^{(t)} . Spot-conditioned cross-attention then retrieves relevant spatial evidence for each spot, yielding an update Δ ​ 𝐇 ( t ) \Delta\mathbf{H}^{(t)} . The MeSH write–read operation integrates this update into the persistent states and memory buffers, producing 𝐇 ( t + 1 ) \mathbf{H}^{(t+1)} and 𝐌 ( t + 1 ) \mathbf{M}^{(t+1)} . Experiments Experimental Setup We evaluate HistoLoop on HEST-1K ( Jaume et al. 2024 ) , covering six cancer types. We construct two data splits while retaining the original hold-out test sets from HEST-Bench, and stratify the remaining samples by cancer type into training and validation sets. For each spot, the model takes a precomputed histology feature extracted by UNI ( Chen et al. 2024 ) from the corresponding H&E image together with its two-dimensional spatial coordinate, and predicts a gene expression profile over a fixed vocabulary of 38,982 genes. We compare HistoLoop with four recent methods: HiST ( Wu et al. 2026 ) , HistoPrism ( Hu et al. 2026 ) , MoLF ( Hu and Speidel 2026 ) , and STFlow ( Huang et al. 2025a ) . All methods are evaluated using the same UNI features, spatial coordinates, fixed 38,982-gene vocabulary, and data partitions. For each baseline, we follow its official training configuration while keeping the input data and evaluation protocol consistent across methods. For the primary evaluation, we follow the HVG50 protocol of HEST-Bench ( Jaume et al. 2024 ) , using the corresponding predefined panel of 50 highly variable genes (HVG50) for each cancer type. The panel membership is fixed independently of the held-out test data and remains unchanged across splits and methods. For each run, we compute the gene-wise Pearson correlation coefficient (PCC) across valid spots for every benchmark gene on each held-out slide, average the resulting values over the benchmark genes and held-out slides to obtain a cancer-level score, and report the mean and standard deviation across five random seeds. The macro average is calculated as the unweighted mean of the six cancer-level mean PCCs. For all experiments, HistoLoop uses a hidden dimension of 2048 and performs 4 recurrent refinement steps with context-token ratios of ( 1 / 16 , 1 / 8 , 1 / 4 , 1 / 2 ) (1/16,1/8,1/4,1/2) . The pre-spatial, recurrent regional, and post-spatial Transformer stages each contain 3 blocks with 8 attention heads. We train the model end-to-end with the mean squared error over valid spot–gene pairs, using AdamW with a learning rate of 5 × 10 − 5 5\times 10^{-5} and a weight decay of 0.05 0.05 for up to 500 epochs. Experimental Setup We evaluate HistoLoop on HEST-1K ( Jaume et al. 2024 ) , covering six cancer types. We construct two data splits while retaining the original hold-out test sets from HEST-Bench, and stratify the remaining samples by cancer type into training and validation sets. For each spot, the model takes a precomputed histology feature extracted by UNI ( Chen et al. 2024 ) from the corresponding H&E image together with its two-dimensional spatial coordinate, and predicts a gene expression profile over a fixed vocabulary of 38,982 genes. We compare HistoLoop with four recent methods: HiST ( Wu et al. 2026 ) , HistoPrism ( Hu et al. 2026 ) , MoLF ( Hu and Speidel 2026 ) , and STFlow ( Huang et al. 2025a ) . All methods are evaluated using the same UNI features, spatial coordinates, fixed 38,982-gene vocabulary, and data partitions. For each baseline, we follow its official training configuration while keeping the input data and evaluation protocol consistent across methods. For the primary evaluation, we follow the HVG50 protocol of HEST-Bench ( Jaume et al. 2024 ) , using the corresponding predefined panel of 50 highly variable genes (HVG50) for each cancer type. The panel membership is fixed independently of the held-out test data and remains unchanged across splits and methods. For each run, we compute the gene-wise Pearson correlation coefficient (PCC) across valid spots for every benchmark gene on each held-out slide, average the resulting values over the benchmark genes and held-out slides to obtain a cancer-level score, and report the mean and standard deviation across five random seeds. The macro average is calculated as the unweighted mean of the six cancer-level mean PCCs. For all experiments, HistoLoop uses a hidden dimension of 2048 and performs 4 recurrent refinement steps with context-token ratios of ( 1 / 16 , 1 / 8 , 1 / 4 , 1 / 2 ) (1/16,1/8,1/4,1/2) . The pre-spatial, recurrent regional, and post-spatial Transformer stages each contain 3 blocks with 8 attention heads. We train the model end-to-end with the mean squared error over valid spot–gene pairs, using AdamW with a learning rate of 5 × 10 − 5 5\times 10^{-5} and a weight decay of 0.05 0.05 for up to 500 epochs. Conclusion We presented HistoLoop, a looped Transformer for histology-based spatial gene expression prediction. HistoLoop progressively reconstructs multi-resolution spatial context from evolving spot states, allowing each refinement stage to use updated spatial evidence rather than relying on a fixed context representation. On HEST-1K, HistoLoop achieves the highest macro-averaged HVG50 PCC among the compared methods and obtains the best mean performance on four of the six cancer-specific tasks. Controlled ablations highlight the contributions of recurrent refinement, coarse-to-fine ordering, and state-dependent context reconstruction. Complementary marker-gene visualizations and expression-derived clustering analyses indicate that the improvement extends beyond isolated gene-wise agreement to the recovery of spatially organized multigene patterns. The current evaluation is conducted within HEST-1K under the predefined HVG50 protocol, while the qualitative analyses cover a limited number of representative held-out slides. Future work will explore improving generalization across datasets for histology-based spatial gene expression prediction.
