4 Proposed Methodology 4.1 Dataset In this paper, we will use the BreakHis (Breast Cancer Histopathological Image Classification) dataset which is one of the most popular benchmarks of 7,909 images of 82 patients with 40, 100, 200, and 400 magnifications of both benign and malignant tumor cells. In order to guarantee methodological rigor and prevent data leakage, a patient-level strict splitting strategy was utilized, and pictures of a patient were only used in training (70%), validation (20%), or testing (10%) sets, which ensured that no overlap occurred between splits and simulated clinical conditions in real-life. The data has an inherent imbalance of classes and this was overcome by using weighted sampling methodology in training. All the images were subjected to a standardized processing procedure that included resizing (224x224 or 299x299 based on the needs of the model), ImageNet normalization, and augmentation as rotation, flipping, zooming, and contrast adjustment to enhance generalization. Also, evaluations of the magnification specifics were performed to measure the robustness of various magnifications of images. In spite of these measures, there can still be intra-patient and inter-magnification similarities in the dataset, thus, can bring subtle correlations, this is a limitation that is noted in order to make the results transparently reported and carefully interpreted. 4.2 Data Augmentation This study utilizes customized data augmentation techniques to enhance generalization and reduce overfitting in deep learning models for breast cancer classification, addressing the lack of labeled medical images and class imbalance [ 23 ] . Augmentations have been proposed to maintain diagnostic integrity while improving robustness: rotation (±20°) to establish rotational invariance, horizontal/vertical flipping to enhance spatial diversity [ 3 ] , shearing to replicate distortions from scanning or tissue preparation, zooming (±20%) to represent scale variations across magnifications [ 23 ] ., and contrast adjustment to accommodate discrepancies in staining and lighting conditions [ 24 ] . Bilinear interpolation was employed during scaling and translating to maintain smooth transitions and reduce aliasing problems [ 12 ] . These augmentations collectively mimic real-world variability, allowing models to learn invariant, clinically significant characteristics that enhance classification performance across various imaging settings. 4.3 Transfer Learning Transfer learning has become a potent approach in medical image analysis, especially in contexts with scarce labelled data. Transfer learning utilises deep neural networks pretrained on extensive datasets like ImageNet to generate robust, generalisable characteristics that may be refined for specific applications, such as breast cancer prediction. This work utilised many advanced pretrained models, including ResNet, VGG, DenseNet, EfficientNet, Xception, MobileNet, and vision transformer architectures, to evaluate their efficacy in breast cancer detection and classification. The models were modified by substituting their final classification layers and fine-tuning specific layers on target datasets to enhance performance for the particular diagnostic task. 4.3.1 DenseNet121 This study utilized DenseNet-121, a densely connected convolutional neural network pre-trained on ImageNet, as the foundation for classifying breast cancer histopathology images. The architecture utilizes extensive connectivity, allowing each layer to receive inputs from all prior levels, thus enhancing gradient flow, optimizing parameter efficiency, and alleviating vanishing gradient problems. The backbone consists of four dense blocks (6, 12, 24, and 16 layers, growth rate = 32) interspersed with transition layers utilizing 1×1 convolutions and 2×2 average pooling, culminating in global average pooling [ 25 ] , [ 26 ] . A bespoke classifier head was incorporated, comprising a fully connected layer (1024→512) with ReLU activation, dropout (p=0.5) for regularization, and a concluding dense layer (512→2) generating logits for binary classification (benign versus malignant) [ 2 ] . Input photos were downsized to 224×224, normalized, then augmented by random rotations, shifts, scaling, and flips to improve generality. The model underwent fine-tuning utilizing Label Smoothing Cross-Entropy loss ( ε \varepsilon =0.1), optimized via AdamW (weight decay = 0.01) with differential learning rates, and trained employing Cosine Annealing Warm Restarts [ 25 ] , [ 26 ] . Supplementary regularization encompassed RandAugment and gradual layer unfreezing. Training was conducted with a batch size of 32 over 50 epochs, incorporating early stopping (patience = 15, monitored on validation F1-score). 4.3.2 EfficientNet This study utilized EfficientNet, a scalable convolutional neural network created by Google AI, owing to its exceptional balance between accuracy and computational efficiency in medical image classification [ 10 ] , [ 27 ] . The model uses the compound scaling method, which uniformly adjusts network depth, width, and input resolution, and is constructed using MBConv (Mobile Inverted Residual Bottleneck) blocks integrated with squeeze-and-excitation (SE) modules for channel-wise recalibration. We specifically fine-tuned EfficientNet-B0 and its larger variants (EfficientNetV2S and EfficientNet-B7), which were pretrained on ImageNet, by substituting the classifier head with a bespoke binary classification architecture [ 28 ] . The backbone generated 1280 feature maps, which underwent global average pooling, succeeded by dropout (p = 0.5), a fully connected layer (1280 → 512, ReLU), an additional dropout, and a concluding linear layer (512 → 2) to yield logits for benign and malignant classifications. The training utilized Cross Entropy Loss with label smoothing and softmax activation during inference. To mitigate overfitting and enhance resilience, input photos were downsized (224×224 or 380×380), normalized, and subjected to augmentation by random flipping, zooming, shifting, and rotation with Keras’ ImageDataGenerator [ 28 ] . The model underwent training with a progressive unfreezing of the pretrained backbone and varying learning rates, resulting in roughly 4 million trainable parameters post fine-tuning. 4.3.3 ConvNeXt Using the BreakHis dataset, we optimized ConvNeXt, a state-of-the-art CNN architecture that draws inspiration from ResNet and Vision Transformers, for the purpose of breast cancer histological picture categorization. ConvNeXt maintains CNN inductive biases, such as locality and translation equivariance, while integrating transformer-inspired design elements, including extensive kernel depthwise convolutions, inverted bottlenecks, Layer Normalization, GELU activation, stochastic depth, AdamW optimization, and cosine learning rate scheduling [ 29 ] , [ 30 ] . We employed the ConvNeXt-Tiny/Base variation pretrained on ImageNet-21k and substituted the classification head with a bespoke architecture using LayerNorm, GELU activation, dropout (p=0.5), and fully connected layers (768 → 512 → 2) for the binary classification of benign and malignant instances. Input photos were reduced to 224×224 pixels, normalized with ImageNet statistics, and augmented by RandAugment (n=2, m=10), random cropping, color jittering, flipping, and rotation, while test-time augmentation (TTA) was utilized for enhanced inference robustness [ 30 ] . Training utilized label-smoothing cross-entropy (smoothing=0.1), the AdamW optimizer (weight decay=0.01), cosine annealing for learning rate scheduling, and mixed-precision training, alongside a gradual unfreezing of backbone layers to modify pretrained features [ 31 ] . The model comprised around 28.6 million trainable parameters. 4.3.4 ResNet This study utilized the fine-tuning of a ResNet-50 backbone, pretrained on ImageNet, for the classification of breast cancer utilizing the BreaKHis dataset. The input photos were downsized to 224×224 RGB, normalized using ImageNet statistics, and augmented by flipping, rotation, zooming, and RandAugment [ 32 ] . The backbone (about 23.5 million parameters) was initially fixed and subsequently unfixed with varying learning rates, while the custom classifier head (approximately 1.05 million parameters) comprised fully connected layers of 2048→512→2 with ReLU activation and a dropout rate of 0.5 [ 33 ] . The training employed CrossEntropyLoss with label smoothing, the Adam optimizer, and cosine annealing with warm restarts, while the assessment metrics comprised accuracy, precision, recall, specificity, F1-score, and AUC-ROC [ 32 ] . To improve interpretability, Grad-CAM, LIME, SHAP, and LRP were combined for visualizing decision boundaries. 4.3.5 InceptionV3 The study used InceptionV3, a deep convolutional neural network architecture designed for effective multi-scale feature extraction, as a transfer learning model for breast cancer classification [ 34 ] . InceptionV3 enhances the original GoogLeNet by integrating factorized convolutions, auxiliary classifiers, asymmetric filters (1×7 and 7×1), and batch normalization, hence facilitating computational efficiency and superior generalization. The model employs Inception modules that concurrently process various convolution filters (1×1, 3×3, 5×5), effectively capturing both local and global representations [ 1 ] . In our approach, the pretrained backbone was initialized using ImageNet weights, and the final classification head was substituted with a dense block (2048→512→2) with ReLU activation and dropout (p=0.5), in addition to a modified auxiliary classifier. Histopathology photos were downsized to 299×299, normalized using ImageNet statistics, and augmented with RandAugment, including cropping, flipping, rotating, zooming, and color jittering [ 34 ] . Training utilized the AdamW optimizer, label smoothing cross-entropy, cosine annealing warm restarts, mixed precision, and a gradual unfreezing of backbone layers for stable fine-tuning [ 1 ] . 4.3.6 VGG19 This research used VGG-19, pretrained on ImageNet, as a transfer learning model for the classification of breast cancer. The backbone, consisting of 16 convolutional and 5 pooling layers (about 20 million parameters), was initially frozen and subsequently fine-tuned using differential learning rates and a gradual unfreezing approach [ 35 ] . The initial head was substituted with a bespoke classifier ( 102.8M parameters) comprising Linear (25088→4096→4096→512→2) layers, ReLU activations, and several dropout layers (p=0.5) for regularization purposes. The input photos were downsized to 224×224, normalized, and augmented using RandAugment, along with cropping, flipping, rotation, and color jittering [ 10 ] . The training employed Label Smoothing Cross-Entropy (smoothing=0.1), the AdamW optimizer, cosine annealing warm restarts, AMP mixed-precision, and early halting, resulting in about 122.8 million trainable parameters optimized for histopathology picture classification [ 35 ] . 4.3.7 MobileNetV2 This research utilized MobileNetV2, a lightweight convolutional neural network optimized for processing efficiency by depthwise separable convolutions and inverted residual bottlenecks with linear projections [ 31 ] . The model accepts 224×224 RGB inputs, commencing with a 3×3 convolution (32 filters, stride 2), succeeded by a series of 17 bottleneck layers incorporating expansion through 1×1 convolutions, depthwise filtering, and projection layers. Each block integrates ReLU6 activations and residual shortcuts when the input and output dimensions are congruent [ 1 ] . The backbone generates 1280 feature maps, which are subsequently processed by a global average pooling layer and a bespoke classifier head (Linear 1280→512, ReLU, Dropout 0.5, Linear 512→2) for binary classification. MobileNetV2, with around 3.5 million parameters, attains a balance between precision and low complexity, rendering it appropriate for medical imaging applications where efficiency and scalability are paramount [ 36 ] , [ 24 ] . 4.3.8 Vision Transformer (ViT) This study utilized a Vision Transformer (ViT-B/16) pretrained on ImageNet-21k and subsequently fine-tuned for breast cancer categorization [ 37 ] . Input images (3×224×224) were segmented into 16×16 non-overlapping patches, resulting in 196 tokens embedded in 768-dimensional vectors, together with an additional learnable class token and positional embeddings [ 38 ] . The sequence underwent processing through 12 transformer encoder blocks, each consisting of multi-head self-attention (12 heads), MLP layers (hidden=3072, GELU), LayerNorm, residual connections, and dropout (p=0.1). The ultimate representation underwent LayerNorm, followed by a bespoke classifier head incorporating LayerNorm, Linear (768→512), GELU, Dropout, and Linear (512→2) for binary classification [ 39 ] . The architecture comprised over 86 million parameters, with around 400,000 derived from the custom head, facilitating efficient fine-tuning on histopathological pictures while maintaining the ViT’s robust feature extraction and contextual modeling abilities [ 36 ] , [ 40 ] . 4.3.9 Swin Transformer (Shifted Window Transformer) This research used the Swin Transformer Tiny, a hierarchical vision transformer pretrained on ImageNet-1K, for the classification of breast cancer images [ 41 ] , [ 40 ] . Input images (3×224×224) were divided into 4×4 patches and transformed into 768-dimensional tokens, which underwent four steps of shifted window-based multi-head self-attention (W-MSA, SW-MSA), residual connections, LayerNorm, and MLPs with GELU activations. Patch merging systematically diminished resolution while augmenting channel depth (768→1536→3072) [ 41 ] . The backbone output, a 3072-dimensional feature vector, was consolidated using global average pooling and forwarded to a bespoke classifier head consisting of Linear (768→512), ReLU, Dropout (0.5), Linear (512→256), ReLU, Dropout (0.5), and Linear (256→2) for the prediction of benign versus malignant outcomes [ 29 ] . The architecture had approximately 28.3 million parameters, with around 0.5 million being trainable during the initial fine-tuning phase, integrating transformer-based global context modeling with computational efficiency appropriate for high-resolution medical imaging [ 29 ] . 4.3.10 Xception The study utilized Xception (Extreme Inception), a deep convolutional neural network pretrained on ImageNet, modified for binary breast cancer classification [ 16 ] . Xception substitutes conventional convolutions with depthwise separable convolutions integrated with residual connections, facilitating efficient feature extraction. The network processes 299×299 RGB inputs via an entering flow of convolutional and separable convolutional layers (32–728 channels), succeeded by a middle flow including 8 repeating blocks (728 channels), and concluding with an exit flow that expands to 2048 channels [ 42 ] . The backbone output undergoes global average pooling, dropout (p=0.5), and a concluding linear layer (2048→2) for the classification of benign and malignant cases. The training utilized the AdamW optimizer, cosine annealing warm restarts, and label-smoothed cross-entropy loss, rendering the model both computationally efficient and highly expressive for histopathology picture processing [ 31 ] . 4.4 Explainable Artificial Intelligence (XAI) The black-box aspect of deep learning models presents major obstacles to their clinical adoption, where trust and transparency are of the utmost importance, notwithstanding their impressive accuracy in breast cancer detection. Explainable Artificial Intelligence (XAI) methodologies seek to mitigate this constraint by offering human-comprehensible insights into model predictions [ 43 ] . This study utilised XAI technologies, including Grad-CAM, LIME, SHAP, and XRAI, to visualise and elucidate the decision-making processes of diverse deep learning models. These tools not only improve model transparency but also aid doctors in verifying the regions or features most significant in diagnosis results. 4.4.1 Grad-CAM (Gradient-weighted Class Activation Mapping) Grad-CAM (Gradient-weighted Class Activation Mapping) was utilized to improve the interpretability of deep learning models by producing class-discriminative heatmaps that emphasize the picture regions most significant for classification [ 37 , 44 ] . It calculates the gradients of the projected class score relative to the feature maps of the final convolutional layer, use global average pooling to obtain channel weights, and generates a ReLU-activated weighted sum to create the final heatmap. The heatmap is subsequently overlaid on the original image, clearly highlighting areas essential for decision-making [ 28 , 31 ] . This study employed Grad-CAM across various architectures (ResNet50, DenseNet121, Xception, EfficientNetV2, Swin Transformer) to assess the models’ attention to aberrant tissue patterns. Its post hoc nature, necessitating no architectural alterations, renders it especially appropriate for medical imaging applications where trust and transparency are important [ 29 ] . 4.4.2 LIME (Local Interpretable Model-Agnostic Explanations) The study used LIME (Local Interpretable Model-Agnostic Explanations) to produce localized, case-specific elucidations of breast cancer classification models. In contrast to gradient-based approaches, LIME is model-agnostic, approximating intricate predictions with a straightforward interpretable surrogate by perturbing inputs into superpixels and analyzing output fluctuations [ 9 ] . Each image underwent masking or modification, and a local linear model was applied to assess the significance of various image segments. LIME was utilized on models such as ResNet50, EfficientNet, DenseNet121, and Swin Transformer, emphasizing the areas most critical for benign or malignant predictions. This offered clear, instance-specific interpretability, augmenting Grad-CAM by elucidating local decision limits that global saliency maps would neglect, thus enhancing clinical confidence in AI-assisted breast cancer diagnosis [ 45 ] . 4.4.3 SHAP (SHapley Additive Explanations) SHAP was used to measure feature significance through principles of cooperative game theory, assigning the contribution of each input region to the model’s output. By estimating Shapley values, SHAP equitably allocates prediction influence among picture superpixels or patches [ 46 ] . This work employed SHAP on models such as ResNet50, EfficientNet, DenseNet121, and Swin Transformer, generating heatmaps that emphasized areas influencing malignant or benign predictions. In contrast to gradient-based techniques, SHAP delivers globally consistent and theoretically robust explanations, furnishing doctors with insights into the influence of each input feature on decision outcomes [ 24 ] . 4.4.4 Integrated Gradients (IG) Integrated Gradients were used to attribute model predictions by calculating the path integral of gradients between a baseline (e.g., a black images) and the input image. This method offers a systematic methodology for allocating contribution scores to each pixel by averaging gradients along the interpolation trajectory [ 46 ] . This study employed IG on CNN and transformer-based architectures to elucidate subtle tissue patterns affecting benign-malignant classification. Its efficacy is rooted in generating smoother and more accurate attributions than raw gradients, rendering it particularly suitable for medical imaging, where detailed interpretability is essential [ 43 ] . 4.4.5 Layer-wise Relevance Propagation (LRP) LRP was utilized as a backpropagation-based explanation technique, redistributing the prediction score backwards through the network to identify the input areas that were most responsible for the decision [ 47 ] . Using relevance conservation concepts, LRP assigns pixel-level contributions that aggregate to the model’s output, producing comprehensive and intuitive visual representations. LRP was incorporated into CNNs (ResNet, DenseNet, Xception) for the analysis of breast cancer images, emphasizing significant histological features. LRP’s ability to deliver detailed pixel attributions improves clinical confidence by guaranteeing that diagnostic predictions correspond with medically relevant structures. 4.1 Dataset In this paper, we will use the BreakHis (Breast Cancer Histopathological Image Classification) dataset which is one of the most popular benchmarks of 7,909 images of 82 patients with 40, 100, 200, and 400 magnifications of both benign and malignant tumor cells.
