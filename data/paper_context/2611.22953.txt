General architecture and terminology Fiberpose converts a raw microscopy image (Fig. 1 a, INPUT ) into a geometric representation of the fiber network. First, a neural network predicts a distance-transform (DT) field, in which each foreground pixel encodes its estimated distance to the nearest fiber boundary. In this representation, fiber centerlines correspond to the ridges of the DT field, which reaches a local maximum along the direction perpendicular to the fiber. Fiberpose traces the candidate ridges and groups them into uniquely identified FiberFragments . Geometrically derived rules are then applied to filter the candidate ridges, and the retained ones serve as measurement centerlines for quantitative morphometry (Fig. 1 b,c, REPRESENTATION ). This representation supports three ANALYSIS steps (Fig. 1 d–f). First, instance segmentation post-processes the DT field and candidate ridges to produce labeled fiber fragments. In the second, independent step, Fiberpose obtains local fiber diameters. The methodology uses the fact that the value of the DT field at the ridge equals half the fiber thickness at that point. Therefore, by sampling twice the DT value along fiber centerlines at some spacing, Fiberpose estimates the distribution of local fiber diameters. Third, local fiber orientation is estimated from DT gradients in the vicinity of centerline points. Since these gradients point across the fiber, the local fiber orientation is taken to be perpendicular to the gradient direction. The resulting orientations are interpolated within segmented fragments and color-coded to produce orientation maps. Fragment-level measurements are aggregated into sample-level descriptors, including fiber-thickness distributions and orientation histograms (Fig. 1 g). This unified pipeline is implemented as an interactive analysis environment (Fig. 1 h) that links neural inference, geometric post-processing, visualization and quantitative export in a desktop application. Instance segmentation of elongated fibers We benchmark Fiberpose for instance segmentation on a set of ten SEM images, including five with randomly oriented fibers and five with aligned fibers, and compare it with uncurated SAM2 (automatic masks without filtering; see Methods ) [ 31 , 32 ] and Cellpose-SAM [ 19 ] . We evaluate two variants of Fiberpose , using watershed and ridge-expansion post-processing. To focus on elongated structures, the evaluation is restricted to ground-truth instances with circularity ≤ 0.5 \leq 0.5 , corresponding approximately to an equivalent aspect ratio ≥ 4 \geq 4 under a rectangular approximation (see Methods). Segmentation performance is evaluated using instance recall and precision, together with matched-pair intersection over union (IoU), normalized surface Dice (NSD), and the 95th-percentile Hausdorff distance (HD95) [ 33 ] . The differences in segmentation performance may be partly explained by the underlying field representations (Fig. 7 ). Cellpose-SAM predicts a center-directed flow field (Fig. 7 a), which becomes increasingly ambiguous for elongated structures with extended central regions, complicating flow integration and instance reconstruction (Fig. 7 c). In contrast, Fiberpose predicts a distance field (Fig. 7 b), encoding local distances to fiber boundaries. This representation enables the detection of elongated centerlines through ridge detection and subsequent reconstruction of individual fiber instances (Fig. 7 d). The difference is further illustrated by instance recall as a function of equivalent aspect ratio (Fig. 7 f). While Cellpose-SAM recall decreases substantially as progressively more elongated instances are considered, Fiberpose maintains consistently high recall, with SAM2 showing intermediate performance. In rare cases, Fiberpose remains susceptible to segmentation errors arising from inaccuracies in the predicted distance field. In particular, artificial connections between adjacent fibers may cause several fibers to merge into a single instance (Fig. 7 d). Diameters are estimated by sampling local radii along detected centerlines rather than from reconstructed instance boundaries (Fig. 7 e). Diameter accuracy against manual reference The diameter benchmark included 20 SEM images not used for training, covering both high-contrast images with clearly defined fibers and challenging images with faint borders, non-uniform fiber contrast and variable sizes. Diameters obtained with Fiberpose in default and strict modes, DiameterJ [ 7 ] , Fibar [ 35 ] and GIFT [ 9 ] were compared with manual measurements, using the relative absolute error per image (%) as the metric and the paired Wilcoxon signed-rank test for pairwise comparisons ( n = 20 n=20 images; n = 18 n=18 for GIFT ). Fiberpose measured fiber diameters significantly more accurately than all three existing tools. The median relative absolute error was 4.2% in default mode and 4.1% in strict mode, compared with 13.7% for DiameterJ , 17.7% for Fibar and 82.2% for GIFT (Fig. 8 e), and every tool differed significantly from both Fiberpose modes ( p < 0.01 p<0.01 for all comparisons). Fiberpose errors also varied least between images and never exceeded 20%, over the whole range of fiber diameters (Fig. 8 f). Fiberpose showed a small systematic underestimation, with a median signed error of − 1.20 -1.20 px in default mode and − 0.70 -0.70 px in strict mode. The lower accuracy of DiameterJ and Fibar did not result from a systematic error. Their median signed errors were close to zero ( − 0.20 -0.20 px and + 1.10 +1.10 px, respectively); instead, both tools were accurate on some images and produced large errors on others, which is reflected in their wide error spread (Fig. 8 e,f). These errors can be explained by the quality of the semantic segmentation on which both tools rely: merging of adjacent fibers increased the estimated diameter, whereas incomplete segmentation of a fiber decreased it. The SRM segmentation of DiameterJ is susceptible to both errors, and the skeleton derived from it is rough: although DiameterJ attempts to filter out fiber junctions, its skeleton remains less precise than the measurement centerlines of Fiberpose (Fig. 8 a,b). Fibar does not provide intermediate segmentation results; a mask reimplemented following its published pipeline contained more incompletely segmented fibers than the DiameterJ mask, and the only output of the tool contained many misaligned measurement marks (Fig. 8 c). Consequently, both tools provide reliable results only on carefully selected images. GIFT was the most fragile method: it performed acceptably on some images but failed on most of them, and returned no valid estimate for two of the 20 images. Its edge-detection result and the lines retained after filtering at 0 ∘ 0^{\circ} show that the underlying signal can be weak and unstable (Fig. 8 d). In contrast, Fiberpose measures diameters directly on the predicted DT field, and its performance was robust to image appearance (Fig. 8 a). Orientation agreement with established tools As no manual ground truth is available for fiber orientation, Fiberpose was evaluated by its agreement with two widely used tools, OrientationJ [ 25 ] and Directionality [ 37 ] . Qualitatively, Fiberpose and OrientationJ produced orientation maps with consistent predominant directions (Fig. 9 a–d; color wheel in Fig. 4 a). However, OrientationJ coloring was blurred and for thick fibers concentrated along their borders, where intensity gradients are strongest, leaving fiber interiors empty (Fig. 9 c). Fiberpose maps were uniformly filled across the full fiber width and sharply bounded by the segmented instances, independently of fiber thickness (Fig. 9 b,d). Quantitatively, Fiberpose agreed with the established tools as closely as they agreed with each other. Across all images ( n = 20 n=20 ), the absolute difference in orientation index was similar for all three method pairs, with no statistically significant difference (median | Δ ​ OI | = 0.068 |\Delta\mathrm{OI}|=0.068 , 0.078 0.078 and 0.067 0.067 for Fiberpose–OrientationJ, Fiberpose–Directionality and OrientationJ–Directionality, respectively; paired Wilcoxon signed-rank test, p ≥ 0.87 p\geq 0.87 ). Qualitative generalization beyond the training domain Fiberpose detected fibrous structures in every image group tested, including materials and microscopy modalities absent from its training data (Fig. 10 ). The model was trained exclusively on real and synthetic SEM images and was designed to detect bright fibers against a dark background. It was applied here without retraining and with default parameters; the only adaptation was intensity inversion of images acquired using transmission-based modalities, including TEM and conventional transmitted-light microscopy. The examples were taken from openly licensed publications and Wikimedia Commons, and none of the SEM examples belong to the training collection. The examples are ordered by increasing distance from the training domain. The SEM examples cover polymer and mineral fibers (an electrospun PCL scaffold, amosite asbestos, a woven polycotton fabric and a swimwear fabric) and biological samples (a cell spread over electrospun fibers, wood-pulp paper and porcine collagen fibrils). The TEM examples show collagen fibrils in uterine tissue and in a human skin equivalent, isolated banded collagen fibrils, and myofilaments of Drosophila flight muscle. The optical microscopy examples show dandelion pappus fibers, eucalyptus kraft-pulp fibers, an archaeological animal-hair fiber and aspen wood fiber cells. Detections were obtained across all groups despite substantial differences in image contrast, background texture, fiber appearance and scale, from collagen fibrils and myofilaments to textile yarns and wood fibers. Visual inspection indicated a higher frequency of false-positive or incomplete detections in the TEM and optical images, which are not represented in the training set; typical errors were spurious detections in textured background and fragmented outlines of weakly contrasted fibers. Thus, although trained only on real and synthetic SEM images, Fiberpose produced qualitatively meaningful detections across polymer, mineral, plant and animal fibers imaged by electron and optical microscopy. As a next step, we will extend the training dataset to these imaging domains to further improve generalization. Discussion Fiberpose shows that fiber morphometry does not have to pass through a binary mask. By predicting the DT field directly from the image and measuring on that field, it reached a median diameter error of about 4%, compared with 14–82% for DiameterJ , Fibar and GIFT , and detected elongated fibers more completely than Cellpose-SAM and more specifically than SAM2. We attribute this to combining the strengths of two lines of work in a single predicted field: the learned geometric field prediction of Cellpose and Omnipose [ 17 , 18 ] and the DT-based measurement of DiameterJ [ 7 ] . Three developments were needed to make this approach work. The first is an annotated dataset of fiber SEM images, complemented by synthetic images, which we created for this work. The second is a training procedure that yields a geometrically correct DT field, combining gradient supervision with static and dynamic Eikonal regularization. The third is a pipeline that extracts quantitative data from such predictions: geometric selection of measurement centerlines excludes fiber ends and crossings, where the field is distorted, and two-offset sampling corrects for the rounding of the predicted ridges. The main consequence of working on the predicted field is that measurement no longer depends on instance segmentation. In our benchmark, the errors of DiameterJ and Fibar could be traced to their masks, in which merged fibers increased the estimated diameter and incompletely segmented fibers decreased it. Fiberpose diameters, read along the measurement centerlines of the DT field, remained measurable even where adjacent fibers merged into a single instance (Fig. 7 e). The accuracy of Fiberpose diameters stayed consistent across images on which the existing tools ranged from accurate to failing. Orientation results agreed with OrientationJ and Directionality as closely as these two established tools agreed with each other, while Fiberpose additionally provided orientation maps within segmented instances. In terms of mask quality, Fiberpose reached matched-instance IoU comparable to Cellpose-SAM, with no statistically significant difference between the two methods. Thus, Fiberpose combines mask quality on par with a foundation-model-based segmenter with reliable fiber detection and accurate morphometry. This was achieved with a compact network. The pretrained FPUNet contains 0.93M parameters (3.7 MB in FP32), about 7-fold fewer than the models used in the original Cellpose and Omnipose frameworks ( ∼ \sim 6.6M) and about 330-fold fewer than the transformer-based Cellpose-SAM ( ∼ \sim 305M). Even with the quality-control branch, the complete FPWNet remains compact (1.35M parameters, 5.4 MB). One possible explanation is the relatively constrained geometry of fibrous structures. Unlike cells, which exhibit considerable diversity in shape and internal organization, fibers share characteristic geometric properties, including elongation, locally parallel boundaries, and gradual variations in radius along their length. These properties are largely independent of material composition and imaging modality, potentially reducing the complexity of the features required to infer geometric distance from image intensity. Consequently, a compact convolutional network may be sufficient to learn this mapping without requiring a large number of feature channels. Larger architectures, particularly transformer-based models [ 52 , 53 ] , may offer advantages in representational capacity and cross-domain generalization, but these potential benefits must be balanced against increased computational requirements. Systematic benchmarking on larger and more diverse datasets, including measurements of inference time, memory consumption, and accuracy, is needed to determine whether additional model complexity provides a meaningful advantage for DT prediction. Although the present framework was developed primarily for SEM images, qualitative experiments demonstrated its ability to detect fibrous structures in other imaging domains, including transmission electron and optical microscopy of biological materials. These observations suggest that the model has learned geometric invariants of fibrous structures that generalize across differences in image appearance. However, the model was trained exclusively on real and synthetic SEM images, and reduced detection specificity was observed in previously unseen imaging domains. Broader quantitative validation and training on multimodal datasets will therefore be necessary to establish reliable performance on natural fibers, collagen networks [ 54 , 55 ] , cytoskeletal structures [ 56 ] , and other biological specimens. Several limitations remain. Very thin fibers, particularly those only a few pixels wide, are challenging to represent accurately with a smooth distance-transform field because of the limited spatial information available for reliable ridge extraction and gradient estimation. Upscaling may improve numerical stability, although interpolation cannot recover geometric information absent from the original image. Another limitation is that Fiberpose identifies individual fiber fragments rather than reconstructing complete fiber networks. Recovering network connectivity would require additional methods, potentially incorporating graph-based approaches [ 57 , 58 ] to link fragments and resolve intersections. Finally, the present work focuses on two-dimensional images, although the underlying methodology could be extended to volumetric data through the prediction of three-dimensional distance fields and extraction of spatial centerlines. In summary, predicting the DT field instead of a binary mask turns a single network output into instance segmentation, diameter and orientation measurements, and on the evaluated SEM images it outperformed existing tools in diameter accuracy and fiber detection. Fiberpose is provided as a desktop application and a web tool that displays every intermediate image object, from the DT field to measurement centerlines, so that each measurement can be inspected and reproduced [ 59 ] . With further validation across materials, imaging modalities and laboratories, this common geometric representation could replace the collection of task-specific image-processing procedures currently used for fiber morphometry and support standardized quantitative analysis of fibrous structures. Technical Methods Neural network training Neural network architecture. We use a FPUNet network based on [ 60 ] , which is a compact residual U-Net encoder–decoder with a configurable number of resolution levels. Each level contains a residual block consisting of two 3 × 3 3\times 3 convolutions with group normalization [ 61 ] , ReLU activation, and a shortcut connection. Downsampling is performed using strided convolutions, while the decoder uses bilinear interpolation and skip connections from corresponding encoder levels. A final 1 × 1 1\times 1 convolution produces the output. The FPUNet comprises five resolution levels with feature widths of 16, 24, 48, 96, and 144 channels and maps a grayscale input image I ⁡ ( x , y ) I(x,y) to a single-channel distance-transform field D ​ T ​ ( x , y ) DT(x,y) without an output activation function. In addition to the main FPUNet network described above, we add an additional second FPUNet that estimates foreground probability from the predicted distance field. It is used to identify fiber regions suitable for reliable analysis. This network uses a smaller configuration with four resolution levels and feature widths of 12, 24, 48, and 96 channels, with sigmoid activation applied to its output. When combined, the two networks form FPWNet [ 62 ] , a cascaded architecture in which the distance-transform prediction serves as input to the foreground network. Training dataset and annotation. A collection of 275 distinct SEM images of fibrous structures was assembled from published scientific articles distributed under CC BY licences. Of these, 60 images were selected for sparse instance annotation and used as real-image training data. The training set additionally included 50 synthetically generated SEM-like images to increase the diversity of fiber geometries and image appearances. Sparse instance annotation was used [ 63 ] , distinguishing positive, negative, and ignored regions. Selected fiber fragments were annotated as individual instances, while selected background regions were labeled as negative. All remaining regions were left unlabeled and excluded from direct supervision. Coarse initial masks were generated using Segment Anything 2 [ 32 ] and subsequently refined manually in custom Python script and GIMP image editor. Annotated fiber regions were used for supervised distance-transform regression, while trusted background pixels were assigned a target value of zero. Training procedure. Input images were converted to grayscale and independently normalized to the range [ 0 , 1 ] [0,1] . During each training epoch, a single random 384 × 384 384\times 384 pixel crop was extracted from each image after applying a random rotation. Additional on-the-fly photometric augmentations were applied including variations in brightness, contrast, gamma, non-uniform illumination, Gaussian noise, and blur. Geometric transformations were applied consistently to the input images and distance-transform (DT) targets, whereas photometric augmentations were applied only to the input images. The network was trained using the AdamW optimizer [ 66 ] in two stages. The initial stage consisted of 300 epochs with a learning rate of 10 − 3 10^{-3} and a batch size of 16, using only the Huber loss on predicted DT values. The subsequent refinement stage consisted of 1,700 epochs with a reduced learning rate of 10 − 5 10^{-5} and a batch size of 4. During this stage, the loss function combined the Huber loss on DT values, the Huber loss on DT gradients, static and dynamic Eikonal regularization terms, with relative weights of 1, 10, 10, and 1, respectively. Model performance was monitored on a separate validation dataset, and the checkpoint minimizing the dynamic Eikonal loss was selected. Training was implemented in PyTorch [ 67 ] . The benchmark included both high-quality, high-contrast images with clearly defined fibers and more challenging images characterized by faint boundaries, non-uniform contrast, blur, or reduced image quality. None of the benchmark images were used for training. Measurements obtained with Fiberpose in its default and strict modes were compared with those obtained using DiameterJ [ 7 ] , Fibar [ 35 ] and GIFT [ 9 ] , with manual measurements serving as the reference. Manual diameter measurements were performed in ImageJ using an overlaid grid consisting of three horizontal lines (top, middle, and bottom), each containing ten sampling points. At each point, the diameter was measured on a clearly identifiable segment of the fiber whose boundary was closest to the sampling point, yielding 30 measurements per image. DiameterJ and Fibar were run with their default settings. GIFT was run with filtering angles of 0 ∘ 0^{\circ} , 45 ∘ 45^{\circ} , 90 ∘ 90^{\circ} and 135 ∘ 135^{\circ} . The source code with installation instructions is freely available on GitHub https://github.com/vikba/fiberpose Neural network architecture. We use a FPUNet network based on [ 60 ] , which is a compact residual U-Net encoder–decoder with a configurable number of resolution levels. Each level contains a residual block consisting of two 3 × 3 3\times 3 convolutions with group normalization [ 61 ] , ReLU activation, and a shortcut connection. Downsampling is performed using strided convolutions, while the decoder uses bilinear interpolation and skip connections from corresponding encoder levels. A final 1 × 1 1\times 1 convolution produces the output. The FPUNet comprises five resolution levels with feature widths of 16, 24, 48, 96, and 144 channels and maps a grayscale input image I ⁡ ( x , y ) I(x,y) to a single-channel distance-transform field D ​ T ​ ( x , y ) DT(x,y) without an output activation function. In addition to the main FPUNet network described above, we add an additional second FPUNet that estimates foreground probability from the predicted distance field. It is used to identify fiber regions suitable for reliable analysis. This network uses a smaller configuration with four resolution levels and feature widths of 12, 24, 48, and 96 channels, with sigmoid activation applied to its output. When combined, the two networks form FPWNet [ 62 ] , a cascaded architecture in which the distance-transform prediction serves as input to the foreground network. Training dataset and annotation. A collection of 275 distinct SEM images of fibrous structures was assembled from published scientific articles distributed under CC BY licences. Of these, 60 images were selected for sparse instance annotation and used as real-image training data. The training set additionally included 50 synthetically generated SEM-like images to increase the diversity of fiber geometries and image appearances. Sparse instance annotation was used [ 63 ] , distinguishing positive, negative, and ignored regions. Selected fiber fragments were annotated as individual instances, while selected background regions were labeled as negative. All remaining regions were left unlabeled and excluded from direct supervision. Coarse initial masks were generated using Segment Anything 2 [ 32 ] and subsequently refined manually in custom Python script and GIMP image editor. Annotated fiber regions were used for supervised distance-transform regression, while trusted background pixels were assigned a target value of zero. Unannotated regions were treated as unknown and excluded from direct regression losses.
