2.1 Diffusion Model in Medical Imaging Diffusion models have recently shown promising results in medical imaging by generating high-quality images, allowing better control during image synthesis and providing more reliable uncertainty estimation compared to GAN-based methods Kazerouni et al. Lee et al. It achieves state-of-the-art or competitive performance among diffusion-based methods. The generated images show high fidelity, realistic textures, and strong structural consistency (Fig. 3 ). The key distinction from standard diffusion baselines lies in the domain-informed measurement operator at the core of GWDM. This operator explicitly encodes three types of features: morphological features (nuclei density, edges, and texture), multi-scale spatial features spanning fine cellular detail to broader tissue architecture, and staining features that standardise H&E contributions. Together, these guide the model to emphasise biologically meaningful tissue structures at each denoising step. As a result, the model avoids learning generic image statistics. This targeted conditioning improves robustness to variations in resolution and tissue composition. It also mitigates common failure modes such as mode collapse and structural inconsistency Goodfellow et al. (2014) ; Ho et al. (2020) , yielding more stable convergence and stronger generalization. Quantitative results in Table 2 confirm these observations across all three benchmarks. On BCSS, GWDM achieves the lowest FID and KID among all compared methods. This indicates that its samples most closely follow the real tissue distribution while maintaining strong diversity. On MoNuSeg, the dataset is relatively homogeneous, comprising densely packed nuclei with uniform morphology. Despite this limited scope for differentiation, GWDM remains competitive on distribution-level metrics. On PAIP2019, GWDM achieves the lowest KID, demonstrating the strongest statistical consistency with real data across cancer subtypes. These consistent trends confirm the generalization of GWDM and also validate that the domain-specific priors help the model learn biologically meaningful representations rather than superficial image statistics. Qualitative results are shown in Fig. 3 . GWDM faithfully reproduces nuclei organisation, staining balance, and tissue architecture. It also normalises colour variation effectively. Breast-cancer samples exhibit distinct cellular boundaries nearly indistinguishable from real images (Fig. 3 a). Liver-cancer samples show compact, well-defined structures (Fig. 3 b). MoNuSeg samples display complex cellular boundaries (Fig. 3 c). The clinical relevance of GWDM extends beyond generation quality. This was validated through downstream segmentation experiments. GWDM-synthesised patches were used to augment a self-supervised pretraining and limited-label fine-tuning pipeline. As reported in Table 4 , consistent gains in mIoU, Dice, and pixel accuracy were observed across DeepLabV3+, UNet++, and SegFormer. These gains confirm that the structural fidelity of GWDM-generated images translates directly into improved segmentation generalisation. This is particularly significant in digital pathology, where large-scale annotated datasets remain costly to obtain Koohbanani et al. (2021) . To further investigate the impact of the proposed synthetic augmentation, we stratified the test images into Easy and Hard subsets based on the median per-image IoU obtained by the real-only DeepLabV3+ baseline. The results, summarized in Table 5 , show that the proposed method consistently improves segmentation performance across both subsets. Notably, the largest gains are observed on the Hard subset of the BCSS dataset, indicating that the synthetic data are particularly beneficial for challenging cases with complex tissue morphology. In contrast, improvements on the Easy subset are smaller but remain consistent. For MoNuSeg, where the baseline performance is already close to saturation, synthetic augmentation provides modest yet uniform gains across both difficulty levels. These findings suggest that the proposed approach primarily enhances robustness on difficult samples while preserving performance on easier cases. Several limitations remain, as the current implementation operates at the patch level and relies partly on handcrafted descriptors, which may limit scalability. Future work will explore WSI synthesis and learned pathology embeddings, including transformer-based tissue encoders and foundation models such as UNI Chen et al. (2024) , to improve generalisation and reduce reliance on manual feature design. Overall, GWDM offers a principled and domain-aware approach to high-fidelity histopathology image generation, providing a strong foundation for synthetic data augmentation in computational pathology workflows. 8 Conclusion In this work, we introduced GWDM, a guided wavelet diffusion framework for histopathology image generation. It incorporates domain-specific tissue priors directly into the diffusion process. Specifically, GWDM integrates multiscale morphological, spatial, and staining features. These are combined with wavelet-based parameterisation and a dual perceptual-structural loss. Together, they guide the model toward biologically meaningfulreconstruction. The result is high-frequency cellular detail, stain consistency, and strong structural fidelity. Experiments were conducted across three benchmarks: BCSS, MoNuSeg, and PAIP2019. GWDM achieved consistent improvements in FID, KID, pFID and path-FID across all datasets. The generated images exhibit realistic tissue textures and nuclear organisation that closely align with true pathological appearance. Downstream segmentation experiments further validate these findings. GWDM-synthesised images consistently improved performance across DeepLabV3+, UNet++, and SegFormer. This confirms their practical value for training robust segmentation models in clinical digital pathology settings.
